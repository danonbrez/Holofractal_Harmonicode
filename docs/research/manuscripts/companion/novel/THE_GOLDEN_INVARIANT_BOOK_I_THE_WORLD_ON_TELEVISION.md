# THE GOLDEN INVARIANT

## BOOK I — THE WORLD ON TELEVISION

### Chapter One — Sirens

Julian Vance was nineteen years old when every television in Bellwether began showing the same picture.

He was under a municipal building at the time, which later seemed appropriate. The building was called the Cavanaugh Annex, though nobody named Cavanaugh had worked there in living memory, and the room Julian occupied was technically not a room but an equipment void between the basement boiler plant and an old civil-defense corridor whose yellow paint had turned the color of nicotine.

He had a flashlight in his mouth and both hands inside a ventilation controller manufactured before he was born.

The controller had stopped obeying the thermostat on the fourth floor. It believed, with the absolute confidence of obsolete machinery, that the building was forty-one degrees Fahrenheit and falling. The actual basement was warm enough to make Julian's shirt stick to his back.

He was trying to determine whether the fault lived in the sensor, the wire, or the box.

This, he would later think, was the useful thing about old machines. Their lies were rarely philosophical.

"Vance."

The voice came from the corridor.

Julian took the flashlight from his mouth. "Yeah?"

"Come upstairs."

It was Mr. Redding, the building engineer, a square man with a white mustache and a habit of moving as though somebody had once told him that walking quickly was a confession of weakness.

"I got the panel open."

"Leave it."

"If I leave it open somebody's going to—"

"Leave it."

There was something in Redding's voice Julian had never heard before.

He set the screwdriver on the metal shelf beside him and followed.

On the first floor, every office door stood open. The receptionist from Licensing was crying with one hand against her mouth. Two security guards were standing in front of a television mounted in the lobby, and the elevator mechanic, who had spent all morning swearing at a hydraulic seal, had stopped swearing.

Nobody looked at Julian when he came in.

On television, a building far away was burning.

The camera was too distant to make the fire seem hot. It looked diagrammatic: gray sky, black rectangular tower, orange wound.

A caption ran beneath it.

Nobody seemed to know whether the caption was correct.

"What happened?" Julian said.

"Plane," somebody answered.

"Accident?"

Nobody answered.

Then another aircraft crossed the frame.

For several seconds Julian's mind refused to admit what his eyes had done. The plane looked small, almost slow, like an icon being dragged by a mouse across a desktop.

It disappeared into the second tower.

The lobby made a single sound without anyone deciding to make it.

Julian remembered that sound better than the impact.

It was not a scream. It was the sound of thirty people discovering that they had been living in a room with a wall they had not known was there.

The day widened around the television.

News arrived faster than understanding. The anchors repeated the same words because there were no better ones. Smoke rose from another public complex. Flights disappeared from the sky. Rumors multiplied. A bomb at the capital. No bomb at the capital. More planes missing. No more planes missing. A school under threat. A bridge closed. A harbor evacuated. A man in a dark suit told everyone to remain calm with the grave urgency of somebody explaining that calm had become compulsory.

At noon the Annex closed.

At twelve-fifteen it reopened as an emergency coordination site because too many city departments had already sent their people home.

At one-thirty Julian was told to help Security drag steel barricades from a storage room that had not been unlocked since a protest twelve years earlier.

At two he found a box of gas masks whose filters had expired when he was in middle school.

At three he was running extension cords through the old civil-defense corridor for a room full of officials who had never met each other before that morning.

He did not go home until after midnight.

Bellwether looked wrong.

The streets were too empty, but every apartment window seemed blue with television light. The city had not become quiet. It had moved indoors.

Julian lived with his mother in a narrow brick row house in South Bellwether, three blocks from an elevated train and half a block from a corner store that sold detergent, lottery tickets, batteries, and every newspaper printed within a hundred miles.

His mother was asleep on the sofa when he entered.

The television was still on.

A commentator was explaining the meaning of the day.

Julian stood in the doorway and watched for perhaps thirty seconds.

Then he turned the television off.

The silence startled him.

His mother woke immediately.

"Put it back."

"You were sleeping."

"Put it back."

He did.

She settled under the blanket, eyes half closed.

"You hear from your brother?"

"Yeah. He's fine."

"Your aunt?"

"Fine."

"You sure?"

"I called."

She nodded.

The commentator kept talking.

Julian went upstairs and lay in bed fully dressed. The elevated train did not pass. No aircraft crossed the usual route toward the airport. The absence of ordinary machinery felt louder than machinery.

He slept badly.

At six the next morning Redding called.

"City wants everybody in."

Julian looked at the clock.

"What am I supposed to do?"

"Maintenance."

"Maintenance on what?"

"Everything."

That was how the Long Emergency began: not with soldiers at the door, not with martial music, but with maintenance.

Locks changed.

Badges changed.

Visitor policies changed.

Doors that had stood open for thirty years acquired electronic readers.

Windows received film.

Trash cans moved away from entrances.

Deliveries stopped at new checkpoints.

Loading docks received cameras.

Parking garages received gates.

Employees received laminated instructions about suspicious packages, suspicious vehicles, suspicious behavior, suspicious odors, suspicious questions.

At first every change came with a temporary notice.

TEMPORARY ACCESS CONTROL.

EMERGENCY PROCEDURE.

INTERIM SECURITY REQUIREMENT.

Julian learned to install electromagnetic locks.

He learned how to pull low-voltage cable through walls without destroying plaster.

He learned which municipal buildings had no accurate floor plans.

He learned that almost every public institution depended on at least one old woman who knew where a key was kept.

He learned that the city's idea of redundancy often meant two systems plugged into the same outlet.

The work suited him.

A lock either engaged or did not. A sensor either changed state or did not. A cable had two ends. A fuse was open or closed. The building's purpose might be bureaucratic, but the building itself remained honest.

Months passed.

The temporary notices yellowed.

Nobody removed the hardware.

A year later Julian was replacing a failed power supply for a security panel at the Annex when he noticed the original installation sheet.

TEMPORARY ACCESS SYSTEM — 180 DAYS.

He carried the paper upstairs to Redding.

"What's this?"

Redding looked at it.

"What?"

"It says temporary."

"Everything's temporary."

"It's been a year."

Redding put on his reading glasses, though the print was large enough to read from across the desk.

"City renewed it."

"Where?"

"Somewhere."

"You know where?"

Redding took off the glasses.

"Julian."

"What?"

"Door works?"

"Yeah."

"Then we did our part."

Julian folded the sheet and put it in his pocket.

That evening he stopped at a used computer shop on his way home. He had been teaching himself programming from books and copied disks, mostly because the computers at work were always doing something stupid and nobody who understood buildings understood code.

He bought a replacement hard drive he could not afford.

At home, he installed it in a beige tower case assembled from three different machines.

His mother called upstairs that dinner was ready.

"Minute."

"Now."

"Minute."

"Your minute takes forty-five minutes."

He went down.

The television was on during dinner.

It was always on now.

Not the same channel, not the same story, but always the same atmosphere: colored banners, urgent music, maps with arrows, experts in remote studios, little boxes containing faces.

His mother had learned the names of generals, foreign ministers, and television prosecutors she had never heard of before.

"You hear they're checking bags at the stadium now?" she said.

"No."

"Good."

"What's good?"

"They should've done it before."

Julian ate.

She pointed with her fork toward the television.

"People don't understand. You can't be too careful."

"Sure you can."

"What?"

"You can definitely be too careful."

His mother stared at him.

"That's exactly what people said before."

"Before what?"

She gestured at the screen.

Julian looked.

There it was: the new grammar.

Before.

After.

Safe.

Unsafe.

Necessary.

Irresponsible.

The words had not changed definitions exactly. They had changed weight.

He finished eating, carried his plate to the sink, and went upstairs.

On his computer he created a plain text file.

TEMPORARY.txt

He typed:

Cavanaugh Annex access system.

Installed under emergency order.

Original sheet says 180 days.

Still active.

He paused.

Then he added the date.

He did not know why he kept it.

He did not imagine himself an archivist.

He was a maintenance worker with a secondhand computer and a habit of not throwing out instructions until the machine was gone.

Years later, when people asked him when the whole thing started, Julian would think of the burning towers because everybody did.

But he did not believe that was when it started.

The event was too large.

Large events announced themselves.

The changes that lasted were smaller.

A door that required a badge after nobody remembered why.

A camera that stayed because the bracket was already drilled.

A temporary database that became the easiest place to find a name.

A form that gained a box no one later dared remove.

A television that remained on because turning it off felt irresponsible.

History, Julian would decide much later, did not usually enter through the front door.

It came in through maintenance.


For months afterward, Julian kept expecting the city to admit that the emergency was ending.

He expected a memo.

The way a building engineer eventually signed off after a leak. Cause located. Damaged material removed. Wall dried. Area returned to service.

Instead the emergency became a category under which unrelated things could be purchased.

First came the access systems.

Then radios.

Then databases.

Then contractor badges.

Then interoperable identification.

Then training.

Julian attended a mandatory session in a conference room where an instructor from a security company taught municipal employees to notice anomalous behavior.

The instructor was cheerful.

"You're not being asked to profile people," he said. "You're being asked to detect deviations from context."

Julian raised his hand.

The instructor looked at him.

"Yes?"

"Deviation from whose context?"

The room sighed.

Benson whispered, "Don't."

Julian continued.

"Like, if somebody's walking fast, that could be suspicious or they could be late."

"Correct."

"So what makes it suspicious?"

"Totality of circumstances."

"Which means?"

"It means you use judgment."

"So the policy is trust judgment?"

"Trained judgment."

"How do you measure whether training improved it?"

The instructor smiled in the specialized way people smiled before moving on.

"We'll get to scenarios."

They did.

A man taking photographs of infrastructure.

A person asking unusual questions.

An unattended bag.

A vehicle parked too long.

An employee accessing an area unrelated to assigned work.

Julian recognized himself in three of the examples.

At lunch he told Redding.

"They're going to arrest maintenance."

"Only you."

"They specifically described taking pictures of equipment."

"You take pictures of serial numbers."

"Exactly."

"Then stop looking terroristy."

Julian laughed.

The training did not make him angry.

Most of it was reasonable.

That was what interested him.

Almost every individual recommendation was reasonable.

Do not prop secure doors.

Verify visitors.

Report unattended objects.

Protect access credentials.

Question unexplained entry.

The drift appeared not in any single instruction but in accumulation.

A building once designed around presumed ordinary access became a building designed around continuous verification.

The wall did not get thicker.

The meaning of being inside changed.

At home Julian's mother had begun saving bottled water in the basement.

"How much water do you need?"

"Enough."

"For what?"

"You never know."

"What event are we surviving?"

"The event nobody warned you about."

"You watch warnings all day."

"Exactly. Imagine what they're not saying."

Julian stared at her.

"That logic doesn't close."

"I don't know what that means."

"It means you can use missing information to prove anything."

She carried another case downstairs.

"You have a computer full of notes about city doors."

"That's work."

"That's weird."

"Both can be true."

He helped her stack the water.

The basement already contained batteries, canned soup, old tax returns, Christmas decorations, and a box of school papers his mother refused to discard.

Julian opened the school box.

His handwriting from age nine.

A drawing of a bridge.

A spelling test.

A report card.

These fragments had survived because no policy governed them.

He thought about the Annex security sheet folded in his desk.

Institutional memory was formal enough to destroy itself on schedule.

Family memory survived by disobedience.

He carried one bottle of water upstairs and placed it beside the television.

The line beneath the broadcast said the national threat level had changed color.

His mother pointed.

"See?"

Julian looked at the color.

"What does that mean we do differently?"

"Be alert."

"From yesterday?"

"More alert."

"How?"

She became irritated.

"Why do you need everything to have instructions?"

He almost answered.

Because otherwise the state had changed only in language.

He did not yet have the words.



The first anniversary of the Sirens arrived with new plaques in municipal buildings.

At the Annex a bronze plate appeared beside the lobby television.

REMEMBER.

Nothing followed the word.

Julian installed the mounting anchors.

Redding held the plaque while Julian leveled it.

"Left."

"It is left."

"Your left."

"My left is the same left."

"Not from here."

Julian adjusted.

The receptionist watched them.

"Little more."

Julian looked at her.

"Everyone is an engineer during plaque installation."

She smiled.

The ceremony lasted eighteen minutes.

Officials spoke.

Names were read.

The television showed similar ceremonies elsewhere.

Afterward the lobby emptied and the bronze remained above a trash can containing paper coffee cups.

Julian stood alone for a moment.

Remember what?

The dead.

The fear.

The unity.

The failure.

The reason for the locks.

The reason for the wars.

The instruction was clear and the object unspecified.

He wrote this nowhere.

Some thoughts felt dishonest when documented too quickly.

### Chapter Two — The Crawling Line

The line appeared beneath the news during Julian's second winter at the Annex.

At first it contained weather warnings and sports scores.

Then markets.

Then elections.

Then war updates.

Then missing children, terror alerts, product recalls, celebrity arrests, school closings, infection counts, gas prices, highway delays, troop movements, stock indices, court decisions, threat levels, and the occasional sentence so truncated by motion that Julian could not determine whether something had happened or might happen.

He learned to watch the line while ignoring the person speaking above it.

The person could speculate.

The line had numbers.

Numbers looked honest.

He knew better, technically. A number could be wrong. A sensor could fail. A database could contain stale information. He had once spent half a day tracing a flood alarm only to discover a mouse had eaten the insulation off a wire.

But the line did not sound uncertain.

It crawled.

It had somewhere to be.

During lunch the maintenance crew sat in a room with one vending machine, one refrigerator, four scarred tables, and the television bolted high in a corner.

Nobody had requested the television.

It had arrived after the attacks under an emergency communications budget and never left.

"You see this?" Alvarez asked one afternoon.

Julian looked up from a sandwich.

On screen, armored vehicles crossed a desert road beneath a copper sky.

"They got the capital."

"Who?"

Alvarez laughed.

"Exactly."

Redding, reading the newspaper, said, "Turn it up."

They did.

A reporter stood on a hotel balcony wearing body armor.

Behind him the city appeared almost peaceful.

The sound cut in and out.

The crawling line continued regardless.

Julian looked around the room.

Seven men in work uniforms watched a war through a television bought to warn them if their own city was under attack.

The war was thousands of miles away.

The television was six feet away.

The distance between the two had become operationally irrelevant.

He felt the thought arrive and immediately distrusted it.

He was twenty-one. He had begun taking night classes at a community college because his mother had informed him, with the practical brutality of a woman who had spent twenty years billing insurance companies, that "being good with machines" was not a retirement plan.

The course was called Introduction to Information Systems.

The professor, Dr. Elaine Sutter, wore bright scarves and treated database normalization as a moral virtue.

"If the same fact exists in seven places," she told them, "how many facts do you have?"

A man in the front row said, "Seven."

"You have seven opportunities to lie."

She wrote on the board:

ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

Julian copied it.

Then crossed out truth and wrote state.

ONE SOURCE OF STATE.

After class Dr. Sutter stopped him.

"Why'd you change it?"

He looked at his notebook.

"You saw that?"

"I saw you argue with my board."

"I wasn't arguing."

"You crossed out my word."

"Truth sounds bigger than a database."

She studied him.

"Good."

"Good?"

"If you thought a database could hold truth I'd tell you to change majors."

He smiled.

She pointed at his notebook.

"But state can be wrong too."

"Yeah."

"So what matters?"

Julian thought of the temporary security sheet in his desk drawer.

"Previous state."

Dr. Sutter nodded once.

"Now you're paying for the class."

The next semester she assigned a paper on large-scale information filtering.

That was how Julian first encountered Elias Vane.

Vane was not famous then.

He had written a paper with a title so dull Julian nearly skipped it: *Relevance Allocation in High-Volume Civic Information Environments*.

The argument irritated him.

Vane's basic claim was that no person could meaningfully process the number of signals generated by a large modern society. Information abundance, he wrote, did not automatically create informed citizens. Without filtering, high-volume systems favored emotionally salient noise, repetition, and adversarial actors willing to spend more effort capturing attention.

Therefore public systems would increasingly require machine-assisted relevance selection.

Julian wrote in the margin:

WHO PICKS RELEVANT?

Three pages later Vane answered.

Nobody, ideally.

The system would rank information against declared goals, contextual needs, urgency, reliability, and user preference.

Julian wrote:

THAT'S PICKING.

The next paragraph acknowledged that too.

Filtering, Vane wrote, was a governance act whether performed by editor, teacher, search engine, administrator, market, social network, or individual attention. The proper question was not whether filtering occurred but whether its criteria were legible, contestable, and bounded.

Julian stopped writing in the margin.

He read the paper again.

For his assignment he attacked it anyway.

He argued that a relevance system could become more powerful than a censorship system because people would mistake omission for absence. A censor told you something had been hidden. A relevance filter simply made the hidden thing difficult to encounter.

Dr. Sutter gave him an A-minus and wrote:

You are arguing with the strongest version. Good. Now distinguish mechanism from abuse.

Julian did not know how.

The line kept crawling.

At the Annex, another security camera appeared above the rear entrance.

Julian asked why.

"Package theft," Redding said.

"Thought these were for emergencies."

"Packages are an emergency if yours gets stolen."

The joke bothered Julian more than the camera.

Not because cameras were sinister.

Because the reason had changed without the hardware changing.

The same device moved from extraordinary threat to ordinary management without any moment at which somebody had to admit the transition.

That spring, city employees received new identification cards with magnetic stripes.

The cards initially opened buildings.

Then the cafeteria.

Then parking gates.

Then printers.

Then time clocks.

The same credential was useful because the same credential was already there.

Julian appreciated the convenience.

He also noticed that convenience had a direction.

Systems rarely became less connected on their own.

His mother thought he was becoming paranoid.

"Nobody cares when you print something."

"The printer cares."

"The printer doesn't care."

"It records it."

"That's not caring."

"What's the difference if the record changes what happens later?"

She put down the mail.

"You got a tuition bill."

"That's caring."

She laughed.

Julian worked overtime.

He completed the certificate program without deciding whether he intended to become a programmer. He liked code, but he liked machinery better. Software could claim success while nothing physical happened. A pump could not.

One night, after replacing a failed relay in the Annex, he climbed to the roof.

Bellwether spread around him in cold orange light.

Television antennas still crowded old rooftops, but more satellite dishes had appeared. Cell towers blinked red above warehouses. Fiber crews had spent the summer opening the same streets the gas company had closed the year before.

The city was growing a nervous system.

Julian could see parts of it.

Not the information, obviously. The conduits.

A helicopter crossed toward the river.

For one second he imagined the city as a creature too large to know itself, communicating through cables laid by contractors who cursed one another's work, remembering through databases nobody had designed to cooperate, seeing through cameras funded for one emergency and retained for another purpose.

It did not feel sinister.

It felt alive.

That was worse.

A sinister thing could be opposed.

A living thing had needs.

The roof door opened.

Redding stepped out.

"You smoking now?"

"No."

"Then why are you up here freezing?"

"Thinking."

"Don't do that on city time."

Julian smiled.

Redding joined him at the parapet.

For a minute they watched traffic.

"You ever think," Julian said, "that we keep installing things nobody knows how to take out?"

"All the time."

"Doesn't bother you?"

Redding shrugged.

"Buildings are layers."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know what you mean."

Redding rubbed his hands together.

"You think somebody's making a plan."

"I didn't say that."

"Good, because they're not."

"That supposed to make me feel better?"

Redding laughed quietly.

"No."

Below them, the city glowed.

Somewhere in the Annex, the television was still on.

The line crawled even when no one watched it.


Dr. Sutter made the class model an emergency database as a semester project.

Each team received the same fictional city.

Hospitals.

Schools.

Roads.

Power substations.

Shelters.

Police.

Fire.

Transit.

The assignment sounded easy until the students tried to decide what counted as the same person.

A hospital patient had one identifier.

The school system another.

Police records another.

Utility accounts belonged to households.

Transit cards belonged to tokens.

A shelter might know only a name, or a name spelled wrong.

"Use social security number," someone said.

Dr. Sutter shook her head.

"Children?"

"Date of birth and name."

"Twins?"

"Address."

"Homeless?"

"Then—"

The class fell silent.

Julian enjoyed this.

Identity seemed obvious until a database had to execute it.

They built composite keys.

Then exceptions.

Then override procedures.

Then procedures for who could authorize overrides.

By the end of the project, the table that represented people had become the most complicated table in the system.

"This is why universal identity projects fail," Dr. Sutter said.

A student objected.

"They don't fail. Government has IDs."

"Government has many IDs."

"Make one."

"For which purpose?"

"Everything."

Dr. Sutter smiled.

"That word should frighten engineers."

After class Julian asked why.

"Because everything is where scope goes to die."

She tapped his textbook.

"A system designed for one purpose inherits assumptions from that purpose. When you reuse the identifier, you import assumptions people stop noticing."

Julian thought of badges that had become cafeteria cards and printer credentials.

"You could design it general from the start."

"You could."

"Then?"

"Then you would make assumptions about purposes that do not exist yet."

He hated this answer.

"There's no way to do it right?"

"Of course there is."

"What?"

"Make assumptions visible. Keep authority narrow. Preserve migration paths. Admit uncertainty."

"That's four ways."

"Engineers pay by the comma."

Years later Julian would remember that sentence when people spoke of technology as though its central question were intelligence.

Intelligence was rarely the shortage.

Scope was.

On the roof with Redding, Julian began asking which systems were allowed to talk to which.

The access-control database knew when employees entered buildings.

Timekeeping knew when they clocked in.

Parking knew which cars entered garages.

The employee directory knew department.

Security knew badge status.

"Do these connect?" Julian asked.

"Some."

"Could you tell if someone entered a building before clocking in?"

"Probably."

"Does anybody?"

"Probably."

"Who?"

Redding laughed.

"Julian, you think I am the city."

"You've been here thirty years."

"Exactly. I know enough to know there isn't one city."

Below them, traffic lights changed in coordinated waves.

Julian watched.

"There should be."

"What?"

"One city."

Redding turned.

"Careful."

"What?"

"That is how people get ideas."

"What kind?"

"The kind that require consultants."

Julian laughed, but the desire remained.

One city.

One record.

One view.

It sounded efficient.

It sounded sane.

He had not yet met Elena, and therefore had nobody nearby whose professional instinct was to ask what one record had to erase in order to become one.



One night after class Dr. Sutter found Julian in the computer lab testing backup restores.

"Assignment was over an hour ago."

"I know."

"Then why are you still breaking my database?"

"Backup says successful."

"Good."

"Restore doesn't."

"Better."

Julian looked at her.

"Better?"

"You learned something."

"The backup is bad."

"The report is bad."

"Same outcome."

"Different repair."

She sat beside him.

The software had produced a green check mark every night.

No one had tested recovery.

Dr. Sutter said, "Systems love proving they performed the operation they know how to measure."

Julian tried another restore.

Failed.

"How do you certify backup?"

"Restore it."

"Every time?"

"Enough to know the chain."

He thought of emergency plans nobody exercised, badge databases nobody reconciled, cameras nobody checked until after incidents.

A record claiming readiness was not readiness.

Later he added a line to TEMPORARY.txt:

TEST RETURN, NOT JUST WRITE.

He did not know this would become one of his permanent rules.



The first municipal attempt at one employee identity failed because of middle initials.

Julian learned this during a data-cleanup weekend when Human Resources, Security, Payroll, and Information Services tried to reconcile personnel directories.

The meeting occupied a windowless training room and produced more hostility than most elections.

A woman from Payroll said, "We have one authoritative employee number."

Security said, "Badge system uses another."

Human Resources said, "Our number is authoritative."

Payroll said, "Ours pays them."

Security said, "Ours opens doors."

Julian sat against the wall with coffee, officially present because access-control exports were involved.

A contractor displayed unmatched records.

JAMES R HOLT.

JAMES ROBERT HOLT.

JIM HOLT.

JAMES HOLT JR.

JAMES HOLT.

Same person?

Sometimes.

Not always.

One employee had changed surname after marriage.

Another had never updated Security.

A third was accidentally sharing a date of birth with an unrelated employee because one legacy system stored an unknown day as January first.

The contractor proposed a master identity table.

"Good," Payroll said.

"Who owns it?" HR asked.

The room became quiet.

The question was not technical.

Everyone had wanted one source until one source required one owner.

Julian raised his hand.

Nobody invited him to.

"What if nobody owns all of it?"

The contractor said, "Then you don't have a master."

"Have references."

"To what?"

"Department source."

"Then queries are slower."

"Yes."

"Conflicts persist."

"Visible conflicts."

The contractor looked at him with professional pity.

"Normalization exists to eliminate inconsistency."

"Some inconsistency is real."

HR said, "Such as?"

"Security says person has badge. Payroll says terminated. That's not duplicate noise. That's a problem."

The room changed.

Not because Julian was brilliant.

Because everyone could imagine the terminated employee whose badge still opened a door.

The contractor added conflict-state handling.

The weekend ran long.

On Sunday night Julian sat alone with the reconciliation report.

Thousands matched.

Dozens did not.

The unmatched list looked like failure.

He printed it.

The printout later proved more useful than the successful merge.

Every unresolved identity was a place where the city admitted it did not yet know.

Julian began to respect explicit incompleteness.

### Chapter Three — Profiles

The first time Julian saw Elena Mor, she was arguing with a scanner.

Not the person operating the scanner.

The scanner itself.

"This is not preservation," she said.

A young contractor in a polo shirt stared at her.

The machine pulled another sheet of brittle paper under a glass plate.

"Ma'am, it is literally an archival scanner."

"No. It is a scanner being used in an archive."

Julian, who had come to repair a dehumidifier, stopped in the doorway.

The municipal records annex occupied what had once been a bank. The basement vault held maps, property records, old engineering drawings, court ledgers, procurement documents, payroll books, and several decades of paper nobody wanted until the day someone desperately needed it.

The air smelled like cardboard and stone.

The contractor lifted both hands.

"We're creating digital copies."

"Of the current document."

"Yes."

"What happens to the previous copy?"

He looked at the page.

"This is the previous copy."

"No."

Elena leaned across the table.

"This page is revision four. Revisions one through three were removed from the binder before your company received it."

"That's not our issue."

"It becomes your issue when your project is called Historical Preservation."

The contractor looked at Julian as if maintenance might have authority over philosophy.

Julian pointed toward the back room.

"Dehumidifier."

The contractor nodded as though Julian had wisely declined combat.

Elena watched him pass.

She was perhaps twenty-seven, dark-haired, thin, wearing a gray cardigan with an elbow repaired by hand. A pencil held her hair in a knot that seemed temporary but structurally sound.

The dehumidifier had failed because a drain line was clogged with mineral scale.

Julian cleared it in twelve minutes.

When he came back through the scanning room, Elena was alone.

"Win?"

"No."

"Machine apologize?"

She looked at him.

Then smiled.

"It has not yet acquired sufficient self-awareness."

"Give the city time."

He signed the work order at the desk.

She glanced at his handwriting.

"Julian Vance."

"That's me."

"Any relation?"

"To who?"

"Elias Vane."

"Different spelling."

"Yes."

"Also, no."

She looked mildly disappointed.

"You know who he is?"

"Read a paper."

"Which?"

"Relevance allocation."

"And?"

"I wanted to punch it."

"Good paper."

"Still wanted to punch it."

Elena nodded.

"That's usually a good sign."

He handed her the pink copy of the work order.

She inspected it.

"You keep the yellow?"

"Office does."

"And when they enter it?"

"Computer."

"What happens to the yellow?"

"Box."

"And the box?"

"Storage."

"And eventually?"

Julian looked at her.

"You really do this all day."

"Somebody has to."

He leaned on the desk.

"Why do you care about old versions if the current one is right?"

Elena took a moment.

"Right about what?"

"Whatever it's for."

"Suppose a bridge was built from drawing three."

"Okay."

"Drawing seven is current."

"Okay."

"The bridge fails."

"Okay."

"Do you investigate drawing seven?"

Julian considered.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because that's not what they built."

Elena tapped the pink work order against the desk.

"Exactly."

That was the first thing she ever taught him.

They got coffee three days later.

Bellwether in those years had begun filling with cafés where customers worked on laptops for hours while purchasing one drink. Julian found this behavior economically mysterious. Elena explained that the customer was not buying coffee.

"They're buying temporary jurisdiction."

"Over the chair?"

"Over the fact nobody asks why they're sitting there."

"You could sit in a library."

"Library closes."

"So does this."

"Later."

At the next table, two college students were taking photographs of themselves with a digital camera.

They reviewed each image immediately, deleting most of them.

"What are they doing?" Julian asked.

"Producing evidence."

"Of coffee?"

"Of being people who drink coffee."

He looked at her.

"You're weird."

"I work in archives."

"That's not a defense."

A student nearby was explaining a new website where everybody received a page with a photograph, interests, friends, school information, favorite music, and public messages.

"Why?" Julian said.

Elena listened.

"Searchable identity."

"For who?"

"Everyone, apparently."

"Sounds terrible."

"You have an email address."

"That's not my identity."

"It will be if people decide it is."

He shook his head.

"No one wants a public file on themselves."

Elena's expression changed into the look he would later recognize as her archivist's pleasure at encountering a statement time could destroy.

"Keep talking."

Within two years, Julian had a profile.

He did not create it himself.

A coworker named Benson created one for him during lunch because a woman from Payroll had asked whether Julian was "on there."

"What'd you put?" Julian asked.

"Basic stuff."

"Delete it."

"You haven't seen it."

"I don't need to see it."

Benson turned the monitor.

There was Julian's name.

A photograph from a work picnic.

Relationship status.

Employer.

Hometown.

Favorite bands, most of them wrong.

"Why am I twenty-six?"

"Better range."

"I'm twenty-three."

"You're welcome."

Julian reached for the mouse.

Benson slapped his hand away.

"Relax. Nobody believes profiles."

"Then why have them?"

"Because girls do."

This logic apparently defeated all objection.

Julian corrected his age.

He removed his employer.

He replaced the photograph.

He deleted the favorite bands.

Then he spent forty minutes looking up people he had not seen since high school.

That night he logged in again.

The interesting thing was not the profile.

The interesting thing was the graph.

People Julian had known in separate parts of his life now appeared adjacent because the software had discovered their common connection to him. Former classmates, cousins, city workers, an old boxing coach, a woman he had dated for three weeks, people from community college, people he had forgotten he knew.

Human memory had boundaries.

The graph did not respect them.

Elena refused to join.

"You have a driver's license," Julian told her.

"The Commonwealth requires that."

"Your job has a directory."

"My job requires that."

"You buy things with a card."

She stared at him.

"Are you trying to persuade me by listing existing surveillance?"

"I'm saying you're already in systems."

"That doesn't create an obligation to volunteer more."

"You're missing people."

"People know my phone number."

"Some don't."

"Then perhaps we have accurately modeled our relationship."

He laughed.

She did not.

Months later the profile site introduced a feature called the Stream.

Previously, if Julian wanted to know what someone had done, he visited that person's page.

Now the site told him automatically.

Benson changed relationship status.

A cousin joined a group.

A woman from Purchasing added photographs.

Someone Julian barely remembered wrote something angry at two in the morning.

The system had transformed human life from a collection of pages into weather.

People hated it.

For about a week.

There were petitions.

Groups formed to protest the Stream using the Stream.

Users complained that ordinary actions had become unnaturally visible.

The company added controls.

Then everyone kept using it.

Julian found the whole episode funny until he realized his own behavior had changed.

He no longer looked people up.

He waited for them to arrive.

This bothered him enough that he called Elena.

"I think you were right."

"About what?"

"The Stream."

"I don't know what that is."

"The feed thing."

"I remain blissfully unqualified."

"It's like—before, you had to go somewhere to know something. Now it tells you."

"Like television."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Television doesn't know who I know."

There was a pause.

"That," Elena said, "is a better distinction."

The municipal network changed in parallel.

An employee portal that had begun as a place to download forms began presenting personalized notices. Required training. Benefits deadlines. Building alerts. Suggested documents. Messages based on department.

Julian appreciated it.

That was important.

He would later become suspicious of people who told the story of technology as if everyone had been dragged unwillingly into obvious traps.

They had not.

Useful things were useful.

The portal saved him time.

The profile site reconnected him with a friend from childhood.

The Stream told him his cousin had a baby.

Navigation software kept him from getting lost in a neighboring state.

Online banking prevented overdraft fees twice.

The network solved problems.

That was why it spread.

One Saturday Elena invited him to the records annex after hours.

"That sounds illegal."

"It is not illegal."

"Why after hours?"

"Because during hours people ask why I'm doing my job."

She had laid six versions of the same city procurement policy across a table.

The newest version existed digitally.

The older versions existed only on paper.

"What am I looking at?"

"Paragraph twelve."

Julian read.

In the first version, emergency purchasing authority expired after ninety days.

In the second, one hundred eighty.

In the third, it could be renewed by department heads.

In the fourth, renewals required only administrative notice.

In the fifth, the word emergency had disappeared from the heading.

The sixth called the same authority expedited procurement.

Julian read them again.

"So?"

"So when did the emergency power become ordinary power?"

He pointed to the fifth.

"No."

"Fourth?"

"No."

"Then when?"

"That's the problem."

"There had to be a vote."

"There were six."

"Then that's when."

Elena shook her head.

"Each vote describes itself as a limited procedural modification to the previous state. Nobody appears to have voted on the final state."

Julian sat down.

On the far side of the room a scanner slept with one green light.

"Maybe that's normal."

"It is normal."

"Then why are you showing me?"

She gathered the pages.

"Because normal is not the same as harmless."

He watched her return each version to its sleeve.

"You're making this sound like somebody planned it."

"I didn't say anyone planned it."

"Then what are you saying?"

Elena looked at him.

"Systems can arrive at states nobody authorized globally because everybody authorized something locally."

Julian thought of Vane's paper.

He thought of the camera above the rear entrance.

He thought of the Stream.

He thought of himself correcting his age, selecting a better photograph, adding people, accepting the usefulness of having them delivered to him.

"That sounds like an excuse."

"For who?"

"Everybody."

"Maybe."

She slid the binder back into its box.

"Or a warning."

On his way home, Julian stopped at a red light.

His phone buzzed.

He looked down.

The Stream had informed him that Elena Mor had joined the network.

For several seconds he stared at the notification.

Then he called her.

"You joined."

"No."

"It says you joined."

"I didn't."

"I'm looking at your name."

"Is there a photograph?"

"No."

"Friends?"

"Three."

"Who?"

He read the names.

Elena was silent.

"You know them?"

"Two."

"So maybe you—"

"I did not join."

Julian glanced at the light.

A horn sounded behind him. It had turned green.

He drove through the intersection.

"When I get home," Elena said, "I'll look."

"Probably somebody made it for you. Benson made mine."

"Who is Benson?"

"Exactly."

"You think that's funny."

"A little."

"It has my name."

"Names aren't unique."

"Julian."

"What?"

"One of those three people died six years ago."

The line went quiet.

He thought he had misheard her.

"What?"

"The third name."

"You said you know two."

"I knew three."

Traffic moved around him.

The phone remained warm against his ear.

"Maybe it's an old account."

"The site didn't exist."

Julian almost said *that's impossible*, but the sentence seemed useless.

Elena spoke first.

"Don't touch anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"Screenshot it."

"Why?"

"Because if we're wrong, it doesn't matter."

"And if we're right?"

"Then I want the previous state."

Julian pulled over.

When he reopened the profile, Elena Mor had no friends.

He took the screenshot anyway.


The false Elena profile became their first shared experiment.

They set rules.

No contacting the account.

No assuming identity.

No inventing motive.

Record only state changes they could independently observe.

Elena created a table.

Julian objected to the number of columns.

"Timestamp, URL, internal identifier, visible fields, friend count, source device, source account, capture hash."

"Capture what?"

"Hash."

"Of a screenshot?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"To show it didn't change."

"Who's accusing us of changing it?"

"No one."

"Then why?"

"Because if this ever matters, future us will be strangers."

Julian stared at her.

"That may be the most depressing thing you've said."

"It's archival optimism."

"Explain."

"I assume future us will care enough to check."

They logged five appearances.

Each account behaved differently.

One existed for eleven minutes.

One for two days.

One accepted a friend request from someone Elena had worked with in college and then disappeared before the person could send a message.

One had an internal identifier that, according to the site, predated the site's public launch.

Julian immediately became excited.

Elena did not.

"Clock migration."

"Could be."

"Imported beta data."

"Could be."

"Bad display."

"Could be."

"You're ruining this."

"Good."

The fifth profile contained a single public status:

I HAVE ALWAYS BEEN HERE.

Julian read it aloud.

"Now that's creepy."

Elena said nothing.

The status disappeared while they watched.

Julian refreshed.

Blank page.

He refreshed again.

Profile unavailable.

He looked at Elena.

"Still clock migration?"

"Now we have a new observation."

"You are biologically incapable of fear."

"Not true."

"What scares you?"

"People who confuse fear with proof."

He wanted to kiss her.

This seemed inappropriate to the experiment and therefore he did.

Their first kiss occurred in front of an apparently impossible social-media account impersonating Elena.

Neither of them later knew what that meant.

The account disappeared.

Their relationship did not.

For a while.



Julian's mother joined the profile network before Elena did.

She used a photograph Julian hated because it showed her at a family cookout holding a paper plate.

"Why that picture?"

"I look good."

"You're eating corn."

"I look good eating corn."

She added cousins Julian had forgotten.

Old neighbors.

People from church.

Former coworkers.

Within weeks the network revived a version of South Bellwether Julian remembered from childhood.

People who had moved away reappeared in comments.

Dead people reappeared in tagged photographs posted by surviving relatives.

His mother's page became a communal porch.

Julian had assumed the network would make people more isolated because they were looking at screens.

Instead he watched his mother speak more often to a friend in Georgia than she had in twenty years.

That complicated his suspicion.

One evening she showed him a photograph of his father taken before Julian was born.

"Where'd that come from?"

"Carol had it."

"Who's Carol?"

"Exactly."

His father stood beside a car wearing a shirt Julian had seen only in another photograph.

The image entered his memory immediately.

Within days Julian could not remember whether he had always known it.

That disturbed him more than he admitted.

A new record did not merely add to memory.

It could rewrite the felt boundary of what had been remembered.

He asked Elena about it.

"Memory is reconstructive."

"I know."

"Then?"

"I don't like how fast the new picture became old in my head."

Elena nodded.

"Save source."

He did.

### Chapter Four — The Black Phone

The phone arrived in Bellwether as an object people laughed at before they bought it.

Julian first saw one in the hand of a project manager from the private security contractor that maintained cameras at the airport.

The project manager's name was Kevin Pike, and he had the smooth, lightly exhausted face of a man who answered email before brushing his teeth.

He came to the Annex because a camera server had stopped accepting timestamps from three older devices.

Julian found him standing in the mechanical room, moving his thumb across a black rectangle.

"What is that?"

Pike held it out.

"Phone."

"That's not a phone."

"It makes calls."

"So does a phone."

"It has the internet."

"So does a computer."

"It's also a music player."

"So you paid for three things that are worse than the three things you already had."

Pike smiled.

"Touch it."

Julian did not.

The front was almost entirely glass.

No buttons except one.

Pike dragged a finger across the screen and the screen obeyed.

Julian's skepticism weakened involuntarily.

"Okay."

"Right?"

"Still stupid."

"You want one."

"No."

"You absolutely want one."

"I want to take it apart."

"Same thing for you."

Pike showed him a map.

The map moved under his fingers.

Then photographs.

Then a web page.

Then an email.

Then a photograph of his daughter wearing swim goggles in a kitchen.

The transitions were fast enough that the device stopped feeling like a collection of applications.

It felt like a hole.

You reached through it and grabbed whatever you wanted.

Julian handed it back.

"Battery life?"

"Not great."

"Service?"

"Not great."

"Durability?"

Pike looked at the glass.

"Don't drop it."

"Then it's not a tool."

Pike put it in his shirt pocket.

"Everything's a toy right before it becomes a tool."

Julian remembered the sentence.

He bought one nineteen months later.

By then half the people he knew had a device close enough to the black phone that nobody used the phrase smart phone anymore except salesmen.

The first week Julian had it, he became intolerable.

He checked weather while standing beneath weather.

He looked up restaurant reviews while sitting inside the restaurant.

He settled arguments nobody needed settled.

He photographed equipment labels rather than writing them down.

He stopped printing maps.

He answered email from tunnels.

He searched error codes while kneeling beside broken hardware and experienced the small, private intoxication of not needing to walk back to a desktop.

Elena hated it immediately.

"You're carrying a slot machine."

"It's not a slot machine."

"It rewards you for touching it."

"So does a piano."

"Your piano tells everyone where it is?"

"I don't have a piano."

"Good."

They were standing in the old records vault where Elena had become, by some combination of stubbornness and attrition, the unofficial custodian of materials scheduled for destruction.

Her official title had changed three times without improving clarity.

Records Specialist.

Digital Continuity Analyst.

Information Steward.

She called herself archivist.

Julian held the phone above a blueprint.

"Look."

The camera found the text.

He enlarged a handwritten note.

Elena leaned closer despite herself.

"Useful," she said.

"Thank you."

"Still a slot machine."

"You want one?"

"No."

"You want this one."

"Absolutely not."

"Touch it."

She glared.

He laughed.

The war continued on the screens.

Not one war exactly.

Operations acquired names and lost them. Regions changed color on maps. A dictator was found. A government fell. Another government nearly fell. New words entered ordinary speech: insurgency, improvised device, detainee, surge, contractor, collateral.

Julian watched mostly in fragments.

Break room.

Barbershop.

Waiting room.

Television over the laundromat counter.

News site while a firmware update ran.

Tiny article on the black phone while standing in line for coffee.

The war no longer arrived as a single broadcast.

It seeped.

He knew enough to have opinions and not enough to know whether they belonged to him.

One afternoon Alvarez slammed a newspaper onto the lunch table.

"They found another one."

"Another what?" Benson asked.

"Road bomb. Took out three guys."

Redding kept eating soup.

Benson looked at the television.

The television was showing a hearing about whether officials had misrepresented something years earlier.

"Whole thing was bullshit," Alvarez said.

Redding said, "Everything isn't bullshit because one thing was wrong."

"That's not what I said."

"It's what you sound like."

Julian looked between them.

"When did you change your mind?"

Alvarez said, "About what?"

"The war."

"I didn't change my mind."

"You used to say they had to go in."

"Yeah, before we knew."

"Knew what?"

Alvarez stared at him.

"You know."

"No, I mean exactly."

"You trying to start something?"

"No. I'm asking."

Benson said, "Julian asks questions like a cop."

Redding said, "Worse. Cops eventually leave."

The room laughed.

Alvarez did not.

Julian returned to his sandwich.

It bothered him that he could remember Alvarez saying something different three years earlier but could not remember the exact words.

He could not prove the difference even to himself.

That evening, he searched his old email.

Nothing.

Then his profile messages.

Nothing.

Then an abandoned discussion board where several city workers had posted after the attacks.

He found Alvarez's username.

The posts were still there.

Some supported intervention.

Some were cautious.

None matched Julian's memory exactly.

He read them twice.

The record did not prove Alvarez had never said what Julian remembered.

It proved only that the available record was incomplete.

The distinction irritated him.

The phone buzzed.

A message from Elena.

YOU ARE NOW ONE OF THOSE MEN WHO ARGUES WITH THE PAST AT MIDNIGHT.

He called her.

"How do you know what I'm doing?"

"You sent me four links."

"I did not."

"Check your sent messages."

He did.

Four links.

He had shared them without noticing he was sharing them, using a new browser button that appeared whenever he highlighted text.

"Okay," he said.

"Slot machine."

"Shut up."

There was another reason the phone bothered Elena.

The profile bearing her name had returned.

It appeared occasionally, never for long.

No photograph.

Different friend counts.

Sometimes no information at all.

The account would exist for a day or a week, then disappear.

Julian had begun taking screenshots.

"What if it's a bug?" he asked.

"Then the screenshots document a bug."

"What if somebody is messing with you?"

"Then they document that."

"What if—"

"Do you hear yourself?"

"What?"

"You're trying to choose an explanation because having none makes you uncomfortable."

Julian sat on the edge of his bed.

"Fine. We don't know."

"Correct."

"You enjoy saying that."

"I enjoy not lying."

"Those aren't opposites."

"They should be closer."

The black phone made uncertainty easier to escape.

There was always another result.

Another article.

Another person.

Another map.

Another opinion.

Julian began to understand Vane's filtering problem.

Abundance did not end ignorance.

It made ignorance harder to locate.

One could spend six hours searching and emerge with greater confidence but no clearer provenance.

The city adopted mobile devices faster than its policies could be rewritten.

Maintenance supervisors began texting work orders.

Employees photographed damage.

Security personnel emailed incident pictures before writing reports.

Building inspectors used map applications.

Transit managers received live passenger updates.

Nothing had been approved as a grand transformation.

People simply used the faster tool.

Then software was purchased to formalize what they were already doing.

The workflow followed behavior rather than preceding it.

This struck Julian as both democratic and dangerous.

At the Annex, the emergency operations room acquired a wall display connected to public news, police dispatch summaries, transportation feeds, weather, and building alarms.

The room had once required twelve people to gather information.

Now one person could see most of it.

Redding stood in front of the new display the day it went live.

"Beautiful."

Julian looked at the sixteen boxes.

"Where's it get this?"

"Everywhere."

"That's not a place."

"You installed it."

"I installed screens."

"Then ask the screens."

Julian found the documentation.

The system imported data from seven departments and three contractors.

Each source used different update intervals.

Some were live.

Some refreshed every fifteen minutes.

One public-safety feed could be delayed an hour without displaying any warning.

He showed Redding.

"So?"

"So you can look at one screen and think everything is now."

"Mostly is."

"Mostly now is not now."

Redding rubbed his forehead.

"Put a note."

Julian did.

DATA REFRESH RATES VARY BY SOURCE.

The note stayed for three weeks.

Someone removed it because it looked unprofessional.

The television still showed war.

Now the phone did too.

Julian noticed something else.

When a story first broke, everyone saw approximately the same thing.

Hours later, they did not.

One site emphasized casualties.

Another politics.

Another markets.

Another scandal.

Another military strategy.

The common event fractured into audience-shaped versions.

The event remained.

The pathway to it changed.

Late one night Julian was replacing a network switch in a public-safety building when his phone received an alert.

A bombing overseas.

He opened it.

The page displayed a short summary and links.

Before he could read, another alert appeared.

Then another.

The same event.

Different numbers.

Different certainty.

He placed the phone on the floor beside his tools.

The switch in front of him had twelve blinking ports.

A packet did not care whether its content was true.

It cared whether the address existed.

He completed the repair.

As he was packing, Pike—the airport project manager, now promoted somewhere into management—called.

"Still got the black phone?"

"Third one."

"Told you."

"Don't get excited."

"I win."

"You sold me nothing."

"History vindicated me."

Julian looked at the phone on the floor.

"History doesn't vindicate people."

"What?"

"It just keeps copies if you're lucky."

"Jesus, man. I'm calling about a camera contract."

Julian laughed.

"Sorry."

"What are you doing down there?"

"Maintenance."

"On what?"

Julian looked at the switch.

"Everything."


The black phone changed driving before Julian admitted it had changed anything important.

He had always kept a folding map in the glove compartment.

Bellwether streets were familiar, but municipal work sent him into suburbs, industrial corridors, half-abandoned river wards, and neighboring counties whose roads seemed to have been named during arguments.

With the phone, wrong turns became temporary.

The blue dot moved.

The route recalculated.

Julian stopped planning.

This was the first cognitive function he knowingly outsourced.

He noticed because the paper map remained in the glove compartment.

Six months later he tried driving to a pump station without navigation and took the wrong exit twice.

This offended him.

"You made yourself worse," Elena said.

"I made something else better."

"Did you?"

"I arrive faster."

"That's an outcome."

"Yes."

"Not the same as you being better."

"Why do you make useful things sound morally suspicious?"

"I don't."

"You absolutely do."

"I make dependency visible."

"Same voice."

They were driving to a wedding in another state.

Julian had the phone mounted on the dashboard.

The device spoke directions in a woman's calm synthetic voice.

Elena disliked the voice.

"Why?"

"It sounds certain."

"It's reading a route."

"It says 'recalculating' like I made a mistake."

"You did. You told me to get off."

"The road looked wrong."

"The road was right."

"The machine was right."

"Yes."

"I dislike it."

Julian smiled.

The phone directed them through three turns neither would have chosen.

They arrived twelve minutes early.

At the reception, people placed phones on tables beside forks.

Every few minutes a screen lit.

Somebody glanced.

An invisible hand entering a visible gathering.

A cousin showed photographs from a war zone taken by a friend of a friend serving overseas.

The images had arrived that morning.

Dust.

A burned vehicle.

A room with a hole in a wall.

Soldiers making faces beside bottled water.

The war suddenly contained grocery-store lighting and bad composition.

It looked less like television and more like work.

Julian realized official images had trained him to expect visual grammar.

Wide shots.

Flags.

Podiums.

Maps.

Professional framing.

The soldiers' photographs contained no argument.

That did not make them neutral.

It made their selection harder to interpret.

On the drive home Elena slept.

Julian listened to radio news until he became tired of repetition.

He switched to music stored on the phone.

Then a podcast.

Then silence.

At a red light the screen offered a notification:

NEW DEVELOPMENTS IN—

Julian turned the screen face down.

For perhaps fifteen miles the car contained only tires and road.

He felt relief.

Then anxiety.

What if something happened?

Something was always happening.

That was the new condition.

Not danger.

Availability.

The network had made the world's unfinished state continuously addressable.

Nobody had decided that citizens needed this.

Nobody had to.

The phone made it possible.

People built habits around possibility.




The phone also changed waiting.

Before the black phone, waiting had texture.

Bus stop.

Doctor's office.

Elevator lobby.

Line at the permit counter.

People stared.

Read old magazines.

Listened to strangers.

Now every pause acquired a door.

Julian noticed this while waiting for a replacement breaker at an electrical supply warehouse.

The clerk said ten minutes.

Julian checked mail.

News.

Weather.

A forum about control systems.

A message from Elena.

A photograph from Marcus.

A work ticket.

The clerk called his name.

Twenty-three minutes had passed.

Julian felt cheated.

The phone had removed boredom and with it his sense of elapsed time.

He mentioned this to Elena.

"You're upset the device made waiting easier."

"I'm upset I didn't know I waited."

"Why?"

"Because then ten minutes can become twenty-three without friction."

She looked at him.

"That is the most municipal complaint possible."

He laughed.

But he began noticing how many transitions entered through removed friction.

Buy now.

Reply now.

Navigate now.

Approve now.

Rate now.

Share now.

The network's great promise was not intelligence.

It was fewer pauses.

Julian wondered what pauses had been doing besides annoying people.

### Chapter Five — Easy Houses

The houses became cheap by becoming expensive.

That was how Julian understood it afterward.

At the time, everybody explained it differently.

Interest rates were low.

Credit was available.

Property only went up.

Rent was throwing money away.

Bellwether was coming back.

The old industrial neighborhoods were undervalued.

The suburbs were expanding.

Homeownership was responsible.

Homeownership was adulthood.

Homeownership was wealth.

Homeownership was safety.

Julian's friend Marcus bought first.

Marcus worked for the transit authority and made four dollars an hour more than Julian.

He purchased a three-bedroom house with a finished basement, attached garage, and a deck overlooking a retention pond.

Julian drove out one Sunday.

The development had curving streets named after trees that had been removed during construction.

Marcus stood in the driveway with a beer.

"Look at this."

"I am."

"No, look."

Marcus opened the garage.

Two cars.

New lawn mower.

Television box as tall as a refrigerator.

"How much?"

"Doesn't matter."

"That's usually when it matters."

Marcus waved him inside.

The kitchen counters were stone.

The refrigerator had an ice dispenser.

His wife, Kendra, was choosing paint colors from a fan deck.

Julian walked from room to room.

The house was enormous by the standards of the row house where he grew up.

"How much did you put down?"

### Chapter Six — Mortgage Winter

Bellwether survived the recession the way an old building survives winter: by discovering which repairs had been postponed too long.

Street resurfacing stopped.

Libraries shortened hours.

Two recreation centers closed.

School roofs leaked into buckets.

The city deferred replacement of emergency generators Julian had already deferred replacing the year before.

At work, every budget meeting acquired the same phrase.

Do more with less.

Redding hated the phrase.

"Less is less," he said.

They were in the basement of a police administration building, staring at a cooling unit that had failed beside a server rack.

"If less was more," Redding continued, "we'd have been geniuses our whole lives."

The server room was ninety-three degrees.

Julian set up portable fans.

"What happens if these go down?"

"Reports."

"What reports?"

"Everything."

"Backed up?"

Redding looked at him.

"Ask Computer."

Computer Services said the data was backed up.

Julian asked where.

"Secondary facility."

"Where?"

"North campus."

"Same power grid?"

Silence.

"Probably not relevant."

Two days later a transformer failure took down both facilities.

The backup existed.

The power required to reach it did not.

Julian spent eighteen hours running temporary circuits while officers returned to paper.

Watching them work manually was instructive.

The process was slower.

It was also easier to understand.

A report sat on a desk.

A person carried it.

Another person signed it.

A supervisor knew what had not been processed because the pile remained physically visible.

When the database came back, unresolved work disappeared into queues whose existence depended on people knowing which screen to open.

Julian began keeping more paper.

Not because paper was superior.

Because he had learned that every medium concealed a different kind of failure.

The recession produced new systems.

Automated eligibility checks.

Fraud screening.

Electronic procurement approvals.

Centralized vendor lists.

Online unemployment claims.

Digital tax payments.

Each was sold partly as efficiency and partly as necessity.

The city had fewer employees.

Software had to absorb the missing labor.

Elias Vane became more visible during this period.

Julian saw him on a panel discussing institutional information overload.

Vane was younger than Julian expected, perhaps forty, with a narrow face and the irritating calm of someone who could complete a sentence while three other people interrupted him.

The host asked whether algorithmic decision systems endangered human judgment.

Vane said the question reversed the actual problem.

"Institutions already use algorithms," he said.

"Policies are algorithms. Forms are algorithms. Eligibility rules are algorithms. Management chains are algorithms executed by people. Software makes the logic faster and more consistent, which can reveal defects that human discretion previously hid."

A critic asked what happened when the software was wrong.

"The same thing that should happen when a person is wrong," Vane said. "You need provenance, appeal, and bounded authority."

Julian leaned toward the television.

The critic pressed.

"But software operates at scale."

"Yes."

"So mistakes scale."

"Yes."

"Then why automate?"

Vane smiled faintly.

"Because correct decisions scale too."

Not yet.
