# Chapter 3: The Sealed Number

The Records Annex was older than the men and women who worked in it. It had once been designed for the possibility that the computers would all fail at once. The designers had not trusted lights, voices or magnetic memory to remain loyal for a hundred and sixty years, so they had included paper presses, photographic film and shelves deep enough to stand a person in.

Most of those shelves held replacement water filters now. The paper presses were used for graduation certificates and anniversary poems. The preserved manual ledgers slept behind a climate door on the lowest floor.

Holt opened that door for Anja with two keys.

"Why two?" she asked.

"Because there used to be a third."

The air inside smelled of machine oil and dry wood. A wall clock ticked over a table made of material imported from Earth. Anja had never felt wood that old beneath her fingers. It was scarred by cups, burns and knife marks, small human violations the wood had survived.

Holt spread an accident log before her. INCIDENT E-72. PRESSURE AND FIRE LOSS. Fifty-six years and four months earlier, during a solar-particle event, a ventilation-control failure had caused fire and rapid decompression. The entry listed three hundred fourteen dead, eight hundred sixty evacuated, and a permanent sealing order. The signatures belonged to the captain, the chief engineer and the civic recorder.

"That is what we teach," Anja said.

"Yes."

"Is any of it wrong?"

"The fire happened. Some people died. The rest is not one kind of statement."

He pointed to a field beside EVACUATED: COMPLETE. There was no destination list. No receiving shelter, no medical roll, no transit count, only the word COMPLETE.

"A completion judgment," Holt said, "should have children. An arrival manifest. A ration addition. A treatment register. People who go somewhere continue to make records."

"Maybe the archives were damaged."

"Some were. I searched the uncompressed copies held by thirteen independent departments. I found eighty-seven arrivals, all before the seal order. Not eight hundred sixty."

"Then the missing were killed."

"Possibly. But the ship's death ledger names three hundred fourteen. It doesn't name another seven hundred seventy-three."

He left the arithmetic between them. Anja thought of the mass model. Sixteen hundred tons included plants, water and steel. Human bodies were a small fraction of it. A society could be physically hidden behind a blank administrative category.

"Who closed the ledger?"

Holt's eyes moved to the signatures. "Your father was a junior engineer on the seal team."

Anja had known that. She had known it the way a person knows a place name from an old map. When she was eight her father had told her the doors were dangerous and that good engineers kept their hands away from dangerous doors. He had never described the people inside.

"His name is on the technical supplement," Holt said.

He opened the supplement. It contained pressure measurements, flame-front predictions and a recommendation to isolate the cell for six minutes. Six minutes. The seal had lasted fifty-six years.

Anja saw a note in handwriting she recognized from her father's small repair cards: TEMPORARY HOLD PENDING MANUAL ACCOUNTING.

There was no later accounting.

"Where did the six minutes go?" she asked.

"That's the question I've been trying to ask for twelve years."

He handed her another sheet. It was an appeal filed by someone named Sada Sorel, aged fourteen. The date was one day after the accident. The petition requested reopening of a family passage and independent census certification. Its final line read: I HAVE NOT RECEIVED MY MOTHER'S BODY. I HAVE NOT RECEIVED MY MOTHER'S VOICE. DO NOT SAY SHE IS ABSENT FROM THE SHIP.

Anja sat down.

"My mother."

"Yes."

"She never told me."

"That can be true and this can be true."

Anja stared at the name. Her mother had a way of arranging grief into domestic prohibitions. There were foods they never ate, songs she refused to sing, corridors she would walk miles to avoid. Anja had treated those refusals as habits acquired by someone who had never liked the world outside their household. She had not considered they might be directions on a map.

"What happened to the appeal?"

"Rejected as procedurally redundant. The evacuation had been certified complete."

The phrase made Anja feel cold despite the climate controls.

***

She found her mother tending mushrooms in Habitat Twenty. Sada had made a business of coaxing food from materials other people threw away: sawdust, bitter paper, the leaves clipped from municipal vegetables. She wore a green apron and old glasses repaired with copper wire. Seeing her inside the humid glow of the growing racks, Anja wondered how much of her life she had inherited without knowing the invoice.

Sada greeted her by asking whether she had eaten. Anja said yes. It was a lie too ordinary to count.

"I found your appeal," Anja said.

Sada's knife stopped halfway through a stem.

"Then you went looking."

"I was looking at the accident."

"That would bring you to me sooner or later."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Which part?"

"The part where your mother was trapped behind a door that everyone says leads to vacuum."

Sada lowered the knife. "You were my daughter. Not a public hearing."

"I've been an engineer on the same ship for nineteen years. I work three cells away."

"You have been an engineer. That made me more afraid, not less."

"Afraid of what?"

"That you would open something because the numbers told you it ought to be open."

Anja looked at the mushrooms. Tiny pale heads pressed up from old soil in a place where nothing had grown naturally for generations.

"Is there someone alive in there?"

Sada would not look up. The silence lasted long enough to become an answer, then long enough to become something more deliberate.

"I need you to understand," Sada said, "that the door was not always locked from this side."

"Then from which side?"

Sada covered the growing tray with a clean cloth. "If you found the appeal, find what was filed after it. Not in the public annex. In the technical rolls your father kept."

"Where?"

"Under the floor of our old kitchen. The panel with the ugly yellow screw."

Anja almost smiled. Her father had always refused to replace that screw, even when she brought home the right part from the workshop.

"Why make it so hard?"

"Because he couldn't make it easy without bringing Command to the same place. He had to decide which way to fail."

Before Anja left, Sada touched her arm.

"One more thing. If you reach the door, do not tell the people behind it that you're rescuing them."

"Why not?"

"Because they know the history of being rescued by us."

***

The box beneath the kitchen contained three brass fasteners, a cracked photograph and a narrow roll of copper ribbon. The photograph showed her father as a young man beside a group of exhausted workers. One girl stood in front of him, filthy with soot, clutching a younger child's hand. The girl's face was her mother's face before adulthood made it guarded.

On the back, in her father's careful script: WE HAVE NOT FINISHED COUNTING.

Anja unrolled the ribbon.

Numbers had been hammered into it by hand, not in a uniform machine typeface but at different depths, some almost invisible. Beside each number was a single word. Alive. Born. Dead. Alive. Born. Alive.

The entries continued through fifty-six years of ship time.

When she reached the last line her vision blurred. The date was from the previous morning.

Alive: 3,108.

The uncounted had been sending evidence all the time.

Someone had been receiving it.
