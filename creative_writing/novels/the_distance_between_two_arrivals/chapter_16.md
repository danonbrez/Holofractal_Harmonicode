# Chapter 16: The Inheritance of Silence

The first person to suffer from the new census was an infant who had never heard of it.

The child's name was Leta. She was six months old, lived with her parents in Habitat Forty, and depended on an oxygen-enriched atmosphere during episodes of respiratory inflammation. Her mother arrived at Miro's ward carrying a reserve cylinder that had been marked for redistribution.

"They say we're over our allotment," she told him. "Tell me if she's using too much. I'll sign whatever I have to sign."

Miro took the cylinder from her, checked the regulator and connected it to the ward's emergency supply.

"She isn't using too much," he said. "She's using what she needs."

"Then why are they taking it?"

"Because the distribution system is trying to correct an error by moving the cost onto whoever it can see."

The automatic registry reconciliation had been held, but some subsidiary services had already copied the new population estimate from a provisional bulletin. The supplies office treated it as an active quota change. Local managers were receiving mutually contradictory orders: protect existing clinical reserves, and normalize reserves under the revised population.

Miro filed an emergency exception. It came back approved, then superseded by a later routine allocation. He called Distribution and found Peren at his desk amid an argument with three departments.

"Your new estimate is true," Peren told him. "The quota law isn't. Or it was written for a ship that has never existed. I can't tell which."

"Then the law has to stop changing babies' oxygen cylinders."

Peren said he would freeze the transfer list. Ten minutes later the central optimizer released a new one.

It was a machine behaving precisely according to instructions no one had remembered agreeing to compose.

***

Anja and Daret inspected Home's water heat-exchanger. Years earlier, the hidden engineers had modified it to recapture energy from the hull cooling loop, which had made their home habitable without a registered power feed. The common ship interpreted the recovered output as a spontaneous reduction in cooling demand.

"If we include this output," Anja said, "the ship's resource pool is larger than the allocation ledger admits."

"Some of it," Daret replied. "Don't forget the losses. Our plants die when the filters clog. People skip showers when the pumps are down. The books look neat because we keep the bad days in a second column."

He showed her the paper ledger. A thousand tiny adjustments had been made by households rather than any central plan. Home's survival was not an efficiency miracle. It was labor, sacrifice, stubbornness and occasional luck.

They compared the common system's reserves with the hidden production. Under full reconstruction, Habitat Forty could keep its clinical oxygen buffer without reducing Home to an unregistered supply. But the correction would require admitting that the central administration had misstated consumption for decades and releasing maintenance authority to technicians it had never certified.

"I'll sign your qualifications," Anja said.

Daret looked at her. "You'll have to sign that I can reject you."

"I know."

"Most of your people don't."

They worked for three shifts, testing valves, repairing a pump whose casing had been patched so many times no original metal remained, and balancing the oxygen recovery flows. Miro called every few hours with new medical demands. By the end of the second shift, the infant's emergency supply was secure.

It was a small victory achieved through a thoroughly unglamorous number of wrenches.

***

The next morning Tavi found a note on Home's public notice board.

YOU TAKE OUR AIR AND NOW YOU WANT OUR NAMES.

It had been transmitted from a registered neighborhood and copied by someone who wanted the council to see how the outside world spoke about them. Tavi read it twice, then took it down before the youngest children arrived for school.

Nalin asked if she intended to hide it.

"I'm deciding whether a frightened person's sentence deserves to become a permanent part of our wall," she said.

"It is evidence."

"Of what? That one person wrote a frightened sentence? Or that we should organize our government around it?"

Nalin did not answer.

Tavi carried the note to the civic office, where the council kept both private complaints and public decisions. She entered it as a hostile statement, marked the sender unverified, and attached the resource reconciliation report showing where the air had actually gone.

"You see," she told Nalin, "we can preserve a lie without helping it speak louder."

Later she visited Elsa Bener in the infirmary. The old gardener was sitting upright and telling a nurse that her tea needed more acid.

"You're worried," Elsa said.

"Everyone is worried."

"That's not a diagnosis."

Tavi sat beside her. "I think the ship might hate us."

"The ship is very large," Elsa said. "It will have several opinions."

"How do you stand being so calm?"

"I'm not calm. My heart is working badly. It gives me a useful sense of urgency. You could try something simpler."

Tavi laughed in spite of herself.

Elsa reached for her hand. "If they let you see the sea, don't come back and tell me it's beautiful. Tell me what it smells like."

Tavi promised. It was a promise she could not know whether she would keep.

***

In the afternoon the revised resource ledger was admitted as an authenticated technical finding. Peren signed, Daret signed, Anja signed, and Holt certified the derivation without claiming that the certificate would feed anybody.

Within an hour the automatic transfer lists stopped.

The relief was real. So was the knowledge that nothing would have changed if a mechanic from Home and a clerk from Distribution had not chosen to compare their separate truths.

Anja stood in the repaired exchanger room, listening to the pumps settle into a new rhythm. She had wanted to find a way to make the truth painless. Instead the truth had become a way to locate pain accurately enough to begin fixing it.

Then the public announcement system requested an emergency speech from Commander Sev.

The schedule for disclosure had arrived.
