# Chapter 22: A Door for the Dead

Sada had kept her mother's letters in a box labeled WINTER SHOES.

There had never been a winter on Meridian. The word came from Earth and survived in cupboards, old recipes and stories about frost on windows. Anja's grandmother had written the letters before the fire and never posted some of them. The rest arrived through the improvised pass-through after the sealing. Over the years Sada had handled them so often that the paper had softened at the folds.

She brought the box to Home on an ordinary working day. It was the first time she had crossed the doorway as an adult.

Tavi met them by the vestibule with the copper ledger. Nalin had offered an escort, but Sada refused the ceremony. She wanted to walk through a place whose existence she had spent most of her life thinking about without seeing.

The first room held seed trays and buckets of clean water. A young man was repairing a lamp. Somewhere someone sang a tune in a language Anja could not identify. Tavi explained it was a song whose words had been altered so many times that no one knew which language it began in.

Sada stopped beneath a narrow window.

"My mother described that window," she said.

Tavi looked at the glass. It faced a service shaft and showed nothing but a painted marker.

"She said you could see the stars through it if they turned the maintenance lights off."

"You can sometimes," Tavi replied. "Only a few. Our teachers used to make us wake at the right rotation."

Sada closed her eyes. Anja did not touch her.

They walked to the archive room where Hena Merrow's old copper spools lay on a shelf. Jori had arranged a small gathering of those whose families appeared in the first entries. There were seven people, all related by history if not by blood. No public camera had been allowed.

Tavi read the entry for Sada's mother: **MILA SOREL. MEDICAL WORKER. PRESENT AT SEAL.** A second entry recorded her death six years later. Between the two were notes about medical teaching, water shortages, a birth attended, and a dispute with the council about whether to reopen negotiations with Command.

Sada took out one letter. "She wrote this when I was fifteen."

Tavi asked if she wished to read it aloud. Sada said she did.

"My dearest Sada," she began, then stopped. The letter's private voice made the room difficult to breathe in. She tried again.

Her mother had asked her to eat properly, sleep, keep her anger and put it toward something she could repair. She apologized for not being able to come home. She did not know whether the people outside were receiving the letters. She was afraid that silence would make her daughter imagine she had been abandoned.

Sada read to the end without weeping. Then she folded the paper and stood with it in both hands.

"For fifty-six years I have told myself she was taken from me," she said. "That's true. I also told myself there was nothing I could have done after the first appeal. That is not true."

No one reassured her. Jori nodded once.

"My husband helped maintain this wall," Sada continued. "He also helped keep it closed. We told ourselves any public opening would bring command violence. Perhaps it would have. We stopped asking the people inside what risk they were prepared to bear. We acted as if fear gave us custody of their choice."

Tavi watched Anja, who was standing at the end of the table with a rigid expression.

Sada placed the letters beside Mila's entry. "I want them to be copied for the family record. I don't want them made public without the family's consent."

Nalin agreed.

***

Anja stayed after her mother returned through the passage. She walked with Tavi to the old sealed emergency wheel. It had been repainted twice, but the original scorched rim showed beneath the new coating. This was the wheel her father had helped turn when he was eighteen. A technical fix intended to last six minutes had become a fifty-six-year wound.

"Do you want to hate him?" Tavi asked.

"I do hate what he did."

"That's not what I asked."

Anja sat on the floor. The wheel hung above her, too big for one person to operate easily. She had inherited her father's notebooks, his habits, his hands' familiar motions around a tool. It hurt to imagine the young man he had been at the moment he helped close the door. It hurt differently to imagine the older man who could have opened the problem and chose silence again every year.

"I loved him," she said. "I still do. I think that makes it harder to finish the sentence."

"Maybe it doesn't need to be finished today."

Tavi unwound a little copper from her hair and wrapped it around one of the wheel's spokes. She said it was not a memorial. It was a marker so a future repair team would know there had been a decision here, not an accidental feature of the building.

Anja examined the hinge. It was sound enough for use after servicing. She made a list of repairs and safety tests. No flourish, no absolution, no attempt to reset the scene to the day before it was closed.

The wheel would open again.

The people on both sides would have to keep deciding what that meant.

***

Sada waited at Anja's kitchen that night. She had made soup, but neither woman was hungry. They sat without speaking until the kitchen timer announced that the meal was cooling.

"Was it what you thought?" Anja asked.

"No. I had imagined the rooms from every letter. They were smaller. And the people were less like ghosts than I had permitted myself to believe."

"Are you sorry you went?"

"No."

Anja looked at the old photograph of her parents on the shelf. "I don't know what to do with him."

"Neither do I," Sada said. "Perhaps we stop trying to turn him into one thing. He was frightened, and he loved us, and he helped maintain an injustice. We can keep all three facts without calling them equal excuses."

Anja took her mother's hand. It was a small movement, long delayed. She did not forgive everything in the room. She did not know how.

The soup was cold by the time they ate it.
