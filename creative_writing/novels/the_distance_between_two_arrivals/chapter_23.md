# Chapter 23: Projection Is Not Permission

The false admission attempt happened at 03:12.

It left no new canonical record, which was the first reason Holt knew something was wrong. A command-side navigation console had tried to promote an old mass estimate into the active burn plan by supplying a compatibility certificate rather than a verified predecessor and change lineage. The service refused the transition. A candidate could be displayed, compared or discarded; it could not become an authoritative state merely by arriving from a privileged-looking interface.

Holt was woken by a terse alarm and the smell of his neighbor's breakfast drifting through the vents. For a moment he resented that the universe could contain both toast and a constitutional emergency.

He called Anja.

"Someone has tried to use the obsolete map as the source of truth."

"Was the commit admitted?"

"No."

"Then the boundary worked."

"That is the good part. The bad part is someone wanted it to fail."

The transaction contained the old invalid equivalence: the order-reversed phase measurements of Seventy-Two were collapsed into one reported value, its mass assigned elsewhere, the direct capture plan certified on the obsolete estimate. The Witness returned a missing-lineage result and preserved the proposed operation as a rejected candidate with its source metadata intact.

"Can we find who submitted it?" Anja asked.

"The console is Command-owned. It identifies a role, not a person."

"Then don't infer a person. Keep the role."

Holt allowed himself a small smile. "You have learned how to make an archivist agreeable."

***

At Command, Sev received the incident with less surprise than Holt expected. Operations Chief Rin Kes stood beside him, pale and furious.

"It was a recovery profile," Rin said. "We need a burn configuration if the new route isn't approved."

"A recovery profile can be modeled without falsely attesting to an obsolete mass," Holt answered.

Rin crossed her arms. "The command adapter certified that mass for decades."

"The certificate did not include the occupied address. You cannot promote it as though the defect were unknown."

Sev asked whether the attempt had reached the engine system. Holt said no. It had been held at the admission boundary. The engine authorization remained unchanged.

"Then no material damage was done," Rin said.

"Not yet," Anja replied. "But a team attempted to turn a candidate into a command without the required witness history. That is a serious event."

Rin's face flushed. "Serious? We're six days from a maneuver that keeps this ship alive. The alternate trajectory hasn't been approved. The ordinary plan has been condemned by a woman who found a hidden compartment, and nobody will say whether delaying for eleven years is politically possible. I put a known configuration into a recovery queue because Command needs options."

"A known unsafe configuration," Dima Voss said from the doorway.

Sev looked at Rin. "Did I authorize this?"

"You authorized maintaining fallback readiness."

"I authorized modeling. Not attestation."

Rin's anger collapsed into an exhausted stare. She had spent the night preparing for failures the others preferred to discuss in hearings. She had not expected the Witness to accept her attempt as a final commit; she had expected it to permit a provisional configuration for command review. The software had refused the label she gave it.

"The interface asked for a certificate," she said. "I supplied one."

"Then we must correct the interface so no one confuses a signed form with a valid state," Holt said.

Sev removed her authority to submit navigation commits pending review. He did not expel her from propulsion. Her expertise remained necessary, and a safety system that resolved every error by banishing a worker would soon have no workers capable of telling it the truth.

Holt entered the incident into the public safety register without revealing private credentials. It was a modest success for the rule they had been trying to restore.

***

The next day an editorial accused the Witness of obstructing human sovereignty.

"Are we to be ruled by a machine's interpretation of a mathematical pedigree?" the author asked. "Can a dying captain not choose a risk merely because old software refuses the proper ceremony?"

Anja was tempted to reply angrily. She did not. The editorial contained a real question, buried beneath its performative outrage. There had to be a lawful way for a human community to choose an extraordinary risk. If a constraint service claimed final moral authority, it would be a different version of the same problem that had sealed Home.

She asked Holt what the Witness actually protected.

"It protects the identity and admissibility of state transitions under its specified conditions," he said. "It doesn't decide whether people should land, whether they have consent, or whether a commander may sacrifice herself. Those are questions whose answers require more than the machine's proof."

"Then why do people call it a judge?"

"Because it says no in a language they can hear."

"And when the public decides on a route?"

"The system may admit an authorized execution record that satisfies the engineering constraints. It cannot mint the public's authority. Nor can the public's authority make a physically unsafe engine safe."

Anja spent the afternoon helping Nyla prepare a readable version of the incident report. Every person aboard could see the input assumptions, the rejected candidate and the reason for refusal. Nothing in the report claimed the Witness could run the ship without people.

***

Tavi read it from a study room in Home. She asked Nalin whether they would have been discovered at all if the Witness had allowed the false state.

"Possibly," Nalin said. "Your friend Anja was stubborn. But a machine that accepted the wrong parent might have made her evidence appear inconsistent with the authoritative plan."

"So it saved us?"

"It preserved the possibility of being heard," he answered. "We are the ones who have to do the hearing."

Tavi looked at the narrow corridor outside the classroom. Children were carrying trays of seedlings under warm lights. A boy dropped a tray and cursed. Another boy helped gather the soil. Their lives had been held inside a false category for generations, yet no theorem had planted those seedlings for them.

She wrote a sentence in the public archive: **The refusal of an invalid commit did not decide our future. It kept someone else from deciding it through a false record.**

It was the first sentence she had ever published for an audience she could not count.

When she pressed RELEASE, she felt both frightened and free.
