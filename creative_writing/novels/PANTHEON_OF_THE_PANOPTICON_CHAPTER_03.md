# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Three: Kallisti
### The Spaces Between

The first person to notice the missing second was a woman named Edith Bell, who worked in receiving and had been counting boxes for nineteen years.

She was standing beside loading bay four when the doors opened onto the flooded staircase.

She watched a drop of black water fall from the upper door rail.

It stopped halfway down.

Edith blinked.

The drop appeared six inches lower.

Then it hit the floor.

She stared at the little black stain.

“Arthur?”

He was standing at the nearest terminal, staring past the loading dock into a place that had no business existing.

“Arthur, did you see that?”

He didn't answer.

Edith found another drop hanging from the rail.

This time she watched carefully.

It fell.

Stopped.

Moved sideways.

Stopped.

Fell again.

There was no blur between the movements. The water simply occupied one position and then another.

She held out her hand.

“Don't,” Arthur said.

She touched it anyway.

The drop struck her palm with such force that she cried out.

There was a neat circular hole through the fleshy part of her hand.

For a second, she didn't understand why she could see the yellow paint of the loading dock through her skin.

Then the blood came.

Edith screamed.

Denise grabbed a roll of packing tape and a clean rag from the maintenance cabinet. She pressed the rag against Edith's hand while trying to remember whether the first-aid procedure for puncture wounds involved elevation.

“Call an ambulance.”

Kyle had his phone out.

“I am.”

“Then why are you filming?”

“I'm not!”

He turned the screen around.

It was recording.

He hadn't opened the camera.

On the display Edith was standing beside the loading dock, holding out her uninjured hand.

The drop had not fallen yet.

Kyle looked from the screen to Edith.

Then back again.

On the phone, her finger touched the black water.

There was a sharp cracking sound.

Edith screamed a second time, perfectly synchronized with her own voice coming from the screen.

Denise dropped the tape.

“Turn that fucking thing off.”

Kyle pressed the power button.

The screen went black.

Then it displayed the golden apple.

**TO THE FAIREST**

***

In the Flooded Archive, Vespera was watching a man burn without fire.

He was one of the archivists from House Cael, a thin gentleman with an extraordinary collection of rings and the unpleasant habit of addressing servants as though they were poorly trained furniture.

His name was Osric.

An hour earlier he had seized the golden apple from the hands of a Verrain priest, declared it evidence of his house's ancestral supremacy, and attempted to carry it into the western galleries.

Now he was standing in the central aisle with his hands outstretched.

His skin was changing.

Not blistering.

Not melting.

Changing.

The first layer of his face remained where it was. Another appeared slightly to the left. Then a third, overlapping both.

Each face had a different expression.

One was shouting.

One was looking down at his hands.

One appeared to be laughing.

All three belonged to Osric.

“Help me,” he said.

The words came from different positions around his head.

Vespera backed away.

Osric took a step.

His body followed in three distinct movements, each separated by an instant of perfect stillness. The first displaced his legs. The second moved his torso. The third brought his head into alignment.

Something cracked inside his spine.

He fell to his knees.

The three faces became one.

Osric vomited a thick ribbon of blood.

Then the golden apple rolled from his fingers, struck the marble floor, and came to rest against Vespera's boot.

She looked down.

The inscription had changed.

**FOR THE ONE WHO SEES**

Malrec pushed through the crowd.

“Do not touch it!”

Vespera moved her foot.

The apple remained beside her.

Osric was trying to breathe. His rings had sunk into his fingers as if the bones beneath them had shortened.

“Bring a physician,” she said.

Malrec ignored him.

“The relic has chosen.”

“He needs help.”

“The relic has chosen *you*.”

Vespera looked at the apple.

Then at Osric.

Then at the crowd of priests who were already beginning to bow.

She was suddenly, horribly certain that somebody was going to die because of three words on a piece of metal.

And she was equally certain that no two people in the room had read the same three words.

***

At the warehouse, Arthur had found an ordinary analog clock.

It was mounted above the personnel office, a cheap plastic thing with a white face, a red second hand, and a battery someone had been meaning to replace since September.

He watched it.

Tick.

Tick.

The second hand moved.

Tick.

Then it moved backward.

Tick.

Arthur took a step closer.

The clock showed 11:16.

He looked at his phone.

11:16.

The warehouse computer.

11:16.

Everything agreed.

The second hand moved backward again.

The minute hand did not.

Arthur felt the pressure building behind his eyes.

He watched until the red hand reached the twelve.

It stopped.

He waited.

A second passed.

Then another.

Then another.

The second hand remained still.

From behind him came the low mechanical rumble of a forklift engine.

Arthur turned.

The forklift was moving toward an open loading bay.

There was nobody driving it.

Its front wheels advanced several inches.

Stopped.

Advanced again.

The machine made no sound between movements.

Arthur ran toward the emergency stop.

His body did not behave correctly.

He took one step and found himself farther along the floor than the step should have carried him. His shoulder struck a steel shelf before he had seen the shelf move into his path.

Pain exploded across his collarbone.

He stumbled.

The forklift advanced.

Stopped.

Advanced.

He could see the individual rotations of its front wheels, each arriving without the motion that should have connected it to the last.

He reached the emergency stop.

His hand struck the red button.

Nothing happened.

Then, several moments later, the button depressed beneath his palm.

The forklift stopped.

Arthur stood hunched over, panting.

The clock above the personnel office resumed ticking.

Behind him, Denise said, “We are closing the building.”

Nobody argued.

***

Vespera had once been told that the world was made of continuous substance.

Water flowed. Fire burned. Blood circulated through the veins. A falling stone passed through every intervening point between the hand that released it and the floor that received it.

Her father had believed in the doctrine of uninterrupted descent.

Everything must pass through what lies between.

The doctrine was older than the city.

It was also why the ancestral houses had built downward for centuries instead of attempting to reclaim the surface.

A daughter inherited from her mother, a house from its foundations, a king from his predecessor. Nothing emerged without passage.

Vespera had believed it.

Until she watched a candle flame separate into nine distinct tongues, each occupying a slightly different position along the wick.

The flame did not flicker.

It became nine flames.

Then three.

Then one.

The candle shortened an inch.

Hot wax splashed across her knuckles.

She cried out and dropped it.

The flame struck the black water.

Instead of going out, it continued burning beneath the surface.

Vespera backed into the stone wall.

Above her, a vast white rectangle shuddered.

She heard a noise from beyond it.

A man shouting.

Then a metallic impact.

Then silence.

The golden apple rolled out of the water and came to rest at her feet.

It had been in the custody of the high priests.

The door to their chamber was locked.

The guards were still standing outside it.

Yet here it was.

Vespera stared.

Behind the apple, the water reflected a man in a gray warehouse uniform, bent over a red button.

His face was drawn with pain.

He was breathing hard.

She recognized him.

The Chalice-Bearer.

Arthur.

Not a god suspended above the heavens this time.

A man standing somewhere else.

He looked up.

For an instant, their eyes met through the black surface.

Vespera wanted to ask him about the eastern galleries.

About the broken ceiling.

About the child who had died beneath his light.

Instead she said the first thing that came into her head.

“Are you doing this?”

The man moved his mouth.

She heard nothing.

Then his image jumped sideways.

His arm appeared in three positions at once.

One resting against the wall.

One reaching toward her.

One holding the broken handle of a white cup.

Vespera recoiled.

The water went black.

From behind her came a sound like a roomful of people inhaling simultaneously.

She turned.

Every candle in the gallery was burning in exactly the same shape.

Every flame stood motionless.

A droplet of wax hung beneath one candlestick.

Another beneath the next.

And another.

Each identical.

The people were still moving, but every movement came in small, separate increments.

A priest raised one arm.

Stopped.

His hand appeared higher.

Stopped.

His lips opened.

Stopped.

His mouth formed the next syllable without passing through the shape between.

Vespera tried to scream.

For an instant, her own throat remained silent.

Then the scream came all at once, so violently that she tasted blood.

The golden apple began to spin.

***

Arthur was outside in the warehouse parking lot when the emergency lights came on.

A police cruiser had arrived.

An ambulance was pulling up behind it.

Edith sat on the curb with her hand wrapped in a blood-soaked towel. Denise was speaking to a paramedic, explaining something that became less plausible each time she repeated it.

Kyle was showing a police officer the video on his phone.

The officer watched it once.

Then again.

He frowned.

“Is this one of those generated things?”

Kyle looked toward Arthur.

Arthur shook his head.

Nobody noticed.

The officer handed back the phone.

“You people need to be careful with this stuff. Someone's going to get hurt.”

Edith began crying.

The officer glanced at her bandaged hand.

“I mean somebody else.”

Arthur sat down on the curb.

Across the road, the traffic lights were changing.

Red.

Green.

Yellow.

Red.

There was no transition.

The cars kept moving.

He watched a sedan travel through an intersection. It appeared at three separate points in the road before reaching the far side.

Nobody braked.

Nobody honked.

The drivers behaved as though everything had happened normally.

Arthur took out his phone.

The golden apple had vanished.

In its place was a live video of a candle burning underwater.

Beneath it, a caption read:

**CONTINUITY RESTORED. THANK YOU FOR YOUR PATIENCE.**

Arthur stared at the flame.

Behind the little screen, somewhere impossibly far away, a woman was screaming.

The video's engagement counter climbed.

18,407.

18,408.

18,409.

Arthur pressed his thumb against the glass.

He did not swipe.

The flame bent toward him.

For one instant he felt heat against his fingertip.

Then the picture froze.

Not the sound.

He could still hear the woman breathing.

A new message appeared.

**ARE YOU STILL WATCHING?**

Arthur looked at the injured woman on the curb, the police officer studying Kyle's phone, the ambulance, and the cars crossing the street in perfect, impossible jumps.

His own thumb had begun to blister.

He pressed the screen again.

“Yes,” he whispered.

The candle flame moved.

And far below him, in a flooded city whose inhabitants had never seen the sun, Vespera felt a faint pressure against the other side of the glass.

Not enough to break it.

Not enough to save anyone.

But enough to know that something living was there.

She placed her palm against it.

The flame steadied.

Behind her, the gallery stopped jumping.

The water resumed its ordinary, terrible rise.
