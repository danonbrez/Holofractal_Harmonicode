# Chapter 26: The Uncounted Vote

The first vote held in Home as a recognized part of Meridian's civic life concerned a question no citizen should have been required to answer alone.

Would the ship attempt direct capture despite the structural warning, or adopt the eleven-year return trajectory and accept the cost of continued voyage?

The extraordinary assembly had defined the choices after days of argument. The direct route could not be certified as safe by the technical board. The alternate route had completed preliminary tests but required an immediate correction maneuver and strict resource commitments. The ballot therefore included an option for emergency judicial review if new evidence changed either plan's status before execution. It was not a crude choice between courage and cowardice.

Home's council debated whether to participate at all. Some members believed that taking part in the larger ballot would let the registered majority absorb their independence. Others believed refusing a voice was an extravagant way of preserving the memory of having been denied one.

Nalin called the meeting in the greenhouse. Tavi sat beside Rue, who held a poster bearing the words A REAL ADDRESS, A REAL VOTE.

An elder named Pom said, "What if the other ship votes to open our home to security inspection tomorrow? Do we obey because we joined this election?"

Ressa An, invited as an observer, answered, "No. The temporary compact preserves your council's internal jurisdiction under life-safety limits. A common ballot does not repeal those terms."

"But the common courts can interpret them," Pom said.

"Yes."

"Then they can change them."

"They can be challenged," Ressa replied. "By you, with standing. That's part of the authority you've been denied."

Nalin looked around the room. "We cannot make the larger ship perfectly trustworthy before we decide whether to help keep it alive."

There was silence. People understood the harshness of the calendar.

They voted to participate, by a wide but not unanimous margin. The dissent was preserved alongside the result. Some citizens declined individual registration. Their refusal counted neither as approval nor rejection of the maneuver. Others registered specifically to cast a ballot and then demanded written assurances that their civic status would not make them eligible for compulsory surface labor.

Tavi had never watched an electorate build itself in real time. She had imagined the first act of recognition would feel like liberation. It felt like filling out forms while neighbors argued about who would be harmed by the forms.

***

Anja spent the morning translating the engineering report into language the public could test. She held three live sessions, one in a school, one in the transit yard and one in Home's kitchen. Every question forced her to distinguish what she knew from what she hoped.

"Can the long route fail?" a child asked.

"Yes. We have a contingency plan for the risks we've identified, but no route is without risk."

"Can the direct route work if everybody holds still?" another asked.

"People moving around isn't the main problem. The structure and mass distribution matter even if everyone sleeps."

A worker wanted to know whether the fuel for the return burn might spoil during eleven years. Dima explained that the fuel was stored in stable systems but depended on maintenance, seals and cooling. A mechanic from Home questioned a particular seal life estimate and made the team revise it downward. A woman from Habitat Twelve asked if a decision to delay would give command permission to reduce their children's school program in favor of maintenance labor.

"No," Ressa said. "The life-support plan must be approved separately and include education, health and time off. Children are not spare reactor parts."

The questions were not interruptions. They were an additional set of constraints on a plan that would otherwise be called complete because the engines had an answer.

***

On election day Anja found Tavi at the transit gallery, studying two ballot papers. Home had chosen a paper-verifiable voting procedure supplemented by the shared receipt system. The choice was private; the count would be independently auditable. Tavi held one paper for herself and one to show a grandmother who had poor vision.

"What are you going to choose?" Anja asked, then regretted the question.

Tavi smiled. "You told me I could decide for myself."

"You're right."

"I haven't decided."

"Neither has half the ship."

Tavi looked at the pictures of Nacre on the wall. "I wanted to leave Home so badly that I used to think any new place would be better. Now I can go into other habitats and find out they also have bad food, stupid rules and people who won't listen."

"Disillusionment is a kind of education," Anja said.

"It's overrated."

They sat together until the polling room opened. Anja did not tell Tavi which choice would prove her loyal. She had almost done that, and caught herself.

***

The result came the following morning.

A majority of participating voters chose the conservative return trajectory. The margin was narrow enough that no one could credibly call it a unanimous mandate. Several districts had voted overwhelmingly for direct capture. Home's electorate supported the long route by a smaller margin than the public commentators had predicted. Many of its residents wanted land within their lifetime as fiercely as anyone on the registered ship.

Sev received the certification at his command desk.

"The turnout?" he asked.

Ressa gave him the figure and the audit status. The ballots had been independently checked; the record contained no unresolved counting defect of the kind that had excluded Home for decades.

He read it twice. "Then this is their decision."

"Yes," Ressa said. "Subject to the engineering gate and emergency review defined in the ballot."

He signed the acknowledgment.

***

That evening Tavi visited Elsa's greenhouse. The old woman's plants had begun to outgrow their trays. Jori asked for help moving them to a larger shelf.

"Did you vote?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Will you tell me which way?"

"No."

Jori laughed. "Good."

Tavi moved the pots, careful not to spill soil. It struck her that the first use of her new political voice had changed neither the smell of the room nor the work waiting in it. She still had to water plants, mourn people and repair the old heating panel.

But when the next shipwide order arrived, it would no longer be able to say that the people of Home had never been asked.

She opened her notebook and entered the vote certification number.

Then, after a long hesitation, she wrote a line meant for her grandmother:

**WE WERE HERE. WE CHOSE. SOME OF US DISAGREED.**
