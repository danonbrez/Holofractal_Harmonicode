# Chapter 30: The Hand on the Lever

The event that opened the manual cabinet was real.

Two nights before the planned moon correction, a coolant regulator on the lower spine failed to close after a maintenance test. The failure sent a pressure spike through a secondary thermal loop and triggered an automatic hold on several engine-preparation systems. No one was injured. The repair team isolated the regulator and began replacing its actuator.

Under the approved route, the failure was manageable. The moon correction could be delayed by several hours within the certified window. Dima filed a revised schedule with supporting calculations.

Sev read the notice at 02:40 and ordered a command briefing. He wanted to know whether the delay narrowed the route's reserves. Dima said yes, but the margin remained sufficient if the repair finished within the declared period.

"What if it doesn't?" he asked.

"Then we suspend the burn and use the next bounded correction opportunity. We do not attempt the direct capture."

"Can we preserve the direct engine configuration as an emergency option?"

"Not safely."

He dismissed the briefing. Anja watched him leave with a tension in his shoulders she had seen once in her father before a dangerous repair. It was the posture of a man who had decided that a physical action would simplify an unbearable argument.

***

Sev went to the engine room with Lieutenant Hal Rusk, the overnight duty officer. He carried the emergency command key. Rusk had been trained in manual controls, but he had never exercised them outside a simulation. He knew the coolant fault was real. He did not know the commander intended to preserve the old direct burn configuration.

Sev told him the ship might lose all maneuver options if the preparation system remained held.

"Engineer Voss has certified a delayed correction," Rusk said.

"For the long route. We also need readiness if the primary path collapses."

"Does the emergency statute authorize preparation?"

"It authorizes an immediate protective action when the central system cannot be relied upon."

"The system hasn't failed."

"It is holding our maneuver after a real coolant incident."

Rusk hesitated. Sev showed him the fault notice but not Dima's revised schedule. The document was accurate as far as it went and incomplete in the way that mattered. Rusk believed he was helping preserve optionality while the crew repaired a regulator.

The two officers unlocked the cabinet under the dual-person procedure. A camera recorded their presence. They did not erase it. Sev instructed the manual preparation sequence.

For ten seconds nothing dramatic happened. Pumps began their slow readiness cycle; coolant moved through auxiliary loops; a low hum rose beneath the floor. Sev watched the gauges and felt the relief of action.

Then the lower reaction wheel entered the resonance band Dima had warned about.

The spike did not arrive as a spectacular explosion. A narrow steel coupling in Habitat Thirty-Eight began to vibrate at a frequency it had endured safely for decades under lower load. The unaccounted mass distribution and the altered timing of the thermal circuit combined to move a stress peak into a joint already weakened by age.

The joint failed.

The blast gate closed across a transit corridor, crushing an empty cargo trolley. A nearby pressure rib tore open. Air flowed out of a service compartment and dragged loose equipment into the passage. The automatic system isolated the breach, but three nearby rooms lost power and an elderly maintenance worker was thrown against a wall.

Anja heard the alarm from Home. Her pad showed a pressure loss in Thirty-Eight, close to the new route's water distribution line.

She ran.

***

The engine room filled with alarms. Dima reached the manual cabinet and ordered the preparation stopped. Rusk stood rigid, holding a dead switch.

"Who authorized this?" she shouted.

Sev met her gaze. "I did."

"Under what failure condition?"

"The coolant hold."

"The delayed correction was still valid."

"I believed we were running out of options."

"You had a documented safe option. You chose a dangerous one because it kept the answer you preferred in reach."

He looked down at the gauges. The needles were falling toward safe state. He had wanted to buy time. Instead he had spent part of the ship's structure.

Dima ordered the cabinet sealed and demanded an independent engineering record. She did not ask permission from Sev to preserve the incident logs. He did not stop her.

***

In Habitat Thirty-Eight, smoke smelled of heated insulation rather than flame. Anja found a crew bracing a damaged pressure frame while medics carried patients through a narrow emergency passage. Miro was already there.

"One worker with head trauma, two with fractures," he said. "More may be behind the secondary hatch. Pressure's holding for now."

Anja examined the rib. The fracture was not spreading, but the adjacent load path had exceeded its reserve. A water conduit had buckled across the access tunnel. It was possible to reach the trapped maintenance crew from the registered side, but the route was slow and partly blocked.

Tavi arrived with Daret and two volunteers from Home. The new civic access agreement permitted them to help in a shipwide emergency, but an old security gate still required a registered maintenance clearance.

Anja entered her authorization. The gate opened for the rescue team. No software bypass was necessary; the lawful path existed because they had finally written one.

Daret took one look at the bent conduit. "We can reach them through Home's agricultural return loop," he said.

"Is it safe?"

"Not yet. We can make it safe if you give us the load readings."

Anja showed him the numbers. He shook his head. "You'll need to drop pressure in one of the greenhouses. We'll lose a crop."

"People are behind that wall."

"I know. I'm telling you the price so I can choose it with the others."

He called the greenhouse steward, explained the damage and received agreement. The emergency vent opened. Air rushed through the old return pipe as the water load shifted away from the hatch.

The door yielded.

Four trapped workers emerged, coughing and frightened but alive. A fifth remained pinned beneath a support beam. Anja crawled into the space with a lifting brace while Miro prepared emergency oxygen.

For a moment she could see only the man's hand reaching through dust.

She thought of the hand that had reached through the old medical cassette from Home, the first proof that an official vacuum had fingers and pain.

She took hold of it.

"We're here," she said.

The man's grip tightened.

Far away, Nacre's moon continued along its indifferent orbit. The ship's trajectory had not changed. Its fate had become harder.
