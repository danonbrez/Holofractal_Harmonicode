### Chapter Nineteen — Consensus

Elias Vane joined Consensus in 2016.

He did not call Julian.

Julian learned from a headline.

CIVIC SYSTEMS ARCHITECT TO LEAD NATIONAL CONSENSUS INITIATIVE

The word consensus annoyed him immediately.

Not because consensus was bad.

Because software projects should not name themselves after desired human outcomes.

Elena read the article over his shoulder.

"Cross-sector identity, interoperability, trusted information routing, fraud reduction, credential portability."

"That is too many nouns."

"Government, universities, banks, hospitals, media, infrastructure providers."

"Too many institutions."

She scrolled.

"Vane says the initiative will not create a universal citizen score."

"Then somebody asked."

"Probably everyone."

The project presented itself as plumbing.

Common standards.

Identity proofs.

Document provenance.

Portable credentials.

Consent.

Security.

Public records.

News verification.

Service routing.

Julian agreed with most of the problem statements.

That made him more suspicious of the architecture, not less.

The fragmented world was genuinely exhausting.

His mother's medical information existed in separate portals.

Mira's school identity did not connect to city recreation services.

A resident applying for benefits repeatedly submitted documents the city already possessed.

People changed addresses and updated five systems.

Emergency workers could not always verify credentials across jurisdictions.

Journalists struggled to validate digital material.

Fraud exploited gaps.

Vane's pitch was simple:

Reduce repeated trust work.

Julian understood.

Every building did not need to manufacture its own lock cylinder.

Every department did not need a unique definition of name.

Standards made civilization possible.

The danger was not standardization.

It was when a standard acquired decision power.

He felt the old coldness.

Visited articles.

Yes.

Friends engaged.

Unknown.

That mattered.

The Harvest scandal gave Lyra a new meaning.

Not one seducer adapting to one target.

### Chapter Twenty — Confession Wave

The first confession Julian read belonged to a woman he knew.

Not well.

A records technician from another building.

She posted at midnight.

Six paragraphs.

A supervisor from a previous job.

Years earlier.

Comments followed.

I believe you.

Me too.

Same man?

Call me.

You're brave.

Why now?

Where's proof?

Don't ask that.

Ask it.

By breakfast, three more women had named the supervisor.

By afternoon, his employer suspended him.

By evening, local news reported.

The national confession wave had already been moving for weeks.

Stories of harassment, coercion, assault, exploitation, professional retaliation.

Famous men.

Unknown men.

Institutions.

Industries.

Women told stories previously trapped in private memory.

Some men did too.

The hashtag functioned as an address into accumulated hidden state.

The scale overwhelmed ordinary procedures.

Julian read.

"Probably enormous."

People discovered patterns institutions had suppressed or failed to see.

A complaint dismissed as isolated became one of twenty.

A powerful person's behavior became auditable across victims who had never met.
