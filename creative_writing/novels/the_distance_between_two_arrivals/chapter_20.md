# Chapter 20: The Burn Equation

The engineering board met in a room that had no view of Nacre.

Sev chose it deliberately. He wanted the numbers to arrive without the planet's blue light persuading anyone that the destination had been promised. Anja brought the complete mass map. Four independent teams had reproduced her result within their declared tolerances. Home's measured water stores, metal repairs, people and plants accounted for the missing weight. A fifth survey had found an offset in the old navigation model that tracked the false routing map precisely.

The proof no longer depended on a single acoustic test.

The head of propulsion, a woman named Dima Voss, opened the hearing with a diagram of the ship's engines. The drive was old, brutally reliable and limited in the ways every physical engine was limited. Meridian could change its velocity only by expelling mass, drawing power from reserves and accepting thermal stress. No property of a proof system could supply propellant that was not aboard.

"With the corrected inertial model," Dima said, "the primary capture sequence exceeds our certified lower-spine load on three coupled intervals. We could reduce thrust and extend the burn, but we'd leave the moon-assist corridor. We could shift power to the outer engines, but the thermal margins are insufficient. These are bounded failures, not just uncomfortable probabilities."

Sev looked at Anja. "Do you concur?"

"Yes."

"Is there any uncertainty left that could restore the original plan?"

"Only if one of the independent mass measurements is fundamentally wrong. We should keep testing. But we cannot certify the old plan as safe."

Dima showed the fuel reserve. It was enough for a partial burn, enough for emergency adjustments, not enough for an arbitrary new course. She had tested candidate routes generated by the old planner. All violated at least one mandatory structural limit.

"Then," Sev said, "tell me how you intend to save these people."

Anja had been ready to present a warning. She had not been ready to become the person expected to answer it.

"I don't have the route yet," she said.

The sentence made the room cold.

A civilian delegate rose. "You have invalidated the only maneuver that would let us settle Nacre. Do you intend to strand eighty-six thousand people in transit because a spreadsheet was incomplete?"

"A ship can be killed by a complete spreadsheet with a false parent," Anja answered.

Sev held up a hand before the argument could harden into insults. "This is a technical review. We will not decide whether to tolerate unsafe loads because a member dislikes the implication."

He asked Dima to form an independent search team for alternate trajectories. Anja, Nyla and Kala would join it. Home's technicians would be offered full participation under the joint agreement.

The words were better than the plan, but for the moment they were all the room had.

***

After the hearing Sev visited the children's observatory on Deck Nine. He went alone and stood among models of the two moons. He had grown up in a family that repaired reaction wheels, where stories ended with machines returning to balance. He could not remember a year of childhood when no one spoke of arrival. His father had died after a long illness, holding a picture of Nacre beneath his blanket. He had asked Elian to see the sea for him.

Sev had said he would.

It was a private promise with no authority over other people, but he had carried it into every public decision until the distinction blurred.

A boy in an arrival uniform approached him. "Are you the commander?"

"Yes."

"Will we land?"

"I don't know yet."

"My teacher says you know everything about the ship."

"Your teacher has an excellent imagination."

The boy grinned, then grew solemn. "My grandma says if we don't land she'll die before she sees the sea."

Sev knelt so that their eyes were level. "I hope she gets to see it."

"But you can't promise?"

"No."

He watched the boy leave and felt the familiar temptation to turn uncertainty into orders. Orders gave people something to do. Sometimes that saved them. Sometimes it merely made fear look organized.

***

Dima called Anja late that night. "The engines can give us one more kind of maneuver, if the geometry permits it," she said.

"What kind?"

"Not a direct capture. A flyby with a small targeted correction and a return resonance. It would be a long way around."

"How long?"

"Years. Maybe a decade. The system has multiple moons and a star with useful orbital resonances, but I haven't found a certified path. The old planner rejects it as mission failure before calculating it fully."

"Because it assumes capture and landing are one objective."

"Exactly."

Anja went to Engineering. She saw Nyla at the interface console, shoulders hunched as she worked through archived orbital calculations that had been written before either woman's birth. They switched the mission objective from immediate landing to preservation of a living vessel with a lawful future opportunity to enter Nacre orbit.

The computation took all night. It did not produce a solution.

It produced four candidate trajectories, each with a reason for rejection and one gap where a constraint could not be evaluated because the planner lacked an address for Habitat Seventy-Two's mass distribution.

Anja understood the gap.

The old system had never needed a full map. Now the map was the problem and the possibility at once.

She called Nalin.

"I need every tank, pump and rotating mass in Home," she said.

"To move us?"

"To calculate a way not to lose you."

"Those are not identical questions."

"No," she said. "I will send the complete request."

He agreed to review it at dawn.

The ship continued toward Nacre, inexorably and at precisely the speed that left them too little time to be wise.
