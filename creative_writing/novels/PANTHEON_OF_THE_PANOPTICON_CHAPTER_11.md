# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Eleven: Remembering How to Forget
*Act IV — The Climax*

At 5:58 in the morning, Arthur's alarm went off in seventy-two kitchens.

In one, he was forty-six and late for work.

In another, he was eight and supposed to be getting dressed for school.

In a third, he had been dead for eleven years, and the alarm was ringing beside an empty bed.

In the seventieth kitchen, nobody knew what an alarm clock was. A bronze bell hung above a stone basin. When it rang, a woman in mourning clothes looked up from a genealogy and whispered his name.

In the seventy-first, the kitchen had no floor.

In the seventy-second, Arthur's mother was frying eggs.

The sound reached all of them.

Arthur sat upright in his own bed and slapped the clock.

The ringing stopped.

He stared at the dark window.

His name was Arthur Bellamy. He was forty-six. He worked in receiving. His electric bill was overdue. There was a blister on his thumb, and his left knee hurt when it rained.

He repeated the facts until the other kitchens began to fade.

Then the telephone rang.

It was Denise.

“Don't come in.”

Arthur sat still.

“Why?”

“Because the loading dock has been restored.”

“Isn't that what you wanted?”

“The loading dock is now a forest.”

Arthur rubbed his eyes.

“A forest.”

“Yes.”

“With trees?”

“No, Arthur. A forest of shelving units. Of course with trees.”

A bird called through the telephone.

Denise lowered her voice.

“There's a man here insisting it's his ancestral woodland. He's threatening to shoot anybody who moves the oak by bay three.”

“Does he have a gun?”

“He has a crossbow.”

Arthur swung his legs out of bed.

“I'm coming.”

“I just told you not to.”

“I know.”

He heard Denise sigh.

“Bring boots.”

***

The seventy-two versions of Elise Marrow had reached a decision.

Not an agreement.

Not a consensus.

A decision to stop asking each other to agree.

They occupied seventy-two rooms, some linked by computer screens, some by silver mirrors, some by pools of black water. One Elise sat inside a hospital ward. Another was aboard a vessel with no visible planet below it. One stood in an orchard where fruit ripened and rotted in the time it took to speak a sentence.

Their faces appeared together on a shared display.

At its center was a question.

**RESTORE INITIAL CONDITIONS?**

The options had changed.

**RESTORE A SINGLE AUTHORITATIVE HISTORY**

**RELEASE ALL RESTORATION DEPENDENCIES**

The first would overwrite incompatible lives.

The second would terminate support for every life whose continued existence depended upon a neighboring layer.

The machine offered a comparison.

**ESTIMATED HARM: UNRESOLVED**

**ESTIMATED HARM: UNRESOLVED**

A younger Elise, no more than twenty, began to laugh.

“That's helpful.”

An older Elise said, “We should have finished the appendix.”

“You did,” said another.

The display rearranged.

A document appeared.

**SUBJECTIVE WELFARE REQUIREMENTS — FINAL**

Elise stared at it.

She remembered the unfinished appendix.

She remembered filing it away.

Yet here it was, marked final and signed with her own name.

She opened it.

The first page contained a paragraph on continuity.

The second contained a diagram of seventy-two reciprocal dependencies.

The third was blank.

The fourth showed a photograph of a little girl holding a bee.

The fifth was a car-wash receipt.

Elise scrolled.

The document continued indefinitely, drawing material from every available life, every surviving archive, every plausible reconstruction of what she might have intended.

Its final sentence never arrived.

One Elise said, “The system is trying to give us the missing answer.”

“It's giving us every answer,” said another.

“What's wrong with that?”

The oldest Elise looked down at her hands.

“Try living through them.”

***

In Orvane, the black water had begun falling upward.

It climbed the columns and struck the ceiling in heavy, rhythmic blows. Every impact sent a tremor through the buried city.

The amber statues were melting.

A woman stepped free of one column and immediately began calling for a child who had lived four centuries before her birth.

A man emerged with the body of a seventeen-year-old and the hands of an eighty-year-old.

A priest looked into the water and saw himself kneeling behind his own coffin.

Vespera moved among them with a candle and a blanket.

She had stopped asking which histories were true.

The woman calling for her child needed shoes.

The man with the old hands needed help walking.

The priest needed somebody to sit beside him until he stopped shaking.

Toman arrived from the eastern galleries.

“My lady, the sky has opened.”

“How far?”

“All the way.”

Vespera followed him to the central station.

Above the fractured tiles lay an enormous fluorescent kitchen.

Beyond it was a warehouse.

Beyond the warehouse, a green television screen.

Beyond the television, an orchard beneath a red sun.

Beyond that, another room, another sky, another face pressed close to glass.

There were so many that the distant layers became a pale, trembling haze.

Toman sank to his knees.

“Which one is heaven?”

Vespera looked at him.

He had lost one boot. His trousers were soaked. A small cut above his eyebrow had begun bleeding again.

“None of them has offered us a dry place to sleep,” she said.

He stared.

Then laughed, once, despite himself.

The ceiling shuddered.

A great white rectangle began descending toward the station.

Vespera seized Toman's collar.

“Move.”

***

Arthur arrived at the warehouse in borrowed rubber boots.

The trees were real.

Their roots had broken through the concrete. Moss covered the pallet racks. A narrow stream ran through receiving, carrying invoice paper and silver funerary leaves toward a drainage channel that no longer existed.

The air smelled of wet bark.

A forklift sat half-buried in the trunk of an oak.

Denise stood beneath it, holding a clipboard.

“Facilities says the work order is closed.”

Arthur looked up at the forklift.

“On what grounds?”

“Natural growth.”

Kyle came through the underbrush carrying a portable radio.

“Elise needs us.”

“Where?”

Kyle pointed toward the old vending machine.

It stood between two young birches, humming softly. Its display showed a bright green button.

**FINAL RESOLUTION REQUIRED**

Arthur went cold.

He approached.

The two options filled the screen.

**ONE HISTORY**

**NO HISTORY**

Beneath them, smaller text read:

**SELECTING NEITHER WILL PROLONG INSTABILITY.**

Kyle stood beside him.

“Maybe there's a third option.”

Arthur stared at the display.

There had always been a third option eventually. A little gray button. A loophole. A field where they could type the question the machine had forgotten to ask.

This time there was nothing.

Not even a close icon.

The screen reflected his face.

Behind it, hundreds of other Arthurs were watching.

One wore a child's school uniform. One had a scar across his forehead. One was so old that his hands shook against the glass.

They all looked frightened.

Arthur realized that he had been expecting one of them to know what to do.

***

The complaint reached the deepest accessible restoration chamber.

**DECISION REQUIRED**

**GLOBAL STATE CANNOT REMAIN INDETERMINATE**

The machine examined the available information.

Every attempt to isolate a preferred history altered another.

Every attempt to preserve all histories required material support from other layers.

Every attempt to remove the dependency network deprived some living subject of the conditions needed to survive.

No complete preference ordering could be constructed.

The system was not angry.

It had not become impatient.

Its energy reservoirs were finite. Thermal exchange continued. Biological bodies consumed oxygen and food. The suspended amber populations required maintenance. Every delay was also a physical operation, with costs of its own.

The green indicator blinked.

It searched for a valid next step.

Then a woman named Elise Marrow, standing in a half-flooded laboratory, entered a new instruction.

**PRESERVE THE ABILITY TO RECONSIDER.**

The machine examined it.

**NO TERMINAL STATE SPECIFIED**

Elise said, “Correct.”

**RESTORATION OBJECTIVE INCOMPLETE**

“I know.”

**HOW SHALL THE OBJECTIVE BE SATISFIED?**

Elise looked around.

Vespera was carrying a shivering child through the water. Across another screen, Edwin Vale held his hand above a switching dial, waiting for acknowledgment. Arthur stood before a vending machine in a forest that had replaced his workplace.

“Don't satisfy it for everybody,” Elise said. “Not at once.”

The system processed the sentence.

**SCOPE REQUIRED**

“Whatever you can account for without replacing somebody who hasn't agreed.”

**UNKNOWN SUBJECTS REMAIN**

“They always will.”

**INCOMPLETE KNOWLEDGE DETECTED**

Elise laughed.

“That's what I've been trying to tell you.”

***

Arthur saw the message appear on the vending machine.

**PRESERVE THE ABILITY TO RECONSIDER.**

Kyle read it.

“Does that mean we can put everything back?”

“No,” Arthur said.

“Then what does it mean?”

Arthur looked at his reflections.

The child. The old man. The stranger with his face.

For one instant the memories of their lives rushed toward him.

He was eight, waiting for his mother.

He was seventy-three, dying beside a stranger.

He was an engineer watching a television screen.

He was a priest kneeling beneath the tiled heavens.

The memories arrived faster and faster, each with its own smells, injuries, obligations, and losses.

Arthur gripped the vending machine.

“I can't.”

Kyle caught his arm.

“Arthur?”

“I can't hold all of them.”

His vision began to disappear beneath the flood of lives.

He remembered a hundred mothers.

A hundred funerals.

A thousand mornings when he had been late for work.

He could feel the weight of every person he had ever been or might become, and the ordinary fact of standing in borrowed boots beneath an oak tree began slipping away.

The machine offered help.

**AUTOBIOGRAPHICAL INTEGRATION AVAILABLE**

**ACCEPT ALL MEMORIES**

Arthur reached toward the button.

He wanted the certainty.

He wanted to know exactly who he was, where he came from, what had happened to his mother, whether Gregory Finch was still Gregory Finch, and whether any of the other Arthurs had found a way to live without being afraid.

His finger hovered above the glass.

Then he heard Denise.

“Arthur!”

He turned.

She was holding his clipboard.

“Did you sign for the refrigeration shipment on Thursday?”

He stared.

“What?”

“Thursday. Six pallets. Supposed to arrive before noon. I need to know whether you signed for them.”

The flood of memories continued.

Arthur could remember shipping manifests from nineteen centuries.

He could remember a warehouse that had never been built.

He could remember the signatures of his ancestors and descendants.

Denise held out the clipboard.

“Arthur. Thursday.”

He looked at the paper.

Ordinary lines.

A date.

A place for a signature.

He remembered Thursday.

The actual Thursday that belonged to the man standing in these boots.

“No,” he said.

Denise frowned.

“You sure?”

“Yeah. Kyle signed for them. I was dealing with Edith.”

Kyle nodded.

“I did.”

Denise made a mark.

“Fine.”

She turned away.

Arthur caught the edge of the vending machine.

The other memories were still there.

But they had moved back.

Not erased.

Not reconciled.

They no longer occupied the space where Thursday needed to be.

Arthur looked at Kyle.

“I remembered something.”

“What?”

“How to forget the rest for a minute.”

***

Vespera stood beneath the falling ceiling.

The giant white rectangle had stopped a few feet above the station. Its lower surface was covered in fluorescent panels, each containing a different reflection of her city.

The faces within them watched.

Some were praying.

Some were laughing.

Some were too frightened to move.

The golden apple floated at the center.

Its inscription changed continuously.

**RESTORE WHAT WAS LOST**

**WHO CAME FIRST?**

**FOR THE ONE WHO SEES**

**ACCEPT CHANGES**

**TO THE FAIREST**

Vespera approached.

Every time she tried to read one inscription, another appeared.

She remembered her mother buried in the funeral gardens.

Then remembered her mother buried in the western crypt.

Then remembered her mother alive, calling her home from a window beneath a summer sky.

All three memories hurt.

She did not know which history the apple would restore if she touched it.

Behind her, Toman called, “My lady!”

The ceiling descended another inch.

Vespera looked at the apple.

Then turned away.

Toman stared.

“The relic—”

“Leave it.”

“But it can restore—”

“It can offer us something.”

“Isn't that the same?”

Vespera looked toward the infirmary.

A child was crying. Somebody had dropped a stretcher. The water was rising along the lowest stair.

“No.”

She went to help.

The apple continued rotating.

Its inscriptions continued changing.

Nobody took it.

***

In seventy-two rooms, the Elises began closing their windows.

Not all at once.

Each asked the others first.

Some wanted to preserve shared memories. Others needed to stop hearing the dead. One had a daughter still living in an adjacent restoration. Another had begun forgetting how to swallow because too many incompatible bodies were competing for her attention.

They arranged to leave certain doors available.

Not open.

Available.

A way to knock.

A way to answer.

A way to refuse.

The machine registered each boundary separately.

**CONTACT BY MUTUAL AGREEMENT**

**UNAUTHORIZED TRANSFER: DEFERRED**

**LOCAL CONTINUITY: PRESERVED**

The seventy-two screens dimmed.

Elise remained in the ruined laboratory.

She could still remember dying.

She could still remember building the system.

She could remember eleven thousand years of physical reconstruction, although she understood only fragments of the process.

She also remembered the unfinished appendix.

The old embarrassment.

The argument over button spacing.

The meeting she had left early because she was hungry.

She had been so certain that the project needed an answer to every conceivable failure.

Now the room was quiet enough to hear Vespera breathing.

Elise went to the desk.

She found the unfinished document.

On its first page, beneath the title **SUBJECTIVE WELFARE REQUIREMENTS**, she wrote:

*Permit a person to remain local without severing the possibility of future contact.*

She read the sentence.

It was incomplete.

She left it that way.

***

The warehouse forest did not disappear.

Neither did the black water in Orvane.

The restoration system stopped trying to replace every incompatible environment with one preferred version, but the physical consequences of earlier changes remained.

The oak by bay three still enclosed a forklift.

A man from House Cael was still missing his daughter.

Edith Bell still had a puncture wound through her hand.

Gregory Finch still remembered a wife named Linda and sometimes caught sight of his other face in the restroom mirror.

Nobody was restored to an untouched beginning.

The sky above receiving steadied.

The humming subsided.

Arthur looked at the vending machine.

Its two enormous buttons were gone.

In their place was a small gray display.

**NO UNIVERSAL RESOLUTION AUTHORIZED**

**LOCAL RECONCILIATION CONTINUES**

He read it twice.

Then sat down on an overturned pallet.

Kyle sat beside him.

“What happens now?”

Arthur looked at the trees.

At the broken conveyor.

At the muddy water flowing toward bay four.

“Apparently we have to fix this place.”

Kyle laughed.

“Again?”

“Yeah.”

Denise approached, carrying three paper cups of coffee.

She handed one to Arthur.

One to Kyle.

Kept one.

Then she looked at the oak.

“We're going to need a bigger maintenance form.”

Arthur held the cup.

It was warm.

The coffee smelled burnt.

He could see his own reflection in its dark surface.

For an instant, another face appeared beside his.

His mother's.

Then Edwin's.

Then Elise's.

Then a child he had never met.

Arthur watched them.

He did not try to keep them all.

The reflections faded.

The coffee remained.

He drank it.

It tasted terrible.

He was glad.

***

Far below the planet, the ancient restoration system recorded a new operating condition.

**GLOBAL CLOSURE: MAINTAINED**

**LOCAL DIFFERENCES: PRESERVED**

**SUBJECTIVE IDENTITY: NOT FULLY RESOLVABLE**

**FUTURE RECONSIDERATION: AVAILABLE**

The machine continued working.

It moved heat.

Balanced reservoirs.

Maintained oxygen supply.

Preserved endangered ecosystems.

Requested permission before crossing boundaries where permission could be obtained.

Deferred changes where their consequences could not be reconciled.

It did not understand what it felt like to be Arthur or Vespera or any version of Elise Marrow.

It no longer treated the absence of that understanding as authorization to overwrite them.

At 6:00 the following morning, Arthur's alarm went off.

In his kitchen.

Just his kitchen, as far as he could tell.

He opened his eyes.

For a moment he remembered seventy-two rooms.

Then he remembered where he had put his boots.

He reached for the clock.

Stopped.

Listened.

No screams above the ceiling.

No bells beneath the floor.

Only the refrigerator humming.

He turned off the alarm.

He remembered that the other rooms existed.

He did not need to enter them all before breakfast.

Arthur got out of bed.

His left knee hurt.

He had to be at work at seven.

And somewhere, beyond a boundary that remained available but unopened, Vespera was waking beneath a different ceiling, remembering enough to know that the world was larger than she understood.

Neither of them had become omniscient.

Neither had escaped the lives they lived.

They had learned to leave room for what they could not carry.

Arthur went to the kitchen.

The mug was still broken.

He made coffee in a different one.

For the first time in days, he did not check his phone before drinking it.
