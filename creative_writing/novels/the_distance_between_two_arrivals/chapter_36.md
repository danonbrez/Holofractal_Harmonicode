# Chapter 36: The Open Address

The door no longer said VACUUM.

It said HOME / HABITAT SEVENTY-TWO, and beneath the name was a modest notice: THIS PASSAGE IS OPEN UNDER MUTUAL PRESSURE AND CIVIC ACCESS RULES. LOCAL STEWARDS MAY CLOSE IT FOR DOCUMENTED SAFETY CONDITIONS SUBJECT TO REVIEW.

Anja stood before it on the eleventh anniversary of the first correction burn. She was waiting for her mother.

The passage had been repaired so many times that little of the original machinery remained. The wheel her father had helped turn was mounted on the wall behind a pane of glass. Beside it were two small plaques. One recorded the temporary isolation ordered during the fire. The other recorded the years during which the seal remained closed despite evidence of survivors. Both gave dates, named institutions and linked to the surviving testimony. Neither used the word mistake as though it relieved a person of having chosen.

Sada approached slowly, leaning on a cane she claimed not to need. She carried a small packet of seeds.

"Your grandmother wanted a garden near this door," she told Anja. "She said a passage should lead somewhere worth entering."

Anja offered her arm. Sada took it without protest.

They crossed into Home. The corridor smelled of bread and the sharp mineral soap the people had made for generations. A school choir was rehearsing under the grow lamps. Children from several habitats had joined it; their accents had begun to mingle in ways the older residents found sometimes pleasing and sometimes alarming.

Tavi stood beside the archive desk in a blue coat. She had been elected public keeper of the shared receipt service after promising to make its decisions comprehensible and to protect records from being used as weapons against the people they named. She had won narrowly. Some voters believed she was too severe about provenance. Some believed she was insufficiently severe about old injustices. Her colleagues called that a workable beginning.

She greeted Sada with a hug.

"Your mother would have complained about the paint," Tavi said, referring to Mila.

"She would have repainted it herself and complained that no one helped her."

Anja smiled. It was the first time in years she had heard Sada speak of her mother without the room becoming tight around the loss.

***

The memorial gathering was small and public by design. Families read names from the original fire, names from the unregistered years, and names of people who had died during the eleven-year detour. Elsa Bener's name was spoken by one of the children who now tended her greenhouse. Aro Fen attended with his mobility brace and said nothing about his own injury; the public record already contained the full account and he had chosen to reserve his voice for a talk with apprentices later that evening.

Sev came in ordinary clothes and stood near the rear. He had asked permission from the organizing council rather than assuming that his former rank entitled him to attend. Liora stood beside him. Some families greeted him; others did not. He accepted both as facts that did not need to be made symmetrical.

When the reading ended, Nalin's old friend Jori lifted the original copper ledger from its case. She was ninety-two and could no longer hold the full weight of the spool. Tavi supported the other end.

"The first record said we were alive," Jori told the gathering. "The next ones said we were born and died and quarreled and made bread. We kept them because the outside world was telling us we weren't here. Now the outside world knows. Don't let the ledger become a holy object nobody is allowed to question. It is a record we must keep checking."

She placed the spool on the table and sat down, relieved to surrender the weight.

***

The civic assembly had adopted a permanent charter during the long detour. It recognized the people of all seventy-two peripheral habitats, preserved local powers under common life-safety law, separated records custody from command mutation authority and created a procedure for independent challenges to inherited exclusions. The nine central machine cells remained critical infrastructure, not symbolic thrones. Their role in the public record was technical and accountable: keep the ship's transitions legible, preserve address identity and prevent one convenient summary from erasing a real difference.

The charter had required amendments. It would require more. Some reparations claims remained disputed. The people born after the separation disagreed with one another about what memory should demand of them. No document abolished the possibility of another injustice.

Tavi did not want it to.

A charter incapable of correction would be another sealed door with better lettering.

***

After the memorial, Anja and Tavi went to the viewing gallery above the old transit junction. Nacre filled the window. Its ocean was alive with storms, and the ship's instruments showed subtle shifts in the deep shelf that had become the focus of a generation of research. The planet still offered no unambiguous invitation to land.

A group of children were making paper boats on the floor. One had drawn the ship as seventy-two bright petals around a nine-cell center, then added waves beneath it. Another had drawn a huge shore with trees as tall as towers.

"Do you think we'll ever land?" Tavi asked.

"Perhaps in a way that doesn't harm what lives there. Perhaps not. We can live in orbit while we learn."

"Some people still call that failure."

"They can. Their loss is real. So is the world below."

Tavi watched the children. "I used to think getting an address meant finally having a place that other people couldn't take away."

"And now?"

"Now I think an address tells other people where to find you when they have a responsibility to answer. It doesn't make them answer. We still have to do that part."

Anja nodded.

A boy approached carrying a paper boat that had been folded badly. It listed to one side and had a hole in the bottom.

"Can you fix this?" he asked.

Anja examined the folds. "I can help you. Do you want it to float, or do you want to keep the shape you made?"

The boy thought very hard. "Both."

"Then we'll have to see what is possible without pretending the paper is different from what it is."

Tavi laughed. The boy looked puzzled, and Anja showed him how to make a new fold that supported the weak edge.

He put the boat in a shallow tray of water. It floated, leaning slightly. He seemed delighted.

"Where should it go?" he asked.

"Where do you want it to go?" Tavi said.

He considered the question, then pushed the boat gently toward the other side of the tray. It moved, slowed and touched the rim.

"Here," he said.

***

That evening Tavi made an entry in the shared archive. She recorded the memorial, the current population register, the latest orbital observation and the fact that the old door was open under mutual rules. The document included references to the original false certificate, the years of exclusion, the emergency-control injury and the lawful decision that had carried Meridian through eleven more years of space.

Then she added a final line for the day:

**THE RECORD IS OPEN TO CORRECTION. THE PEOPLE ARE PRESENT. THE NEXT DECISION HAS NOT BEEN TAKEN FOR THEM.**

She signed it and sent it through the ordinary admission process. The Witness verified the predecessor, the change and the receipt. It did not judge whether she had lived a good life. It did not decide how Nacre should be treated. It held the record accountable to its declared boundaries and returned a confirmation.

Tavi closed the terminal.

Outside the archive, Sada was helping the choir fold chairs. Anja stood in the doorway carrying two cups of tea. People were still arguing about the best way to grow tomatoes under a mixed spectrum of light. A child shouted that a paper boat had sunk. Someone offered a repair. Someone else insisted the child be allowed to try first.

The ship continued its orbit.

There were still names to learn and accidents to prevent. There were still people who had been hurt and people who had been unable to keep their promises. The planet moved below them, neither possession nor prophecy.

For fifty-six years the official map had shown a blank place where a community lived. For eleven more years the people of Meridian had learned how to share a map without erasing the differences that made it accurate.

The distance between two arrivals had been measured in more than light and time.

It had been measured in the work required to leave a door open.

And on the other side of that door, the people were still making bread.
