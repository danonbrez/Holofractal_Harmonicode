# Pantheon of the Panopticon

## Prologue: The Meniscus Rupture

The mug made a ticking sound.

Arthur had been ignoring it for ten minutes. The refrigerator was ticking, too, with the weary irregularity of a machine thinking about retirement, and the fluorescent tube over the sink had developed a new kind of hum that made his upper teeth itch. There were dishes in the sink. There had been dishes in the sink on Tuesday, and now it was technically Thursday, though Arthur had not slept enough to agree.

He sat at the kitchen table in his underwear and one sock, with his phone balanced against a jar of instant coffee. On the screen a man in what looked like an enormous wet beetle was trying to pull a breathing hose out of his own chest.

The caption said: **THE LAST DEFENSE OF THE PACIFIC TRENCH**.

Arthur took another sip of coffee. It was cold.

A woman in a black dress appeared next, waist-deep in water beneath a station clock. The dress belonged to an old photograph. The water belonged to a drainage ditch. Something oily kept surfacing against the pale fabric, forming rings that held their shape for too long before bursting. The woman tried to lift a body onto a tiled platform. Whatever the body was wearing had grown into its back.

“Jesus,” Arthur said, not to anyone.

He scrolled.

A gray square filled the screen. Against it, a number repeated itself in a thin black font.

**ACCOUNT 179971.179971**

**PLEASE DO NOT MOVE THE CEILING AGAIN.**

Arthur snorted. The message reappeared. Same letters, same gray, same flat insistence.

**WE HAVE CHILDREN UNDER THE CEILING.**

He pressed and held the screen to bring up the menu. Nothing happened. He tried again. His thumb had left a greasy crescent above the message.

“Garbage.”

On the bottom right, the engagement counter went from eleven to twelve. Somewhere a viewer had decided it was worth a heart.

The mug ticked.

This time it was loud enough to make him look down.

A hairline crack had opened beneath the handle. Coffee swelled through it, forming a perfectly level bead. The bead grew but did not fall. Arthur moved the cup two inches away from the unpaid electricity bill. Its dark meniscus lagged behind it.

He stared.

The bead tipped upward.

Arthur set the mug down too fast. The handle broke off in his hand. Coffee spilled across the laminate, not toward the table edge, but inward, drawing itself into a shining oval roughly the size of a saucer.

“Shit.”

He grabbed a dish towel. His fingers were already in the puddle when he saw that it had depth.

There was tile beneath the surface. White tile veined with green mold. Water rolled across it in black sheets. A station clock hung on a wall at an angle no ceiling could support. It was not a reflection of his kitchen. The clock's second hand moved, and so did its shadow.

Arthur yanked his hand back.

The towel remained in the puddle. Or some part of it did. Its corner darkened, and dark water wicked up through the cloth with a smell like diesel, iodine and something that had been dead inside a sealed pipe.

He stood. His knee hit the underside of the table. The jar of coffee toppled. Granules rattled across the wood like tiny, dry bones.

The woman in the black dress was there, beyond the oval. Arthur could see her teeth chatter. She was looking up, directly at him, as though the kitchen were a great illuminated chamber above a collapsing roof.

Her mouth opened.

What came through the puddle was not the tinny sound from his phone. It struck the air under the table and made the loose metal panel on his radiator sing.

“Hold it!”

Arthur stumbled backward, catching his heel in the chair rung. His other foot slid on the spilled coffee. The chair went over, then he went over it, shoulder-first into the drywall. Something cracked inside the plaster.

The woman put both hands against the underside of his table.

“Hold the sky!”

He tried to answer. His throat produced a dry scraping sound. His heart had begun beating so hard that each pulse blurred the fluorescent light. He had a little sour coffee at the back of his mouth. He swallowed it, and nearly vomited.

A tiny point of white light moved over the woman's face. She flinched and ducked.

Arthur looked at his phone. It had slipped against the coffee jar, tilting the screen.

The numbered message had changed.

**YOU MOVED IT.**

He could not get air into the bottom of his lungs. His hands had begun to shake. He wanted to get out of the kitchen, but the doorway was on the other side of the table, and the table now contained a woman and a flooded railway station.

The fluorescent tube flickered.

Below the coffee, the woman dropped to one knee. The black water rushed toward her collarbone. She braced herself against a wall as a piece of white ceiling settled a few inches lower. On it was the enormous dark shape of a cup handle.

She pressed her palms together, speaking urgently in words Arthur could not understand. He could still see the meaning of her terror in the stiff tendons of her neck, in the lips that had almost turned blue.

The kitchen bulb held.

On another floor of another building, in another world with a ceiling of its own, a clerk named Daniel watched a three-second clip of a half-dressed middle-aged man falling over a chair.

He was on the last hour of his shift. His knees hurt. There was a blister under the adhesive strip on his thumb, and the strip kept catching on the glass.

The clip was labeled **MAN DISCOVERS HAUNTED COFFEE**.

Daniel watched it twice. The man in the clip looked genuinely ill. Daniel gave it a heart because the fall was unexpectedly good.

Then he swiped.

A wall in the flooded station lost the pressure holding it in place.

Arthur, on the floor of his kitchen, felt the air flex against his damp sock. There was a deep impact from under the table—too heavy for any sound his phone could make—and then a crack ran across the laminate between the two halves of the mug.

He screamed. It sounded thin and embarrassing in his own kitchen.

His phone continued playing.

**NEXT VIDEO: FIVE SIGNS YOUR BREAKROOM MICROWAVE IS TRACKING YOU.**

The refrigerator clicked off.

For the first time all night, the kitchen was quiet enough for Arthur to hear the water rising.
