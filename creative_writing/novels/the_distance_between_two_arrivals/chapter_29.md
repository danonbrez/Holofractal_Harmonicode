# Chapter 29: The Emergency Mandate

Commander Sev addressed Meridian from the room where the ship's first captain had made her last broadcast.

The chamber had been preserved as a museum for most of a century. Its walls held photographs of early crews, its ceiling was patched after an old coolant leak, and an original microphone sat behind glass as though the object had once possessed a special authority. Sev took it out of the case. The device was heavier than he expected and did not improve the power of his words.

"I accept the technical evidence," he began.

The declaration surprised his aides, who had prepared paragraphs attacking the new route.

"The corrected mass model has demonstrated that the direct capture profile cannot be certified within our structural limits. The order-sensitive observations of Nacre require further study before irreversible planetary contact. The extraordinary assembly has selected an eleven-year return trajectory, and our engineers have produced a candidate that satisfies the available safety tests."

He paused to look at the camera.

"I will not misrepresent these facts. I also owe you an account of what the decision costs."

He spoke of the infirmaries, the aging agricultural workers, the people who had saved pieces of clothing for an arrival they might never witness. He described the failure risks along the long route and the labor required to maintain air and water. He did not accuse Home of stealing their future. He acknowledged that Home's existence had been wrongfully excluded and that repairing the injustice had made the ship more accurately knowable.

Then he said something that changed the mood of the entire broadcast.

"I believe our assembly has chosen the safer route. I am not certain it has chosen the most humane one."

Ressa, watching from the civic office, put down her pen.

Sev continued. "There are moments when a commander must bear a risk that an electorate cannot fully perceive. The constitution preserves emergency authority for those moments. I will honor the vote unless new evidence establishes an immediate danger that cannot be met under its plan. If such evidence arises, I will act and submit myself to judgment afterward."

He set the microphone down. He had not announced rebellion. He had created a public argument for the possibility of it.

***

Anja listened with Dima and Holt.

"He is keeping a door open," Dima said.

"An emergency door," Holt replied. "That is its purpose."

"Only if there is an emergency."

They reviewed the legal text. The commander could act without an ordinary order if a sudden failure threatened imminent loss of life and there was no time for lawful review. He could not invoke the clause merely because he believed the electorate had undervalued a foreseeable cost. The original route was a known unsafe plan, not a hidden emergency remedy.

"He knows that," Anja said.

"Knowing is not always protection," Holt answered.

They sent the command office a formal notice clarifying the bounds of emergency authority, accompanied by the engineering admission record and the assembly's certified vote. Sev acknowledged receipt.

The notice should have been enough in a lawful world. Anja had learned not to mistake that for an argument that the world was lawful.

***

Liora visited her father after the broadcast. She did not bother with greetings.

"Why did you say the vote might not be humane?"

"Because I believe it."

"Then make the case for improving the long route. We need more medical care, better housing and a plan for people who won't see Nacre again. Don't threaten to turn the engines against their decision."

"I didn't threaten."

"You described the justification in advance."

He stood by the window. Nacre's northern moon crossed the far edge of the glass, little more than a bright mark.

"Do you remember your grandfather?" he asked.

"I remember him making clocks that never worked."

"He wanted to hear waves. When he died he asked me to go down to the shore and listen for him."

"That's a beautiful request," she said.

"He never imagined the shore might be occupied."

"No. But he knew how to listen. If you could ask him now, do you think he'd want you to break the ship to keep a promise?"

Sev's eyes filled and he turned away. He had not cried at his father's funeral. The commander who had to lead the people through grief had stood among them unable to feel his own.

"I'm afraid," he said at last.

"I know."

"Not only of missing land. Of being the man who held this ship in the sky until old people died. Of making the safe decision and learning later that the danger was smaller than our fear."

Liora moved closer. "Then let the uncertainty be part of the decision. Don't make it into proof that you alone must choose."

He did not answer.

***

At the navigation board the following morning, Dima presented a routine vibration anomaly in the outer reaction wheel. It was within tolerance but required monitoring. The long-route correction could proceed if the wheel remained stable; the old direct burn would drive it beyond the tested limit.

Sev looked at the report. The anomaly was not an emergency. It was a warning about the emergency he kept imagining.

He instructed Engineering to prepare both the sanctioned correction and the manual fallback for inspection. Dima objected to bringing the fallback to active readiness. He overruled her for a safety audit but did not authorize an engine preparation pulse.

The language was careful. He was testing how far procedure could bend without breaking.

Rin Kes, removed from digital submission authority, watched from the back of the room. After the meeting she approached Dima.

"You think he'll use the cabinet," she said.

"I think he wants it to be there."

"That's not the same thing."

"It wasn't the same thing when you filed the old mass as a recovery state," Dima replied.

Rin lowered her gaze. "I know."

They stood in a corridor full of engineers hurrying to finish the route checks. Every person passing them had some small practical reason to trust that a command order would mean what it said.

Dima went to the manual controls and requested an independent security witness for the cabinet inspection. She had no authority to remove emergency equipment simply because she feared it might be abused.

She could preserve the evidence and insist that the existing rules be followed.

For the first time, that seemed to her not nearly enough.
