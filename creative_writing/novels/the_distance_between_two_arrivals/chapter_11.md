# Chapter 11: The Original Closing

Sada Sorel told the story with her hands wrapped around a cup of water.

Anja had asked for the account on three evenings before her mother agreed. On the fourth she arrived with no recorder, no notes and no demand that anything be confessed in a useful order. They sat at the same kitchen table where Anja had learned to solve simple circuit problems.

"There was a radiation alarm," Sada began. "Everyone remembers the fire, but the alarm came first. A solar storm was expected. We were told to close vents and shelter near the inner ribs. My mother worked in the Seventy-Two clinic. She stayed to help the children move."

Sada had been fourteen. She recalled the taste of the emergency breakfast, an awful sweet paste served in foil. She recalled a man from Water Maintenance promising to return with masks. She recalled her older brother carrying a girl with burned arms down the central hall. Memory had kept the small details precise while eroding the sequence between them.

The fire started in an oxygen-scrubbing substation. It crossed three ventilation trunks before the automated shutters closed. A pressure front threatened adjacent habitats. The captain ordered a six-minute isolation while crews moved survivors through the emergency passage. The local crew disagreed about whether venting would carry flame away or send it into a medical ward.

"There was an argument," Sada said. "I didn't hear the words. I remember the sound of men's boots running."

"My father was there?"

"Yes. He was eighteen and had only begun working in engineering. He helped turn the mechanical wheel. The shutter dropped. Everybody thought it would open after the pressure equalized."

The fire was suppressed in eleven minutes. The radiation alarm lasted seven hours. The local habitat, cut off from the common circulation loop, had independent life support. It had been designed that way for exactly this kind of event. The surviving crew kept its own oxygen and water until the main system could be repaired.

"Then why didn't they reopen?" Anja asked.

"Because the pressure readings were terrible. Some instruments were burned. Command didn't know what gases were inside. There were wounded people on both sides. The doctors ordered isolation and the captain signed a temporary quarantine."

That, Sada said, was the part she had always been able to forgive. People had been afraid and incomplete information could kill.

Then the quarantine acquired a new owner. Emergency authority became budget authority. The medical department wanted to move supplies into the unaffected wards. The security office wanted a single controlled breach, then no breach at all. A civic committee declared all remaining residents either evacuated or presumed dead because the alternative required a costly long-term quarantine and a second set of ration ledgers.

"My mother was inside," Sada said. "I knew she was. I heard her on the intercom on the third day. She told me to stop shouting because my throat sounded sore. Then someone disconnected the line."

"Who?"

"One of the security officers. He told me the signal was an audio loop."

"Was it?"

"No. She said a thing about our kitchen only she knew. The officer didn't care. He was doing what he'd been told."

Sada set down her cup.

"Your father helped me file an appeal. He kept writing them after we were married. At first he thought the truth would be enough. Later he learned the truth had to pass through every office that benefited from a contrary version. He came to the wall at night. He made small repairs outside the seal. The men and women inside made repairs too. Slowly, quietly, they arranged a way to pass paper and medicine and some power without making a public record."

Anja swallowed. "So you knew people were alive?"

"Some, at first. Then names. There were times we lost contact for years. We didn't know how many remained. We told ourselves that admitting the whole truth would cause the commander to evacuate the cell by force. It became a story we used to explain the silence that frightened us."

"Did you ever see your mother again?"

Sada shook her head. "No."

"Did she die inside?"

"Yes. The people inside sent me a note. She died of an infection six years after the fire. There might have been medicine in the common wards. I don't know if it would have saved her."

"Why didn't you tell me any of this when I joined Engineering?"

"Because I was ashamed. And because the lie had grown so large that truth looked like a new danger every time I approached it."

Anja wanted to be angry with her. She was angry. But she had no clear shape for the anger. Her mother had been a child when the door first closed and an adult during all the years she had allowed it to remain closed. Those were both true.

"Your father died believing you would fix the ship," Sada said.

"Did he think I would find Home?"

"He hoped you wouldn't have to. He was wrong to think that would absolve us."

***

Later Sada brought out a recording wafer hidden inside a sewing machine. It contained six minutes of distorted voices from the first day after the fire. Anja heard alarms, people coughing, somebody crying out for water. Then her father's young voice, shaking but intelligible: "Temporary isolation. We will open again when the pressure is safe."

Another voice asked, "Who has authority to decide it's safe?"

No one answered in the recording.

Anja replayed that silence until it became unbearable.

The question had never been resolved. The seal could have been opened by the people inside, but they could not certify the common system. The people outside held the key and refused to acknowledge the living residents as parties to the decision. A technical emergency had become a constitutional trap.

Sada had left the room when Anja finally switched the wafer off. On the kitchen counter lay a loaf wrapped in cloth and a note in her mother's uneven handwriting:

**I can't ask you to forgive me. I can ask you to stop me if I try to hide again.**

Anja placed the note in her pocket beside the folded slip from Tavi. She had spent years thinking the story of her parents was one of absence and reticence. Now she saw a more difficult inheritance: two people who had loved each other while allowing fear to do work that love should have forbidden.

She did not know whether to forgive them.

She did know the door would not stay closed.
