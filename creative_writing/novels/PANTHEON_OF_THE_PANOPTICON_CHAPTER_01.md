# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter One: The B.S. Filter

Arthur woke with his cheek stuck to the kitchen floor.

For several seconds he couldn't remember why he was there. His left arm had gone numb beneath him, his shoulder hurt, and something sharp was pressing against his ribs. He shifted and heard the brittle crunch of a broken coffee mug.

The fluorescent light was still on.

He stared at it.

Nothing happened.

No screaming woman. No flooded subway. No impossible black water rising through the table. Just a yellowing plastic diffuser containing three dead flies and a moth that had apparently died trying to escape the same way it came in.

Arthur closed his eyes.

“Fuck.”

He rolled onto his back, immediately regretted it, and lay staring at the ceiling while sensation returned to his arm in a swarm of needles.

The microwave said 5:47.

He had to be at work at seven.

His phone was under the table, face down. The chair lay beside him. There was a dent in the drywall where his shoulder had hit it.

That last detail bothered him.

He could explain the mug. Cheap ceramic. Thermal stress. He could explain the chair. He'd fallen asleep sitting up, probably jerked awake, knocked things over.

He could explain the hallucination.

Sleep deprivation. Three nights of four-hour sleep. Too much caffeine. Too much garbage on his phone.

It was almost reassuring how many explanations there were.

Arthur sat up.

The coffee had dried into an irregular brown stain across the laminate. A piece of paper was glued to it. He peeled the electricity bill loose and discovered that the account number had transferred backward onto the table.

He rubbed at the numbers with his thumb.

They didn't come off.

He stood, filled a glass at the sink, and drank half of it before noticing the smell.

Diesel.

Faint, but unmistakable.

He sniffed the glass. Nothing. The tap water smelled like chlorine and old pipes. He sniffed his hand.

Diesel.

Arthur put the glass down.

The ordinary explanations began rearranging themselves in his head, competing for position.

He had been dreaming.

He had spilled something.

He had a gas leak.

He was having a stroke.

His phone buzzed beneath the table.

Arthur flinched so violently that he knocked the glass into the sink.

He crouched and retrieved it.

A notification from H.A.

**Your recent activity is getting attention!**

Beneath it was a thumbnail.

A middle-aged man in underwear and one sock, sprawled on a kitchen floor.

Arthur stared at his own face.

The thumbnail had 18,406 views.

He tapped it.

A loading circle appeared, spun twice, and disappeared.

**This content is no longer available.**

He tapped again.

**This content may have been generated or altered by H.A.**

Arthur sat down very slowly.

For a long time he looked at the message.

Then he said, “Of course it fucking was.”

And because he needed that to be true more than he needed anything else that morning, he got dressed and went to work.

***

The breakroom smelled of burnt popcorn and disinfectant.

Somebody had written **PLEASE CLEAN UP AFTER YOURSELF** on a sheet of printer paper and taped it above a sink filled with mugs.

The irony had apparently survived three management changes.

Arthur stood in front of the microwave waiting for a breakfast sandwich whose packaging had promised *homestyle sausage* and whose ingredients suggested a complicated legal dispute over the definition of both words.

His supervisor, Denise, was talking to a younger employee named Kyle.

“H.A. is getting scary good,” Kyle said.

Denise was stirring powdered creamer into a cup of coffee. “It's been scary good for five years.”

“No, I mean the new stuff. It's making whole environments now. Like entire historical simulations. You can walk around in them.”

“Can it make the loading dock work?”

“I'm serious.”

“So am I.”

Kyle held out his phone.

“Look. This woman is completely generated. They even simulated hypothermia. Like, down to the capillaries.”

Arthur looked before he could stop himself.

Black dress.

White tiles.

Water up to the woman's chest.

His stomach dropped so abruptly that he thought he might lose control of his bowels.

The microwave beeped.

Kyle turned the screen toward Denise.

“See? Her lips are actually changing color. And the water physics—”

Arthur reached across him and grabbed the phone.

Kyle recoiled.

“Dude. What the hell?”

On the screen, the woman was trying to lift something out of the water. Her hands were raw. One fingernail had peeled backward, exposing a bloody crescent beneath it.

The video had no sound.

Arthur pressed the volume button.

Nothing.

He tapped the speaker icon.

**AUDIO REMOVED FOR COMMUNITY SAFETY.**

He watched her mouth moving.

He knew those movements.

*Hold it.*

*Hold the sky.*

Arthur's breakfast sandwich began revolving again inside the microwave.

He hadn't pressed anything.

“Arthur?”

Denise was watching him.

He realized he was gripping Kyle's phone hard enough to bend the cheap protective case.

“Where did you get this?”

Kyle frowned. “It's trending.”

“Where?”

“Everywhere.”

“No. Who posted it?”

“Jesus, man. I don't know. It's H.A.”

Arthur looked at the account name.

**179971.179971**

His mouth went dry.

Underneath the video, a small gray caption read:

**A dramatic reconstruction of events that may never have occurred.**

Arthur read it twice.

Then he handed the phone back.

“Sorry.”

Kyle looked at the screen, checking for damage.

“Are you okay?”

“Didn't sleep.”

Denise gave a short, humorless laugh.

“Welcome to the club.”

The microwave beeped again.

Arthur opened it.

Inside, his breakfast sandwich sat in its plastic wrapper, hot enough to blister his fingers.

Behind it, on the back wall of the microwave, was a wet handprint.

Five narrow fingers.

Black residue running downward from the fingertips.

Arthur shut the door.

Denise glanced over.

“You not eating that?”

He could hear his pulse in his ears.

“Lost my appetite.”

“Then don't leave it in there. People have to use that.”

Arthur opened the microwave again.

The handprint was gone.

He took out the sandwich, burned his thumb, dropped it onto the counter, and stood there looking at it.

Kyle was still scrolling.

“Man, the comments on this are insane.”

Denise sighed.

“What now?”

“People think she's real.”

Arthur turned toward him.

Kyle laughed.

“Like, actually real. Some guy says his aunt works in that subway station.”

“Which station?” Arthur asked.

Kyle shrugged.

“Doesn't exist. That's the point.”

He read another comment.

“Listen to this. ‘If you can see this, please don't turn off the lights. We have already lost the eastern galleries.’”

Arthur felt cold spread through his chest.

Kyle snorted.

“That's dedication. People are roleplaying in the comments now.”

He swiped.

The fluorescent lights flickered.

Arthur looked up.

For one second, between the long white fixtures and the stained ceiling panels, he thought he saw black water.

Not pouring through.

Not dripping.

Just sitting there.

An immense, still body of water held against the other side of the ceiling, with something pale moving slowly through it.

Then the lights steadied.

Denise slapped the side of the coffee machine.

“Maintenance needs to fix this place.”

Kyle put his phone away.

Arthur stood in the breakroom with his burned thumb pressed against his palm, wondering how many people had to be drowning before someone stopped calling it content.

On the noticeboard behind Denise was a new laminated memo.

**H.A. WELLNESS AND PRODUCTIVITY GUIDANCE**

*Employees experiencing persistent immersion, narrative bleed, simulated distress, or difficulty distinguishing generated content from workplace reality should contact Human Resources.*

Underneath, in smaller type:

*Please do not contact Facilities regarding perceived dimensional irregularities unless accompanied by a verifiable maintenance defect.*

Arthur read the memo.

Then he looked at the water stain spreading across the ceiling above it.

A single black drop formed at the center.

It fell.

Nobody else seemed to notice.

It struck the floor beside his shoe with the weight and sound of a distant hammer.

Arthur watched it spread between the tiles.

Deep inside the stain, impossibly far below the breakroom floor, a tiny woman in a ruined black dress was looking up.

She was holding a candle.

Arthur gripped the counter.

Behind him, Denise said, “Are you clocking in or what?”

The woman raised the candle higher.

Arthur could see her lips moving.

This time he didn't need sound.

She was saying his name.
