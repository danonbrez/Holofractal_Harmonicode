# THE GOLDEN INVARIANT

## BOOK I — THE WORLD ON TELEVISION

### Chapter One — Sirens

Julian Vance was nineteen years old when every television in Bellwether began showing the same picture.

He was under a municipal building at the time, which later seemed appropriate. The building was called the Cavanaugh Annex, though nobody named Cavanaugh had worked there in living memory, and the room Julian occupied was technically not a room but an equipment void between the basement boiler plant and an old civil-defense corridor whose yellow paint had turned the color of nicotine.

He had a flashlight in his mouth and both hands inside a ventilation controller manufactured before he was born.

The controller had stopped obeying the thermostat on the fourth floor. It believed, with the absolute confidence of obsolete machinery, that the building was forty-one degrees Fahrenheit and falling. The actual basement was warm enough to make Julian's shirt stick to his back.

He was trying to determine whether the fault lived in the sensor, the wire, or the box.

This, he would later think, was the useful thing about old machines. Their lies were rarely philosophical.

"Vance."

The voice came from the corridor.

Julian took the flashlight from his mouth. "Yeah?"

"Come upstairs."

It was Mr. Redding, the building engineer, a square man with a white mustache and a habit of moving as though somebody had once told him that walking quickly was a confession of weakness.

"I got the panel open."

"Leave it."

"If I leave it open somebody's going to—"

"Leave it."

There was something in Redding's voice Julian had never heard before.

He set the screwdriver on the metal shelf beside him and followed.

On the first floor, every office door stood open. The receptionist from Licensing was crying with one hand against her mouth. Two security guards were standing in front of a television mounted in the lobby, and the elevator mechanic, who had spent all morning swearing at a hydraulic seal, had stopped swearing.

Nobody looked at Julian when he came in.

On television, a building far away was burning.

The camera was too distant to make the fire seem hot. It looked diagrammatic: gray sky, black rectangular tower, orange wound.

A caption ran beneath it.

Nobody seemed to know whether the caption was correct.

"What happened?" Julian said.

"Plane," somebody answered.

"Accident?"

Nobody answered.

Then another aircraft crossed the frame.

For several seconds Julian's mind refused to admit what his eyes had done. The plane looked small, almost slow, like an icon being dragged by a mouse across a desktop.

It disappeared into the second tower.

The lobby made a single sound without anyone deciding to make it.

Julian remembered that sound better than the impact.

It was not a scream. It was the sound of thirty people discovering that they had been living in a room with a wall they had not known was there.

The day widened around the television.

News arrived faster than understanding. The anchors repeated the same words because there were no better ones. Smoke rose from another public complex. Flights disappeared from the sky. Rumors multiplied. A bomb at the capital. No bomb at the capital. More planes missing. No more planes missing. A school under threat. A bridge closed. A harbor evacuated. A man in a dark suit told everyone to remain calm with the grave urgency of somebody explaining that calm had become compulsory.

At noon the Annex closed.

At twelve-fifteen it reopened as an emergency coordination site because too many city departments had already sent their people home.

At one-thirty Julian was told to help Security drag steel barricades from a storage room that had not been unlocked since a protest twelve years earlier.

At two he found a box of gas masks whose filters had expired when he was in middle school.

At three he was running extension cords through the old civil-defense corridor for a room full of officials who had never met each other before that morning.

He did not go home until after midnight.

Bellwether looked wrong.

The streets were too empty, but every apartment window seemed blue with television light. The city had not become quiet. It had moved indoors.

Julian lived with his mother in a narrow brick row house in South Bellwether, three blocks from an elevated train and half a block from a corner store that sold detergent, lottery tickets, batteries, and every newspaper printed within a hundred miles.

His mother was asleep on the sofa when he entered.

The television was still on.

A commentator was explaining the meaning of the day.

Julian stood in the doorway and watched for perhaps thirty seconds.

Then he turned the television off.

The silence startled him.

His mother woke immediately.

"Put it back."

"You were sleeping."

"Put it back."

He did.

She settled under the blanket, eyes half closed.

"You hear from your brother?"

"Yeah. He's fine."

"Your aunt?"

"Fine."

"You sure?"

"I called."

She nodded.

The commentator kept talking.

Julian went upstairs and lay in bed fully dressed. The elevated train did not pass. No aircraft crossed the usual route toward the airport. The absence of ordinary machinery felt louder than machinery.

He slept badly.

At six the next morning Redding called.

"City wants everybody in."

Julian looked at the clock.

"What am I supposed to do?"

"Maintenance."

"Maintenance on what?"

"Everything."

That was how the Long Emergency began: not with soldiers at the door, not with martial music, but with maintenance.

Locks changed.

Badges changed.

Visitor policies changed.

Doors that had stood open for thirty years acquired electronic readers.

Windows received film.

Trash cans moved away from entrances.

Deliveries stopped at new checkpoints.

Loading docks received cameras.

Parking garages received gates.

Employees received laminated instructions about suspicious packages, suspicious vehicles, suspicious behavior, suspicious odors, suspicious questions.

At first every change came with a temporary notice.

TEMPORARY ACCESS CONTROL.

EMERGENCY PROCEDURE.

INTERIM SECURITY REQUIREMENT.

Julian learned to install electromagnetic locks.

He learned how to pull low-voltage cable through walls without destroying plaster.

He learned which municipal buildings had no accurate floor plans.

He learned that almost every public institution depended on at least one old woman who knew where a key was kept.

He learned that the city's idea of redundancy often meant two systems plugged into the same outlet.

The work suited him.

A lock either engaged or did not. A sensor either changed state or did not. A cable had two ends. A fuse was open or closed. The building's purpose might be bureaucratic, but the building itself remained honest.

Months passed.

The temporary notices yellowed.

Nobody removed the hardware.

A year later Julian was replacing a failed power supply for a security panel at the Annex when he noticed the original installation sheet.

TEMPORARY ACCESS SYSTEM — 180 DAYS.

He carried the paper upstairs to Redding.

"What's this?"

Redding looked at it.

"What?"

"It says temporary."

"Everything's temporary."

"It's been a year."

Redding put on his reading glasses, though the print was large enough to read from across the desk.

"City renewed it."

"Where?"

"Somewhere."

"You know where?"

Redding took off the glasses.

"Julian."

"What?"

"Door works?"

"Yeah."

"Then we did our part."

Julian folded the sheet and put it in his pocket.

That evening he stopped at a used computer shop on his way home. He had been teaching himself programming from books and copied disks, mostly because the computers at work were always doing something stupid and nobody who understood buildings understood code.

He bought a replacement hard drive he could not afford.

At home, he installed it in a beige tower case assembled from three different machines.

His mother called upstairs that dinner was ready.

"Minute."

"Now."

"Minute."

"Your minute takes forty-five minutes."

He went down.

The television was on during dinner.

It was always on now.

Not the same channel, not the same story, but always the same atmosphere: colored banners, urgent music, maps with arrows, experts in remote studios, little boxes containing faces.

His mother had learned the names of generals, foreign ministers, and television prosecutors she had never heard of before.

"You hear they're checking bags at the stadium now?" she said.

"No."

"Good."

"What's good?"

"They should've done it before."

Julian ate.

She pointed with her fork toward the television.

"People don't understand. You can't be too careful."

"Sure you can."

"What?"

"You can definitely be too careful."

His mother stared at him.

"That's exactly what people said before."

"Before what?"

She gestured at the screen.

Julian looked.

There it was: the new grammar.

Before.

After.

Safe.

Unsafe.

Necessary.

Irresponsible.

The words had not changed definitions exactly. They had changed weight.

He finished eating, carried his plate to the sink, and went upstairs.

On his computer he created a plain text file.

TEMPORARY.txt

He typed:

Cavanaugh Annex access system.

Installed under emergency order.

Original sheet says 180 days.

Still active.

He paused.

Then he added the date.

He did not know why he kept it.

He did not imagine himself an archivist.

He was a maintenance worker with a secondhand computer and a habit of not throwing out instructions until the machine was gone.

Years later, when people asked him when the whole thing started, Julian would think of the burning towers because everybody did.

But he did not believe that was when it started.

The event was too large.

Large events announced themselves.

The changes that lasted were smaller.

A door that required a badge after nobody remembered why.

A camera that stayed because the bracket was already drilled.

A temporary database that became the easiest place to find a name.

A form that gained a box no one later dared remove.

A television that remained on because turning it off felt irresponsible.

History, Julian would decide much later, did not usually enter through the front door.

It came in through maintenance.

### Chapter Two — The Crawling Line

The line appeared beneath the news during Julian's second winter at the Annex.

At first it contained weather warnings and sports scores.

Then markets.

Then elections.

Then war updates.

Then missing children, terror alerts, product recalls, celebrity arrests, school closings, infection counts, gas prices, highway delays, troop movements, stock indices, court decisions, threat levels, and the occasional sentence so truncated by motion that Julian could not determine whether something had happened or might happen.

He learned to watch the line while ignoring the person speaking above it.

The person could speculate.

The line had numbers.

Numbers looked honest.

He knew better, technically. A number could be wrong. A sensor could fail. A database could contain stale information. He had once spent half a day tracing a flood alarm only to discover a mouse had eaten the insulation off a wire.

But the line did not sound uncertain.

It crawled.

It had somewhere to be.

During lunch the maintenance crew sat in a room with one vending machine, one refrigerator, four scarred tables, and the television bolted high in a corner.

Nobody had requested the television.

It had arrived after the attacks under an emergency communications budget and never left.

"You see this?" Alvarez asked one afternoon.

Julian looked up from a sandwich.

On screen, armored vehicles crossed a desert road beneath a copper sky.

"They got the capital."

"Who?"

Alvarez laughed.

"Exactly."

Redding, reading the newspaper, said, "Turn it up."

They did.

A reporter stood on a hotel balcony wearing body armor.

Behind him the city appeared almost peaceful.

The sound cut in and out.

The crawling line continued regardless.

Julian looked around the room.

Seven men in work uniforms watched a war through a television bought to warn them if their own city was under attack.

The war was thousands of miles away.

The television was six feet away.

The distance between the two had become operationally irrelevant.

He felt the thought arrive and immediately distrusted it.

He was twenty-one. He had begun taking night classes at a community college because his mother had informed him, with the practical brutality of a woman who had spent twenty years billing insurance companies, that "being good with machines" was not a retirement plan.

The course was called Introduction to Information Systems.

The professor, Dr. Elaine Sutter, wore bright scarves and treated database normalization as a moral virtue.

"If the same fact exists in seven places," she told them, "how many facts do you have?"

A man in the front row said, "Seven."

"You have seven opportunities to lie."

She wrote on the board:

ONE SOURCE OF TRUTH

Julian copied it.

Then crossed out truth and wrote state.

ONE SOURCE OF STATE.

After class Dr. Sutter stopped him.

"Why'd you change it?"

He looked at his notebook.

"You saw that?"

"I saw you argue with my board."

"I wasn't arguing."

"You crossed out my word."

"Truth sounds bigger than a database."

She studied him.

"Good."

"Good?"

"If you thought a database could hold truth I'd tell you to change majors."

He smiled.

She pointed at his notebook.

"But state can be wrong too."

"Yeah."

"So what matters?"

Julian thought of the temporary security sheet in his desk drawer.

"Previous state."

Dr. Sutter nodded once.

"Now you're paying for the class."

The next semester she assigned a paper on large-scale information filtering.

That was how Julian first encountered Elias Vane.

Vane was not famous then.

He had written a paper with a title so dull Julian nearly skipped it: *Relevance Allocation in High-Volume Civic Information Environments*.

The argument irritated him.

Vane's basic claim was that no person could meaningfully process the number of signals generated by a large modern society. Information abundance, he wrote, did not automatically create informed citizens. Without filtering, high-volume systems favored emotionally salient noise, repetition, and adversarial actors willing to spend more effort capturing attention.

Therefore public systems would increasingly require machine-assisted relevance selection.

Julian wrote in the margin:

WHO PICKS RELEVANT?

Three pages later Vane answered.

Nobody, ideally.

The system would rank information against declared goals, contextual needs, urgency, reliability, and user preference.

Julian wrote:

THAT'S PICKING.

The next paragraph acknowledged that too.

Filtering, Vane wrote, was a governance act whether performed by editor, teacher, search engine, administrator, market, social network, or individual attention. The proper question was not whether filtering occurred but whether its criteria were legible, contestable, and bounded.

Julian stopped writing in the margin.

He read the paper again.

For his assignment he attacked it anyway.

He argued that a relevance system could become more powerful than a censorship system because people would mistake omission for absence. A censor told you something had been hidden. A relevance filter simply made the hidden thing difficult to encounter.

Dr. Sutter gave him an A-minus and wrote:

You are arguing with the strongest version. Good. Now distinguish mechanism from abuse.

Julian did not know how.

The line kept crawling.

At the Annex, another security camera appeared above the rear entrance.

Julian asked why.

"Package theft," Redding said.

"Thought these were for emergencies."

"Packages are an emergency if yours gets stolen."

The joke bothered Julian more than the camera.

Not because cameras were sinister.

Because the reason had changed without the hardware changing.

The same device moved from extraordinary threat to ordinary management without any moment at which somebody had to admit the transition.

That spring, city employees received new identification cards with magnetic stripes.

The cards initially opened buildings.

Then the cafeteria.

Then parking gates.

Then printers.

Then time clocks.

The same credential was useful because the same credential was already there.

Julian appreciated the convenience.

He also noticed that convenience had a direction.

Systems rarely became less connected on their own.

His mother thought he was becoming paranoid.

"Nobody cares when you print something."

"The printer cares."

"The printer doesn't care."

"It records it."

"That's not caring."

"What's the difference if the record changes what happens later?"

She put down the mail.

"You got a tuition bill."

"That's caring."

She laughed.

Julian worked overtime.

He completed the certificate program without deciding whether he intended to become a programmer. He liked code, but he liked machinery better. Software could claim success while nothing physical happened. A pump could not.

One night, after replacing a failed relay in the Annex, he climbed to the roof.

Bellwether spread around him in cold orange light.

Television antennas still crowded old rooftops, but more satellite dishes had appeared. Cell towers blinked red above warehouses. Fiber crews had spent the summer opening the same streets the gas company had closed the year before.

The city was growing a nervous system.

Julian could see parts of it.

Not the information, obviously. The conduits.

A helicopter crossed toward the river.

For one second he imagined the city as a creature too large to know itself, communicating through cables laid by contractors who cursed one another's work, remembering through databases nobody had designed to cooperate, seeing through cameras funded for one emergency and retained for another purpose.

It did not feel sinister.

It felt alive.

That was worse.

A sinister thing could be opposed.

A living thing had needs.

The roof door opened.

Redding stepped out.

"You smoking now?"

"No."

"Then why are you up here freezing?"

"Thinking."

"Don't do that on city time."

Julian smiled.

Redding joined him at the parapet.

For a minute they watched traffic.

"You ever think," Julian said, "that we keep installing things nobody knows how to take out?"

"All the time."

"Doesn't bother you?"

Redding shrugged.

"Buildings are layers."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know what you mean."

Redding rubbed his hands together.

"You think somebody's making a plan."

"I didn't say that."

"Good, because they're not."

"That supposed to make me feel better?"

Redding laughed quietly.

"No."

Below them, the city glowed.

Somewhere in the Annex, the television was still on.

The line crawled even when no one watched it.

### Chapter Three — Profiles

The first time Julian saw Elena Mor, she was arguing with a scanner.

Not the person operating the scanner.

The scanner itself.

"This is not preservation," she said.

A young contractor in a polo shirt stared at her.

The machine pulled another sheet of brittle paper under a glass plate.

"Ma'am, it is literally an archival scanner."

"No. It is a scanner being used in an archive."

Julian, who had come to repair a dehumidifier, stopped in the doorway.

The municipal records annex occupied what had once been a bank. The basement vault held maps, property records, old engineering drawings, court ledgers, procurement documents, payroll books, and several decades of paper nobody wanted until the day someone desperately needed it.

The air smelled like cardboard and stone.

The contractor lifted both hands.

"We're creating digital copies."

"Of the current document."

"Yes."

"What happens to the previous copy?"

He looked at the page.

"This is the previous copy."

"No."

Elena leaned across the table.

"This page is revision four. Revisions one through three were removed from the binder before your company received it."

"That's not our issue."

"It becomes your issue when your project is called Historical Preservation."

The contractor looked at Julian as if maintenance might have authority over philosophy.

Julian pointed toward the back room.

"Dehumidifier."

The contractor nodded as though Julian had wisely declined combat.

Elena watched him pass.

She was perhaps twenty-seven, dark-haired, thin, wearing a gray cardigan with an elbow repaired by hand. A pencil held her hair in a knot that seemed temporary but structurally sound.

The dehumidifier had failed because a drain line was clogged with mineral scale.

Julian cleared it in twelve minutes.

When he came back through the scanning room, Elena was alone.

"Win?"

"No."

"Machine apologize?"

She looked at him.

Then smiled.

"It has not yet acquired sufficient self-awareness."

"Give the city time."

He signed the work order at the desk.

She glanced at his handwriting.

"Julian Vance."

"That's me."

"Any relation?"

"To who?"

"Elias Vane."

"Different spelling."

"Yes."

"Also, no."

She looked mildly disappointed.

"You know who he is?"

"Read a paper."

"Which?"

"Relevance allocation."

"And?"

"I wanted to punch it."

"Good paper."

"Still wanted to punch it."

Elena nodded.

"That's usually a good sign."

He handed her the pink copy of the work order.

She inspected it.

"You keep the yellow?"

"Office does."

"And when they enter it?"

"Computer."

"What happens to the yellow?"

"Box."

"And the box?"

"Storage."

"And eventually?"

Julian looked at her.

"You really do this all day."

"Somebody has to."

He leaned on the desk.

"Why do you care about old versions if the current one is right?"

Elena took a moment.

"Right about what?"

"Whatever it's for."

"Suppose a bridge was built from drawing three."

"Okay."

"Drawing seven is current."

"Okay."

"The bridge fails."

"Okay."

"Do you investigate drawing seven?"

Julian considered.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because that's not what they built."

Elena tapped the pink work order against the desk.

"Exactly."

That was the first thing she ever taught him.

They got coffee three days later.

Bellwether in those years had begun filling with cafés where customers worked on laptops for hours while purchasing one drink. Julian found this behavior economically mysterious. Elena explained that the customer was not buying coffee.

"They're buying temporary jurisdiction."

"Over the chair?"

"Over the fact nobody asks why they're sitting there."

"You could sit in a library."

"Library closes."

"So does this."

"Later."

At the next table, two college students were taking photographs of themselves with a digital camera.

They reviewed each image immediately, deleting most of them.

"What are they doing?" Julian asked.

"Producing evidence."

"Of coffee?"

"Of being people who drink coffee."

He looked at her.

"You're weird."

"I work in archives."

"That's not a defense."

A student nearby was explaining a new website where everybody received a page with a photograph, interests, friends, school information, favorite music, and public messages.

"Why?" Julian said.

Elena listened.

"Searchable identity."

"For who?"

"Everyone, apparently."

"Sounds terrible."

"You have an email address."

"That's not my identity."

"It will be if people decide it is."

He shook his head.

"No one wants a public file on themselves."

Elena's expression changed into the look he would later recognize as her archivist's pleasure at encountering a statement time could destroy.

"Keep talking."

Within two years, Julian had a profile.

He did not create it himself.

A coworker named Benson created one for him during lunch because a woman from Payroll had asked whether Julian was "on there."

"What'd you put?" Julian asked.

"Basic stuff."

"Delete it."

"You haven't seen it."

"I don't need to see it."

Benson turned the monitor.

There was Julian's name.

A photograph from a work picnic.

Relationship status.

Employer.

Hometown.

Favorite bands, most of them wrong.

"Why am I twenty-six?"

"Better range."

"I'm twenty-three."

"You're welcome."

Julian reached for the mouse.

Benson slapped his hand away.

"Relax. Nobody believes profiles."

"Then why have them?"

"Because girls do."

This logic apparently defeated all objection.

Julian corrected his age.

He removed his employer.

He replaced the photograph.

He deleted the favorite bands.

Then he spent forty minutes looking up people he had not seen since high school.

That night he logged in again.

The interesting thing was not the profile.

The interesting thing was the graph.

People Julian had known in separate parts of his life now appeared adjacent because the software had discovered their common connection to him. Former classmates, cousins, city workers, an old boxing coach, a woman he had dated for three weeks, people from community college, people he had forgotten he knew.

Human memory had boundaries.

The graph did not respect them.

Elena refused to join.

"You have a driver's license," Julian told her.

"The Commonwealth requires that."

"Your job has a directory."

"My job requires that."

"You buy things with a card."

She stared at him.

"Are you trying to persuade me by listing existing surveillance?"

"I'm saying you're already in systems."

"That doesn't create an obligation to volunteer more."

"You're missing people."

"People know my phone number."

"Some don't."

"Then perhaps we have accurately modeled our relationship."

He laughed.

She did not.

Months later the profile site introduced a feature called the Stream.

Previously, if Julian wanted to know what someone had done, he visited that person's page.

Now the site told him automatically.

Benson changed relationship status.

A cousin joined a group.

A woman from Purchasing added photographs.

Someone Julian barely remembered wrote something angry at two in the morning.

The system had transformed human life from a collection of pages into weather.

People hated it.

For about a week.

There were petitions.

Groups formed to protest the Stream using the Stream.

Users complained that ordinary actions had become unnaturally visible.

The company added controls.

Then everyone kept using it.

Julian found the whole episode funny until he realized his own behavior had changed.

He no longer looked people up.

He waited for them to arrive.

This bothered him enough that he called Elena.

"I think you were right."

"About what?"

"The Stream."

"I don't know what that is."

"The feed thing."

"I remain blissfully unqualified."

"It's like—before, you had to go somewhere to know something. Now it tells you."

"Like television."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Television doesn't know who I know."

There was a pause.

"That," Elena said, "is a better distinction."

The municipal network changed in parallel.

An employee portal that had begun as a place to download forms began presenting personalized notices. Required training. Benefits deadlines. Building alerts. Suggested documents. Messages based on department.

Julian appreciated it.

That was important.

He would later become suspicious of people who told the story of technology as if everyone had been dragged unwillingly into obvious traps.

They had not.

Useful things were useful.

The portal saved him time.

The profile site reconnected him with a friend from childhood.

The Stream told him his cousin had a baby.

Navigation software kept him from getting lost in New Jersey.

Online banking prevented overdraft fees twice.

The network solved problems.

That was why it spread.

One Saturday Elena invited him to the records annex after hours.

"That sounds illegal."

"It is not illegal."

"Why after hours?"

"Because during hours people ask why I'm doing my job."

She had laid six versions of the same city procurement policy across a table.

The newest version existed digitally.

The older versions existed only on paper.

"What am I looking at?"

"Paragraph twelve."

Julian read.

In the first version, emergency purchasing authority expired after ninety days.

In the second, one hundred eighty.

In the third, it could be renewed by department heads.

In the fourth, renewals required only administrative notice.

In the fifth, the word emergency had disappeared from the heading.

The sixth called the same authority expedited procurement.

Julian read them again.

"So?"

"So when did the emergency power become ordinary power?"

He pointed to the fifth.

"No."

"Fourth?"

"No."

"Then when?"

"That's the problem."

"There had to be a vote."

"There were six."

"Then that's when."

Elena shook her head.

"Each vote describes itself as a limited procedural modification to the previous state. Nobody appears to have voted on the final state."

Julian sat down.

On the far side of the room a scanner slept with one green light.

"Maybe that's normal."

"It is normal."

"Then why are you showing me?"

She gathered the pages.

"Because normal is not the same as harmless."

He watched her return each version to its sleeve.

"You're making this sound like somebody planned it."

"I didn't say anyone planned it."

"Then what are you saying?"

Elena looked at him.

"Systems can arrive at states nobody authorized globally because everybody authorized something locally."

Julian thought of Vane's paper.

He thought of the camera above the rear entrance.

He thought of the Stream.

He thought of himself correcting his age, selecting a better photograph, adding people, accepting the usefulness of having them delivered to him.

"That sounds like an excuse."

"For who?"

"Everybody."

"Maybe."

She slid the binder back into its box.

"Or a warning."

On his way home, Julian stopped at a red light.

His phone buzzed.

He looked down.

The Stream had informed him that Elena Mor had joined the network.

For several seconds he stared at the notification.

Then he called her.

"You joined."

"No."

"It says you joined."

"I didn't."

"I'm looking at your name."

"Is there a photograph?"

"No."

"Friends?"

"Three."

"Who?"

He read the names.

Elena was silent.

"You know them?"

"Two."

"So maybe you—"

"I did not join."

Julian glanced at the light.

A horn sounded behind him. It had turned green.

He drove through the intersection.

"When I get home," Elena said, "I'll look."

"Probably somebody made it for you. Benson made mine."

"Who is Benson?"

"Exactly."

"You think that's funny."

"A little."

"It has my name."

"Names aren't unique."

"Julian."

"What?"

"One of those three people died six years ago."

The line went quiet.

He thought he had misheard her.

"What?"

"The third name."

"You said you know two."

"I knew three."

Traffic moved around him.

The phone remained warm against his ear.

"Maybe it's an old account."

"The site didn't exist."

Julian almost said *that's impossible*, but the sentence seemed useless.

Elena spoke first.

"Don't touch anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"Screenshot it."

"Why?"

"Because if we're wrong, it doesn't matter."

"And if we're right?"

"Then I want the previous state."

Julian pulled over.

When he reopened the profile, Elena Mor had no friends.

He took the screenshot anyway.
