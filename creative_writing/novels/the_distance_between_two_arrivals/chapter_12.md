# Chapter 12: Terms of Witness

The agreement was signed in a room without any official flag.

Nalin had insisted on the location. The meeting took place in a maintenance classroom where neither side owned the walls, and where the chalkboard still carried a lesson on coolant pressure from the previous week. The table was borrowed from the ship's agricultural union. Two people from Home and two from the registered habitats inspected it for hidden equipment before the talks began, an absurd procedure that made everyone feel a little better.

Anja sat between Holt and Tavi. Across from them Commander Sev had brought Peren from Distribution, Miro from Medicine and a civilian legal adviser named Ressa An.

The offer was narrow. Home would release raw mass and resource data, engineering diagrams, and the nonprivate parts of its lineage records to a jointly witnessed technical team. The registered ship would recognize Home's residents as persons for urgent medical care, food safety, and emergency transit while an independent body assessed full civic participation. Neither side would receive unilateral control over the other's internal systems.

"You're making our existence provisional," Tavi said.

Ressa shook her head. "Your right to emergency care is unconditional. It's the mechanism of civic admission we're drafting."

"Then say that plainly."

Ressa changed the wording.

Sev wanted exclusive custody of the shared logs until the navigation board confirmed their integrity. Holt refused. "That would recreate the failure we're investigating," he said. "One institution would both hold the evidence and define what counts as evidence."

"We need to avoid panic," Sev said.

"Then tell the ship what is known, what is unknown and what action is authorized," Holt replied. "Don't replace the problem with a performance of certainty."

Nalin proposed parallel evidence stores. The first would remain inside Home. The second would be held by Records in a protected civic archive. The third would be a cryptographically witnessed summary available to anyone aboard, including Command. Private medical and household records would remain private unless their owners consented or a narrowly defined emergency required access.

The arrangement took two hours to draft and another hour to read back. Anja found herself moved by the way the adults took care with verbs. Provide. Retain. Decline. Challenge. Suspend. They were trying to build a door out of words before making another one out of steel.

Then Ressa reached the clause on dissemination.

"All jointly confirmed technical findings will be published in full within twenty-four hours of admission," she read.

Sev stiffened. "That is too fast."

"It is fast enough to matter," Tavi said.

"We may need to change the capture plan. Dissemination before the board's review could provoke a reaction that makes the ship less safe."

"Sev," Anja said, "the missing mass changes the plan whether people hear about it or not."

"People react to information," he replied. "Reactions have mass, pressure and consequences too."

Anja could not dispute that. She offered a safety annotation: publish raw findings with explicit candidate status, delay any operational instruction until the appropriate admission authority acted, and forbid the use of public evidence as a command to open pressure boundaries. Sev agreed to that. He did not agree to let the population see the navigational consequences before his board.

Tavi placed her hand on the paper. "Then we have no agreement."

The room went quiet.

Sev looked at her, not as a child who could be told to behave but as an adversary who had found a decisive pressure point.

"You are asking me to risk the maneuver," he said.

"I'm asking you not to hide the risk from the people who will bear it."

"There isn't time to teach eighty thousand people orbital mechanics."

"They don't all have to agree on the equation," Tavi said. "They have to know someone is choosing on their behalf."

Ressa proposed a compromise: publish the discrepancy, the methods, the independent reviewer names and the range of operational consequences immediately after the technical team confirmed them. The final maneuver options would go to the navigation board and then a special civic assembly within forty-eight hours. Emergency action remained available but required a contemporaneous written justification and independent retrospective review.

Sev looked toward the window. Nacre's crescent had become a broad white arc.

"Forty-eight hours," he said. "And no unauthorized publicity before the certification."

Nalin asked Tavi if the condition was acceptable. She disliked how close it was to the commander's original demand. Still, the timetable was now written and jointly enforceable.

"Yes," she said. "With the amendment that our council receives the same report at the same time."

Ressa wrote it.

***

When the agreement was complete, Holt placed three copies on the table and began the careful process of making each one refer to the same original text. He explained that the compact receipt did not itself establish whether the treaty was fair. It established which people had signed which exact terms and in what sequence. The distinction mattered: an authentic injustice was still an injustice.

Miro gave Tavi a package of medical supplies with her council's permission. She took it without ceremony.

"Thank you," she said. "Elsa will be pleased. She already thinks your first visit was too full of doctors."

"That's a kind of good news I rarely receive," he answered.

Anja watched Sev sign last. His pen hovered over the line, then descended.

She had expected triumph. Instead she felt the gravity of the next problem gathering in the room. They had constructed a means to discover more truth, and truth on a ship preparing for a capture burn was not a passive object. It would change schedules, supplies and people who had imagined those things belonged to them.

Tavi walked beside her to the transit junction.

"Do you trust him?" she asked.

"Sev? I trust him to believe he's saving us."

"That's not what I meant."

"No. I don't trust his judgment to include people he's never had to count."

"Do you trust yours?"

Anja thought about the first acoustic report she had almost let a convenience filter erase.

"Not without witnesses," she said.

Tavi nodded. "That's probably the best answer anybody could give."

Before they parted, the two women stood in the doorway of the shuttle and watched the ship's service lights recede down the spine. Somewhere ahead, beyond steel and synthetic air, lay Nacre's ocean, making waves beneath a foreign moon. Neither woman knew what those waves meant.

For the first time, Anja had agreed in writing not to decide alone.

The next morning Kala sent her a new signal trace.

It had the same ordered signature as the wall in Seventy-Two.

It came from the planet.
