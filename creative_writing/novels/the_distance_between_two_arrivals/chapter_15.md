# Chapter 15: The Consent Record

The landing ballot had been decided before Anja was born.

She learned this in the civic archive, where Holt had summoned a small group of legal and technical witnesses to reconstruct the authority behind Meridian's settlement mandate. The ship's constitution required periodic confirmation that the destination remained lawful and that the colonists still consented to settlement. The current plan had been ratified twenty-eight years earlier by a large majority.

The ballot was authentic. Holt proved that within an hour.

He could reconstruct the voter registry, the authorized counting method, the announcement time and the final totals. The signatures followed from a valid civic process. No one had altered the result after certification.

"Then what's wrong with it?" asked Ressa An, the legal adviser.

Holt projected the registry. "It descends from the population definition established after the sealing of Cell Seventy-Two."

"Those residents were excluded because their status was unknown."

"The status was unknown because the institutions responsible for resolving it refused to admit the evidence."

Ressa frowned. "That doesn't automatically invalidate every act performed afterward."

"No," Holt said. "It creates a claim the court must evaluate. We should not confuse a defect in representation with a proven result in a hypothetical election."

Anja appreciated his care. It would have been easy to declare the mandate void and claim that the truth had solved their ethical dilemma. But the registered majority might still have chosen settlement if Home had voted. Some of Home's residents might have voted in favor too. No one knew.

They could prove the vote had excluded people who existed. They could not prove what those people would have chosen.

Ressa proposed an extraordinary election limited to the landing authority, but Sev objected. There were twelve days before the first deceleration burn; many citizens worked shifts and some lacked reliable access to civic networks. An election run in panic risked losing legitimacy faster than it gained it.

"We must act with the authority we have," he said.

"Authority can be valid and incomplete," Holt replied. "An emergency mandate does not become absolute merely because its schedule is inconvenient."

Anja watched the two men and wondered how many catastrophes began as arguments between people who could both point to a correct clause.

***

Tavi arrived for the hearing carrying a folded sheet of names.

"Are you here to challenge the election?" asked Ressa.

"No," Tavi said. "I'm here to challenge the assumption that our people have no position unless we vote in yours."

She read a resolution passed by Home's council the previous evening. It recognized the importance of safe orbital arrival, demanded legal personhood and a vote on any irreversible landing decision, and declined to endorse surface colonization until the planetary ecological uncertainty had been reviewed. The resolution did not authorize commandeering the registered ship's engines or exposing other citizens to risk. It asked to share authority over decisions that would bind everyone.

Sev listened with visible impatience.

"Would you prefer we miss Nacre altogether?" he asked.

"Your resolution doesn't contain a question about missing Nacre," Tavi answered. "It contains one about landing."

"Without capture we cannot stay."

"Then capture is the problem we need to solve. Why is the landing decision being treated as its parent?"

Anja saw Holt glance at her. The question was technically precise. The vessel's navigation plan treated capture, atmospheric resource harvest and eventual landing as one integrated mission sequence. Pulling apart those decisions would reveal options that the planning committee had never needed to represent.

Sev said, "Because the fuel budget links them."

"Linked is not the same as identical," Tavi replied.

For the first time the commander looked at her without imagining she would be quiet if someone explained urgency one more time.

Ressa entered Home's resolution into the hearing record. She also entered an amendment clarifying that the original landing mandate remained operative pending review but did not authorize irreversible ecosystem alteration if the scientific classification changed.

There was no glorious revelation. The room had produced a limited legal distinction and several new tasks. Anja felt relief anyway.

***

Later Ressa asked Anja to walk with her through the old transit gallery.

"Do you understand what you've done?" the lawyer asked.

"Found people?"

"Made every inherited authority answer for its foundation. That's appropriate in principle. It's also going to terrify people who depend on those foundations."

"The foundations were wrong."

"Some were incomplete. Some were unjust. Some were the only laws that kept the ship functioning for a hundred years. If we tear them down without replacements, we'll hurt the people whose protection gives law its meaning."

Anja was tired of being reminded of danger by people who had better access to power than she did. She nevertheless knew Ressa was right about one part: the ship needed decisions before it could perfect its history.

"Then write lawful replacements," she said.

"With whose consent?"

"Start with the people who are actually here."

The gallery opened onto a dome full of arrival preparations. People had begun making clothes to wear on Nacre, soft fabrics that would not chafe beneath first-landing suits. A child ran past carrying a toy boat made of folded metal. The boat had no water to float on and no guarantee it would ever touch a sea.

Ressa watched the child go. "I'm not against your people."

"They aren't my people," Anja said. Then she corrected herself. "Or rather, they are, but not because I found them."

Anja returned to the engineering deck and requested an independent separation of the capture and landing constraints. The navigation system answered that the request would require a full recomputation of the mission profile.

She authorized a read-only shadow calculation.

It was the first time in her career she had asked the ship to consider arriving without assuming it already owned the shore.
