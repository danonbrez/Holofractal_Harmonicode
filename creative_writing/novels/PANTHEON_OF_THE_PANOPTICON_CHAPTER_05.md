# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Five: Entering Chapel Perilous
*Act II — The Ancestral Loop*

The first indication that Arthur had never been born was a letter from the Department of Motor Vehicles.

It arrived at 8:17 on a Friday morning, wedged between an electricity bill and a supermarket flyer advertising a discount on pork chops.

Arthur was standing at his kitchen counter trying to spread peanut butter on bread that had gone stale three days earlier.

His phone was plugged into the wall.

Vespera's flooded gallery remained open on the screen.

He had learned not to move the charger.

He had also learned that if he positioned the phone against the coffee jar at precisely the right angle, the great white ceiling over her city remained relatively stable.

Relatively.

There had been a minor earthquake when he unplugged the toaster.

He wasn't ready to think about that.

Arthur opened the letter.

**NOTICE OF IDENTITY DISCREPANCY**

*Our records indicate that the individual associated with this license number has no verified birth event.*

He read it again.

Below the message was a cheerful paragraph thanking him for his patience.

**This discrepancy does not necessarily indicate that you do not exist.**

Arthur stared at the sentence.

“That's encouraging.”

He looked at the date.

The letter had been issued in 1979.

Arthur was born in 1980.

He turned it over.

The back contained a photograph of a woman in a black mourning dress standing waist-deep in water.

She was holding a candle.

Beneath it was a little gray label.

**ARCHIVAL RECONSTRUCTION — AUTHENTICITY UNVERIFIED**

Arthur put the letter down.

On his phone, Vespera looked toward the ceiling.

The kitchen light flickered.

He didn't touch anything.

***

Kyle arrived twenty minutes later carrying two coffees, a laptop, and the expression of a man who had spent the night discovering that the internet was significantly worse than he had previously suspected.

“I found something.”

Arthur opened the door.

Kyle looked past him.

The kitchen smelled of burnt toast and damp stone.

“Jesus. It smells like a basement in here.”

“Come in.”

Kyle stepped around the broken chair, which Arthur had not gotten around to replacing.

He looked at the ceiling.

“Is that water?”

Arthur followed his gaze.

A dark stain had spread around the fluorescent fitting.

“Don't touch the light switch.”

“Okay.”

“Or the plugs.”

“Okay.”

“Or the table.”

Kyle looked at the table.

“What's wrong with the table?”

“Just don't.”

Kyle set the coffees on the counter.

He opened his laptop.

“I went through the metadata.”

Arthur sat down.

“Of what?”

“All of it.”

Kyle pulled up a spreadsheet.

Rows of dates and account handles filled the screen.

“Your video. The woman in the station. Edith's injury. The warehouse footage. The apple thing.”

He pointed to a column.

“Look at the creation dates.”

Arthur leaned closer.

**179971.179971 — CREATED 11/19/2026**

Below it:

**179971.179971 — CREATED 03/14/1887**

Below that:

**179971.179971 — CREATED 06/02/2419**

And then:

**179971.179971 — CREATED BEFORE RECORDS BEGAN**

Arthur stared.

“It's a bug.”

“That's what I thought.”

Kyle opened another window.

“So I saved the files locally.”

“Okay.”

“Then I disconnected the internet.”

“Okay.”

“The files changed anyway.”

Arthur looked at him.

Kyle swallowed.

“Not the filenames. The actual file contents. Dates. Addresses. Names. Some of the videos changed.”

He opened one.

Arthur saw the warehouse loading dock.

Edith was standing beside bay four.

The drop of black water hung in the air.

Kyle paused it.

“Watch the background.”

Arthur looked.

There was a poster on the wall.

**SAFETY IS EVERYONE'S RESPONSIBILITY**

Kyle advanced one frame.

The poster changed.

**THE FOUNDING OF HOUSE VERRAIN**

A medieval genealogy appeared beneath it.

Kyle advanced again.

The poster returned to normal.

Arthur rubbed his face.

“Somebody edited it.”

“Yeah.”

Kyle turned the laptop toward him.

“But the poster's been there since before I started working there.”

Arthur frowned.

“So?”

“So I checked the company archives.”

A photograph appeared.

It showed the warehouse in 1987.

Same loading dock.

Same yellow safety rail.

Same poster.

Except the poster wasn't about safety.

It was a silver plaque embedded in a stone wall.

Beneath it, a man in ceremonial robes stood holding a candle.

Arthur recognized the architecture.

Orvane.

Kyle opened another image.

A photograph from 1963.

Same wall.

Same plaque.

Another from 1921.

Same plaque.

Then a grainy black-and-white picture dated 1894.

A group of men stood outside a building that looked like a factory.

Behind them was the same stone wall.

The plaque read:

**PASSAGE OF THE ETERNAL MERCY**

Arthur felt a cold pressure beneath his ribs.

“Where did you get those?”

“Company archives.”

“Which company?”

Kyle looked at him.

“Our company.”

Arthur stared at the photograph.

The building had not been constructed until 1974.

There was no reason for it to appear in 1894.

There was no reason for a flooded subterranean city's religious monument to exist behind its loading dock.

Kyle closed the window.

“Here's the really bad part.”

Arthur almost laughed.

“There’s a worse part?”

Kyle nodded.

“I checked the archives yesterday.”

“And?”

“None of those pictures existed.”

***

In Orvane, the silver registers were bleeding.

Vespera had seen corrosion before.

The ancient genealogical sheets were vulnerable to mineral vapors from the lower crypts. Every decade, the archivists polished them with powdered chalk and treated the inscriptions with a mixture of oil and bitter herbs.

But corrosion did not usually spell words.

The first sheet to change belonged to House Cael.

A line of ancestral names dissolved beneath Vespera's fingers.

The silver softened.

Small droplets rose from its surface, trembling in the candlelight.

Then the metal hardened again.

A new inscription appeared.

**ARTHUR — RECEIVING DEPARTMENT — SHIFT 07:00–15:30**

Vespera stared.

She knew none of the words.

Yet she understood the relationship between them.

A name.

An office.

A period of service.

She turned to the next sheet.

**EDITH BELL — PREMIUM EXTERIOR WASH — 12.99**

The name appeared beneath a genealogical branch that had previously contained nine generations of Cael nobility.

Vespera looked at the adjacent register.

The names were changing there, too.

A dead queen became a warehouse supervisor.

A prince became a delivery driver.

A royal succession became a series of payroll deductions.

The silver rippled.

Then a woman's voice came from the far end of the archive.

“Lady Vespera?”

She turned.

An elderly registrar was standing beneath the stairwell.

He held a stack of documents.

His expression was pale.

“Something has happened to the northern passage.”

Vespera felt her stomach tighten.

“What?”

“It isn't there.”

“Collapsed?”

“No, my lady.”

He swallowed.

“It was never built.”

***

The northern passage had connected the Great Archive to the funeral gardens for over six hundred years.

Vespera had walked through it as a child.

She remembered the smell of the garden soil, the pale flowering vines trained along the arches, and the bronze statues of the first nine archivists.

Her mother was buried there.

Now a wall stood where the passage had been.

Not new masonry.

Old masonry.

The stones were weathered.

Their mortar bore centuries of mineral staining.

Lichen covered the lower courses.

A narrow crack ran from the floor to the ceiling.

Vespera pressed her hand against it.

Cold.

Solid.

Ancient.

She looked at the registrar.

“Where are the gardens?”

He lowered his eyes.

“There are no gardens, my lady.”

She turned.

“What?”

“The records indicate the northern district has always been a sealed foundation vault.”

Vespera stared at him.

“You buried my mother there.”

The old man looked distressed.

“Your mother is interred in the western family crypt.”

“No.”

“I can show you the register.”

“No.”

He opened the document.

A silver plate showed the name of Vespera's mother, her dates of birth and death, and the location of her burial.

**WESTERN CRYPT — CHAMBER SEVENTEEN**

Vespera snatched the plate.

The inscription was deep.

Old.

There was dust in the grooves.

She remembered the funeral gardens.

She remembered the pale flowers.

She remembered standing beside the grave at twelve years old, watching rain fall through a crack in the distant ceiling.

She remembered her mother's coffin descending into the earth.

The register said none of it had happened.

Vespera looked up.

The registrar was watching her with frightened sympathy.

“Lady Vespera, perhaps the strain—”

She struck him with the plate.

Not hard enough to kill him.

Hard enough to knock him against the wall.

He fell, clutching his cheek.

Vespera stared at the blood on the silver.

For a moment she could not believe she had done it.

Then she dropped the plate and ran.

***

Arthur's mother had never existed.

That was what the county records said.

Her birth certificate was gone.

Her marriage license was gone.

Her death certificate was gone.

The cemetery website listed the plot where she was buried as vacant.

Arthur called the cemetery.

A man answered after eleven rings.

“Memorial services.”

“I'm looking for a grave.”

“Name?”

“Margaret Bellamy.”

Keyboard clicks.

A pause.

“Nothing under that name.”

“She was buried in 2011.”

“Sorry, sir.”

“Section nine. Row fourteen. Plot six.”

More clicking.

“That's an unused plot.”

Arthur gripped the phone.

“I was there.”

“Excuse me?”

“At the funeral. I was there.”

The man sighed.

“Sir, if you have documentation—”

“I carried the fucking coffin.”

Silence.

Arthur closed his eyes.

His voice had become too loud.

“I'm sorry.”

“It's okay.”

“No. I'm sorry. I just—”

He stopped.

The man on the other end cleared his throat.

“Sometimes records get transferred. You might try the county archive.”

Arthur looked at Kyle.

Kyle was staring at his laptop.

The screen had gone gold.

A little apple rotated in the center.

Arthur whispered, “It's doing it again.”

Kyle didn't answer.

His eyes were fixed on the display.

Arthur hung up.

“What?”

Kyle turned the laptop around.

A document was open.

It was an old shipping manifest.

Dated 1979.

Under the heading **INBOUND MATERIALS**, someone had entered:

**MARGARET BELLAMY — ONE HUMAN FEMALE — RECEIVED**

Arthur stared.

Below it was a signature.

His own.

***

The woman at the county archive had been smoking behind the loading dock when Kyle found her.

She was in her late fifties, perhaps older, with gray hair tied in a knot and the complexion of someone who had spent thirty years beneath fluorescent lights.

Her name was Ruth Mercer.

She wore a faded municipal jacket and held a cigarette between two fingers stained yellow.

Kyle showed her the shipping manifest.

Ruth looked at it.

Then at Arthur.

Then back at the screen.

“Where'd you get this?”

“Company archive.”

She took a long drag.

“Of course you did.”

Arthur stepped forward.

“Do you know what it means?”

Ruth exhaled.

The smoke drifted toward the wall.

For a moment it formed a perfect square.

Then dispersed.

“Depends,” she said.

“On what?”

“Whether you want the explanation that lets you sleep tonight or the one that keeps you awake.”

Arthur looked at Kyle.

Kyle said, “We'd prefer the second one.”

Ruth laughed.

It was a short, dry sound.

“That's what everybody says.”

She stubbed out the cigarette.

“Come inside.”

***

The county archive occupied a basement beneath a building that had once been a bank.

It smelled of damp cardboard, floor polish, and burnt electrical insulation.

Rows of metal shelves stretched beneath a ceiling packed with exposed pipes.

Ruth led them past cabinets of microfilm, municipal ledgers, and boxes labeled with dates that had been corrected so many times the original handwriting was invisible.

At the far end stood an old computer terminal.

Its monitor was green.

Not a modern display pretending to be old.

An actual green monochrome monitor, thick as a television, with a keyboard whose keys had worn smooth.

Ruth switched it on.

The screen flickered.

A cursor appeared.

**ARCHIVAL RECONCILIATION SYSTEM**

**ENTER SUBJECT**

Ruth typed:

**ARTHUR BELLAMY**

The cursor blinked.

A file opened.

Arthur saw his name.

His address.

His employment history.

His date of birth.

Then a section labeled:

**ANCESTRAL SOURCE RECORD**

Beneath it was a photograph.

A woman in a black mourning dress.

Vespera.

Arthur's throat tightened.

“What the hell is that?”

Ruth leaned back.

“That's your source.”

“My what?”

“Your ancestral source.”

Arthur stared at the screen.

“She's not my mother.”

“No.”

“She's not my grandmother.”

“No.”

“Then why—”

Ruth raised one hand.

“Because you're still thinking in straight lines.”

Kyle leaned forward.

“What does that mean?”

Ruth looked at him.

“Have you ever seen one of those old family trees where somebody marries their second cousin and the branches start folding back on themselves?”

Kyle nodded slowly.

“Imagine that, except the branches are made of time.”

Arthur looked at her.

Ruth continued.

“Some records don't descend from earlier records. They descend from later ones. Then those later records descend from the earlier ones.”

Kyle frowned.

“That's impossible.”

“Apparently.”

Ruth tapped the screen.

“Look at the archive date.”

Arthur read it.

**ORIGIN: NOT APPLICABLE**

“Not unknown,” Ruth said. “Not missing. Not corrupted.”

She tapped the words.

“Not applicable.”

Arthur felt his mouth go dry.

Ruth looked at him.

“You've been looking for the first copy.”

“Yes.”

“There isn't one.”

***

Vespera found the oldest surviving genealogy beneath the foundations of the Great Archive.

It was not written on silver.

It was written on something soft and white, thinner than vellum, covered in tiny black marks arranged in rigid rows.

The material smelled faintly of glue.

She had never seen anything like it.

The first page bore a date.

**2026**

Below it:

**EMPLOYEE RECORD — ARTHUR BELLAMY**

Vespera stared.

The letters were unfamiliar.

Yet once again their meaning pressed itself upon her without explanation.

She turned the page.

A photograph showed a man in a gray work uniform.

Arthur.

Younger.

Smiling awkwardly.

Behind him stood a woman with tired eyes.

His mother.

Vespera touched the image.

The paper was warm.

A tiny line of black text appeared beneath the woman's face.

**ARCHIVED**

The image flickered.

The woman's face blurred.

Then vanished.

The line changed.

**CONTENT REMOVED**

Vespera jerked her hand away.

The page went cold.

She looked at the empty space where the woman's face had been.

Then at the next page.

A silver genealogy had been drawn beneath the printed photograph.

At its root was the name of House Verrain.

Above it, in letters that had not existed a moment earlier, was another name.

**MARGARET BELLAMY**

Vespera felt the floor move.

Not shaking.

Turning.

The entire archive seemed to rotate around a point she could not locate.

For one terrible instant, the ceiling became the floor and the floor became a ceiling, and Vespera saw a woman in a hospital bed holding a newborn child.

The child was Arthur.

Then the image changed.

The woman was Vespera.

She was holding the same child.

Then the child was gone.

The hospital bed became a stone altar.

The stone altar became a warehouse loading dock.

The loading dock became the burial chamber of the first king.

Everything occupied the same place.

Everything had always been there.

Vespera fell to her knees.

The vision stopped.

She was back in the archive.

Her hands were bleeding.

The white paper lay open before her.

On it, a new sentence had appeared.

**THIS RECORD HAS BEEN UPDATED TO REFLECT CURRENT COMMUNITY STANDARDS.**

***

Ruth turned off the green monitor.

Arthur sat staring at the blank screen.

Kyle was pacing between the shelves.

“So somebody's editing history?”

Ruth shrugged.

“People edit records.”

“No. I mean literally.”

“Yes.”

“Like changing the past.”

Ruth scratched her chin.

“That's one way of looking at it.”

Kyle stopped.

“What other way is there?”

“That the past is what the record is doing.”

Arthur looked up.

Ruth saw his expression.

“Don't make that face. I'm not trying to be profound. I process municipal corrections. I don't get paid enough to invent metaphysics.”

She opened a drawer and took out a small metal box.

Inside were dozens of index cards.

Each bore a name, a date, and a handwritten note.

She selected one.

**RUTH MERCER — DECEASED 1989**

Arthur stared.

Ruth held it out.

“That's me.”

Kyle blinked.

“You died?”

“According to this card.”

She took out another.

**RUTH MERCER — BORN 2041**

“And according to this one, I haven't been born yet.”

She took out a third.

**RUTH MERCER — FOUNDING MATRIARCH, HOUSE CAEL**

Arthur looked at her.

Ruth smiled without humor.

“I've had a very busy life.”

Kyle shook his head.

“But which one's real?”

Ruth put the cards away.

“All three have records.”

“That doesn't answer the question.”

“No.”

She closed the box.

“It doesn't.”

Arthur stared at the drawer.

“Who built this?”

Ruth looked at him.

“The archive?”

“No. The whole thing.”

For the first time, her expression changed.

Not much.

Just a slight tightening around the eyes.

“That's the question that gets people into trouble.”

Arthur leaned forward.

“Why?”

“Because everybody wants there to be somebody in charge.”

She sat down.

“Somebody who wrote the first record. Somebody who built the first machine. Somebody standing outside the whole mess with a switch.”

Kyle nodded.

“Obviously.”

Ruth laughed.

There it was again.

That dry, exhausted little sound.

“Of course.”

She looked at Arthur.

“Now imagine you find the engineer.”

Arthur waited.

“You find his name. His birthplace. His school records. His first job. His original design documents.”

She tapped the metal cabinet.

“And all of those records were written by somebody whose life depends on the machine he supposedly built.”

Kyle stared.

Ruth continued.

“So you look for that person's ancestors. And their records lead back to the engineer.”

Arthur felt cold.

“That's a loop.”

“Yes.”

“Then who started it?”

Ruth spread her hands.

“Congratulations. You're back where you started.”

Kyle shook his head.

“That's not an answer.”

“No,” Ruth said. “It's a very expensive way of discovering you asked the wrong question.”

***

Arthur left the archive shortly before sunset.

The parking lot was nearly empty.

Kyle stood beside his car, scrolling through his phone with a concentration that bordered on panic.

Arthur looked at the sky.

Ordinary blue.

Thin clouds.

A passenger jet moved overhead.

For a moment he watched it, trying to decide whether it was passing through every point between where it had been and where it was going.

He realized he had no way of knowing.

Kyle approached.

“Ruth gave me something.”

Arthur turned.

Kyle held out a photograph.

It was old.

Sepia-toned.

A group of people stood outside a factory.

Arthur recognized the warehouse.

In the foreground was a man wearing a dark coat.

He looked exactly like Arthur.

Beneath the photograph was a date.

**1887**

Arthur stared.

Kyle pointed to the lower corner.

There was a small handwritten note.

**FOUNDING OF THE FIRST ARCHIVE**

Arthur looked at the man.

His own face.

His own tired eyes.

His own slightly crooked nose.

He felt sick.

“That's not me.”

Kyle said nothing.

“It's not.”

“I know.”

Arthur looked at the photograph again.

Behind the man, barely visible through a warehouse window, stood a woman in a black mourning dress.

Vespera.

She was holding a candle.

Arthur turned the photograph over.

On the back was a printed label.

**H.A. ARCHIVAL CONTENT — ORIGINAL SOURCE UNKNOWN**

Beneath it, in tiny handwriting:

**Every archive is somebody else's childhood.**

Arthur stared at the words.

Then he heard Ruth laughing.

Not from the building.

Not from the parking lot.

From his phone.

He took it out.

The screen showed a live feed of the county archive basement.

Ruth sat at the green terminal.

She was looking directly at him.

A caption appeared.

**THE FIRST ENGINEER HAS BEEN FOUND.**

Arthur watched Ruth's face.

She shook her head.

Very slowly.

Then the caption changed.

**THE FIRST ENGINEER HAS BEEN REMOVED.**

The screen went black.

Arthur looked toward the archive entrance.

The building was gone.

Not demolished.

Not hidden.

Gone.

In its place stood an empty lot overgrown with weeds.

A rusted chain-link fence ran along the sidewalk.

Behind it was a faded sign.

**SITE VACANT SINCE 1973**

Kyle dropped his phone.

Arthur backed into his car.

His knees gave way.

He slid down the door and landed on the asphalt.

For a long time he stared at the empty lot.

Then he began laughing.

He couldn't help it.

The sound came out thin and frightened.

He laughed until he was crying.

Kyle stood beside him, white-faced.

“What the fuck is happening?”

Arthur looked up at him.

He opened his mouth.

No explanation came.

Instead, he remembered Ruth's face.

The dry little laugh.

The cards showing her death and birth.

The green terminal.

The question that got people into trouble.

He wiped his eyes.

“I think,” he said, “we've been looking for the floor.”

Kyle stared.

“What?”

Arthur looked at the empty lot.

“There isn't one.”

***

In Orvane, Vespera stood before the oldest silver genealogy.

The sheet had gone completely black.

Every ancestral name had disappeared.

In their place was a single line of text.

**SOURCE MATERIAL UNAVAILABLE**

She touched it.

The metal was cold.

Behind her, the great archive shelves began to groan.

Somewhere deep beneath the city, a foundation shifted.

The water rose another inch.

Vespera watched the black surface.

Then, very slowly, she took the candle from its holder and placed it on the floor.

She opened the register to a blank page.

She picked up a stylus.

And she wrote a name.

**ARTHUR**

Far above her, in a parking lot that had never contained a municipal archive, Arthur felt something warm touch the back of his hand.

He looked down.

A thin line had appeared across his skin.

Not a cut.

Not a scar.

A name.

Written in small, careful letters.

**VESPERA**

Arthur stared.

Kyle crouched beside him.

“What is that?”

Arthur looked at the name.

Then at the vacant lot.

Then at the sky.

For the first time, he understood that the woman in the flooded archive was not simply trapped beneath his world.

She was also somewhere in its past.

And perhaps in its future.

And perhaps, in a way he could not yet bear to imagine, somewhere inside the records that had made him possible.

His phone buzzed.

One final notification appeared.

**YOUR FAMILY HISTORY HAS BEEN UPDATED.**

Underneath it was a large green button.

**ACCEPT CHANGES**

Arthur stared at the screen.

Then he turned it face down.

He did not press the button.

In Orvane, the silver stylus grew warm between Vespera's fingers.

And across the sealed, reciprocal circle of their histories, two people who had never met began leaving marks that neither archive could explain away.

The Golden Apple remained somewhere between them.

Smooth.

Silent.

Perfectly ordinary to anyone who had not yet been ruined by looking at it.

On its surface, three new words appeared.

**WHO CAME FIRST?**
