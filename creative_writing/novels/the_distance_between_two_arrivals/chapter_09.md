# Chapter 9: The Nine Empty Cells

The original Meridian schematic was printed in a language no one on the current ship spoke fluently.

It was not a human language. The notation consisted of relation marks, ordered phase labels and a repeating rectangular array. Its first layer counted eighty-one structural cells. The outer seventy-two contained habitation and transit paths. Nine central cells held control systems that were called empty because they began each authenticated cycle without an assigned payload.

The blueprint lay on the floor of the engineering observatory, projected ten meters across so Anja could walk through it. Holt stood near the center with his hands behind his back. Kala watched from a chair beside the cooling console.

"The public display shows seventy-one habitable cells," Anja said. "The old diagram shows seventy-two."

"That alone doesn't prove the missing one is inhabited," Holt replied.

"No. It proves the map must preserve its address even if the contents change. The current admission planner actually maps its neighbors across the empty boundary, as though one of the cells ceased to exist."

"How?"

"An administrative adapter. It merges both phase orders before building the navigation state. If a response comes from Seventy-Two, the adapter assigns it to Seventy-One or the lower spine depending on which way we probe. That was my first reading."

Kala crouched beside the projected grid. "So the same physical thing appears in different places because of the order in which the system asks."

"Right. The carrier knows that the sequence matters. A reporting layer has been taught to forget it."

Holt tapped a square at the center. "And the nine?"

"They are not nine extra habitats. They're addressable control positions. Their payload can be at local zero, but the addresses still exist. It's the difference between an empty room and a room that has been removed from the building."

"Then why would the adapter conflate them?"

Anja summoned the revision record. It had been written forty-nine years earlier. A software committee had classified Seventy-Two as a permanently failed asset, removed it from the active routing domain, and compressed its diagnostic outputs into a neighboring channel. The committee's report praised the result for eliminating spurious alarms.

One signature belonged to a man whose face Anja remembered from her childhood. He had visited her parents on an autumn feast day and brought a carved wooden bird.

"Old maintenance engineer," she said. "He fixed the children’s bicycles."

"Perhaps he thought he was fixing the alarms," Holt said.

Kala rose. "Then your discovery may be a lossless data problem with lethal consequences. That's a particular kind of tragedy."

Anja knew the technical remedy. They could construct a shadow planner using the entire registered geometry, then compare every result with the inherited route. But proving a state correct and convincing a command officer to maneuver a ship by it were different jobs.

"Will the Witness admit the old map?" she asked Holt.

"The Witness can validate a candidate against the original schema, preserve the received lineage and tell us where an unlicensed adapter changed an address. It cannot choose to replace an active navigation plan."

"Good," Anja said.

Kala looked at her. "Why good?"

"Because the only thing worse than an engineer making a life-and-death decision without authority is a machine doing it and pretending authority came from its ability to calculate."

They spent six hours reconstructing the mapping. The canonical transport frame had eighty-one 64-bit words; the text state used a different fixed-length carrier of five thousand one hundred eighty-four character positions. They could view the same relationship as seventy-two by seventy-two or eighty-one by sixty-four, but the distinct carriers were not interchangeable physical objects. The mapping was an address language that prevented them from losing the origin of a reading.

At the end, the shadow planner returned the same anomalous mass estimate Anja had found in Bay Four. This time it assigned the mass to one exact habitat and its stored water, soil, air and people.

Seventy-Two.

Anja printed the result. She liked paper at moments when a computer could make its own work look inevitable.

***

She brought the new model to Sev, who listened without interruption. The commander had the decency to look frightened.

"If we add the mass," he said, "the burn window changes."

"Yes."

"And if you are wrong, changing the model may kill us."

"Yes."

"If we leave the model as it is and you are right, we may also die."

"That is the problem."

Sev crossed to the viewport. Nacre filled half the sky now. Its illuminated atmosphere looked soft enough to enter by hand. Anja tried to imagine standing in a valley beneath that light and decided the effort was a dangerous comfort.

"I have fourteen days," he said. "You have until the next navigation board. Produce an independent mass proof, not merely a plausible model. And produce a resource ledger. We have given you space to investigate. The ship cannot become a debate without a maneuver plan."

"Will you restore Cell Seventy-Two to the public map?"

"Not yet."

"They've given us names."

"Names are not engineering verification."

"Names are civic evidence."

"Then the civic body will have to decide what they mean."

Anja almost thanked him, then remembered he was still withholding something that belonged to its owners.

As she turned to leave he said, "Do not misunderstand me. I want those people alive. I also want the eighty-three thousand whose names are on my screens alive. I cannot let either wish destroy the other."

Anja studied the commander. He had never told her he enjoyed power. That made the power more difficult to challenge.

***

She returned to Habitat Twenty late. Her mother was sitting at the kitchen table, not waiting exactly, but not engaged in anything that might disguise waiting.

"I found the original map," Anja said.

Sada looked at the folded sheet. "Your father wanted you to."

"He could have told me."

"He wanted to. Every year he said he would, then thought of what Command would do if you believed him."

"Did you agree?"

"No."

"Did you stop him?"

Sada watched the table for a while. "I didn't stop him. I let him carry a fear I shared because I was angry with him for being afraid."

Anja placed the map between them. "I can correct the model."

"Can you correct the years?"

"No."

Sada traced the outline of the seventy-second cell without touching the paper.

"Then begin with what you can repair," she said, "and don't call the rest of it erased."
