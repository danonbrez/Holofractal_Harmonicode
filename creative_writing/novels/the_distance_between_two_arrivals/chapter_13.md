# Chapter 13: Nacre's First Weather

Nacre had weather without land.

It was a world of oceans interrupted by basalt islands, volcanic ridges and shallow reefs that extended for hundreds of kilometers beneath pale water. Its atmosphere carried a constant gray cloud band at the equator, where a warm current met a colder one in an immense belt of storms. Two small moons drew tides through the inland shelves, lifting and dropping kilometers of water without anything resembling an Earthly beach to break their movement.

From Meridian's remote instruments the storms looked beautiful. Kala Niren distrusted beauty when it helped a committee finish a report.

She had been awake thirty hours when Anja entered the ecodynamics laboratory. A projection filled the center of the room: Nacre's ocean in a cross-section of sound, pressure and temperature. Layers of data rippled from the outer array. The young researchers around the screen spoke in low voices, as though the planet might hear them.

"Your hull anomaly taught me something," Kala said.

"The echo?"

"Not the echo. The test. Ask one way, then another. Preserve the sequence instead of averaging it away."

She showed Anja two sonar studies. Both used the same frequency components and the same total energy. In the first, a low-frequency pulse preceded a high one. In the second, the order was reversed. On an inert model of Nacre's mineral strata, the returns differed in ways the geologists could predict. In the live remote readings the difference was larger and persisted longer. A broad region of the equatorial shelf changed its response after being measured.

"It has memory?" Anja asked.

"That word has traps. A snowdrift remembers wind. A glass remembers pressure. A living nervous system remembers with mechanisms we have reasons to call biological. What we've found has a history dependence we don't understand."

The laboratory had tried obvious explanations. They varied the baseline, isolated instrument bias, exchanged sensor heads, shuffled pulse timings and compared regions with similar mineral compositions. The unexplained component held together through the controls, though its strength changed with tide phase.

"Could it be contamination from our ship?" Anja asked.

"Perhaps. That is why the dust from Home is useful. It formed in our hull under long-range interrogation cycles. We may have built a feedback path without noticing it."

"Can we separate them?"

"We're trying. We have candidate evidence of a complex planetary response. I cannot tell you whether there's a creature inside it, or an oceanic chemistry acting in organized fashion, or a piece of our instrument suite that is lying coherently."

Anja appreciated the answer. She knew how tempting it was to see a face in a pattern once a face would explain the fear.

Kala lowered the projection and sat on a stool, holding a cup of broth with both hands.

"The committee's draft calls Nacre sterile," she said.

"On what evidence?"

"No detected photosynthesis analog within our optical limits. No confirmed metabolic gas cycling at current instrument resolution. No discrete organisms in the upper atmospheric sample."

"That sounds like grounds for uncertainty."

"It is. Yet the settlement schedule already has a line for biological clearance. Someone wants a yes-or-no answer because rockets require instructions."

"Rockets can wait."

"People sometimes can't."

Anja thought of Sev's face above the navigation table. The commander could not postpone gravity indefinitely.

Kala put the two traces side by side. The shapes almost matched until the tail. In the second reading, a deep resonant band split into nine intervals. Anja recognized the division from a hull diagnostic, not because Nacre knew the ship's mathematics but because the ship's machine had been built to observe the world in that geometry.

"Are we listening to Nacre," she asked, "or hearing the shape of our own ear?"

"That's the right question," Kala said.

***

Later they used an independent passive sensor that had never transmitted toward the planet. It had spent the voyage looking for micrometeorite trails. Its clock was free-running and its calibration history intact. Anja and Kala decoded its old noise files with the order-preserving routines.

The same broad band appeared, weaker but unmistakable, months before the first directed sonar emission.

"No feedback origin," Anja said.

"Not solely from the ship," Kala corrected.

They still lacked samples. They still lacked a mechanism. But the report that called Nacre sterile had lost its strongest convenience: the belief that a null result from a lossy projection was equivalent to absence.

Kala entered the discovery in her candidate register. She did not send it as a landing prohibition. Instead she requested an independent biological review and suspension of irreversible surface operations until the evidence had been evaluated.

The form asked for a species name.

She left the field blank.

The form then asked for certainty of sentience. She entered UNDETERMINED.

It would not accept the submission.

Kala walked down the corridor to the review office and handed the signed pages to a human clerk.

"Your interface needs another answer," she said.

"What answer?"

"That we do not know."

***

Anja left the laboratory at ship midnight. On the public concourse, children had painted Nacre blue on a giant mural. A paper sun hung above it. Their teachers had written FIRST HOME under the picture.

Anja stood before the mural longer than she intended.

She had already heard a child call Home the habitat she had been taught to call vacuum. Now an entire planet might contain a witness her people had not yet learned how to detect.

It was frightening to think the same category error could happen at two scales. It was worse to think the ship would treat caution as obstruction, because that had been the habit by which seventy-two disappeared.

She took out Tavi's note and read it again.

All are alive.

The sentence was proved for the people who signed it. She had no right to transfer it unchanged to the ocean.

She had only the obligation to ask better questions.
