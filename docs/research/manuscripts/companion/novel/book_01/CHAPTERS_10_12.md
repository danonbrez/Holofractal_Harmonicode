# THE GOLDEN INVARIANT

## BOOK I — THE WORLD ON TELEVISION

### Chapter Ten — Counter-Tent

The mistake was thinking the network had politics.

The network had mechanics.

Politics arrived afterward.

Julian understood this when a second movement appeared using almost exactly the same tools as the first and believing almost exactly the opposite things.

The first time he saw them, they were gathered outside a county administration building west of Bellwether, carrying signs about taxation, government debt, bureaucratic power, and a Constitution nobody in the crowd appeared to agree how to interpret.

There were folding chairs.

Flags.

Homemade signs.

Livestreams.

Portable batteries.

Email lists.

Discussion boards.

Volunteer moderators.

A woman with a headset coordinating speakers through a shared schedule on a laptop.

Julian stood beside Elena near a food truck and watched a man in work boots explain to a camera that traditional media had deliberately ignored them.

A traditional media camera stood behind the first camera.

Julian started laughing.

Elena looked at him.

"Again?"

"Same scene."

"What?"

"Square downtown. Different signs."

Elena followed his gaze.

"You're surprised groups reuse cheap coordination tools?"

"No. I just thought the other people invented complaining about coverage while being covered."

"History is disappointing."

The gathering had its own internal media system.

People passed links.

Posted clips.

Ranked speakers.

Promoted phrases.

Shared photographs selected to represent the crowd as larger, older, younger, calmer, angrier, more patriotic, less partisan, depending on audience.

Julian recognized the structure immediately.

Different propositions.

Same compression.

The realization made him less comfortable with several opinions he had developed about the downtown encampment.

He had attributed certain pathologies to the people there that were better explained by network organization.

Popularity concentrated attention.

Constant broadcasting rewarded performers.

Internal disagreement became externally useful evidence for opponents.

Moderators accumulated invisible power.

People adapted speech to anticipated clips.

Every movement became partly a production studio.

The mechanism did not care whether the crowd wanted more government or less.

Julian said this to Elena.

She nodded.

"Propagation structure isn't proposition content."

"Exactly."

"You should write that down."

"I am not writing everything down."

"You started SOURCE and SUMMARY folders."

"That is different."

"You have a brass ruler in a sock drawer because it contains a number."

"It's not in a sock drawer."

"Where?"

He hesitated.

Elena smiled.

"Exactly."

The two movements hated each other.

Online, each described the other as uniquely manipulated.

Julian began following accounts from both.

This made the network's recommendation system increasingly confused about him.

It first suggested extreme versions of each group.

Then products associated with both.

Then dating advertisements that seemed to assume he had multiple incompatible identities.

He enjoyed this until the recommendations improved.

The system learned that he was not ideologically undecided.

He was behaviorally curious.

It began serving him conflict itself.

Posts where one group criticized the other.

Clips designed to provoke response.

Articles whose titles contained questions.

Julian noticed that he spent more time on these than on material he agreed with.

The system noticed too.

Soon his feed became a machine for supplying irritation.

He tried to reset it by deliberately clicking boring things.

Weather.

Transit.

Gardening.

Municipal bond notices.

For three days he achieved an internet so dull he felt spiritually restored.

Then a political account posted a photograph of a city employee sleeping on the job.

Julian clicked because he recognized the uniform.

The machine had him again.

He mentioned this to Elias Vane.

Not in person.

Vane had begun answering questions in a public online forum run by the Civic Systems Institute.

Julian created an account under the name jvance_maintenance and asked:

IF A RELEVANCE SYSTEM OPTIMIZES FOR ATTENTION, AND CONFLICT GETS ATTENTION, HOW DOES IT NOT BECOME A CONFLICT SYSTEM?

Vane responded the next day.

It does, unless attention is subordinate to another objective.

Julian wrote:

WHO DEFINES THE OTHER OBJECTIVE?

Vane:

That is governance.

Julian:

WHO GOVERNS THE GOVERNANCE?

Vane:

Ideally, nobody alone.

Julian sat back.

He disliked the answer because it was the same answer he would have given.

A second user replied:

THIS GUY JUST ADMITTED ALGORITHMS ARE GOVERNMENT.

Another:

THEY ALREADY ARE.

Another:

NO THEY'RE PROPERTY.

Another:

PROPERTY IS GOVERNMENT WHEN EVERYONE LIVES ON IT.

Another:

GO OUTSIDE.

The thread grew to four hundred comments.

Julian's original question disappeared into the argument it created.

He showed Elena.

"Congratulations."

"On what?"

"You generated engagement."

"That's not what I wanted."

"The system cannot read your heart."

"Neither can government."

"Now you're ready for politics."

The app economy arrived in Bellwether disguised as minor convenience.

At first it was games.

Then weather.

Music.

Maps.

Banking.

Restaurant reviews.

Coupon programs.

Transit schedules.

Every useful thing became a square icon.

Julian's phone screen filled.

He arranged apps into folders.

WORK.

MONEY.

CITY.

TOOLS.

TRASH.

Elena refused folders.

"Search."

"Then you don't know what you have."

"I know what I use."

"That's not the same."

"No."

The difference between application and institution began to blur.

A taxi company was a dispatch system plus cars.

A hotel was a booking system plus rooms.

A newspaper was distribution plus reporting.

A bank was authentication plus ledgers.

Once people carried authentication and maps in their pockets, businesses could be decomposed.

The first ride-matching service in Bellwether was technically not a taxi company.

This fact occupied a large portion of its legal defense.

Julian used it after a late archive session.

The driver was a schoolteacher named Ben who drove evenings to cover medical bills.

Julian sat in the back.

"How long you do this?"

"Three months."

"Like it?"

Ben thought.

"I like money."

"Good answer."

"App's fine."

"Who tells you where to go?"

"App."

"Can you reject?"

"Sure."

"What happens?"

"Acceptance rate."

"So yes."

"Yes."

"Does acceptance rate matter?"

"Probably."

"You know?"

"No."

"Then how do you decide?"

Ben looked at him in the mirror.

"You always interview drivers?"

"Maintenance."

"What does that mean?"

"I ask why things work."

"It works because I need money and you need a ride."

Julian smiled.

"Fair."

At the end the app asked him to rate Ben.

Five stars.

He tapped five.

Then stopped before submitting.

What did five mean?

Safe?

Friendly?

Clean car?

Correct route?

Political compatibility?

Music?

Conversation?

He submitted five.

Ben probably needed it.

That thought bothered him.

A score used for service quality had become a resource transfer.

Julian's rating was no longer description.

It was intervention.

The city explored app-based labor for seasonal maintenance.

Not core work.

Supplemental.

Snow emergencies.

Event cleanup.

Temporary staffing.

The procurement pitch used the phrase elastic workforce.

Julian imagined people stretching.

At a meeting a consultant demonstrated a dashboard.

Workers would receive tasks based on location, skill certification, availability, prior completion, and customer feedback.

"Customer?" Julian asked.

"Resident."

"Resident feedback ranks maintenance workers?"

"Among other factors."

"What factors?"

"Proprietary."

"Then how do we audit the ranking?"

The consultant smiled.

"The city defines performance goals."

"That's not ranking."

"Our model translates goals into dispatch optimization."

"What if it translates wrong?"

"We validate outcomes."

"Which outcomes?"

The project manager interrupted.

"We're early."

Julian wrote:

OPTIMIZATION WITHOUT VISIBLE OBJECTIVE.

Later he showed the notes to Elena.

"You are becoming unbearable."

"I was always unbearable."

"True."

"Proprietary logic deciding public work."

"Also not new."

"Software makes it scale."

"Vane says that."

"Stop."

She laughed.

The city's pilot never fully launched.

The union objected.

Procurement rules were unclear.

Insurance costs rose.

But versions of the model appeared elsewhere.

Food delivery.

Freelance work.

Shopping.

Home repair.

Task platforms.

Every service relationship generated scores.

The score became a portable shadow.

People adapted.

Drivers provided water bottles.

Hosts staged rooms.

Workers smiled at customers who insulted them.

Customers feared retaliation from workers they rated poorly.

Everyone became both evaluator and evaluated.

The network turned ordinary civility into measurable reputation and then claimed to have invented trust.

Julian thought Elena would appreciate that sentence.

She did not.

"Reputation is older than cities."

"Exactly."

"Then don't say invented."

"Fine. Industrialized."

"Better."

The political counter-movement and the downtown encampment both faded as physical gatherings.

Their network structures remained.

Pages became organizations.

Email lists became fundraising lists.

Influencers gained followers.

Volunteers became candidates.

Slogans entered television.

Television entered the feeds.

The old distinction between mass media and social media became a loop.

Julian's mother watched television while holding a tablet his brother had given her.

She would hear something on television and search it on the tablet.

Then read a post about the television segment.

Then watch a clip of someone reacting to the post about the segment.

"Ma," Julian said one evening, "you're making a feedback loop."

"Don't tell me what I'm making."

"You're watching people discuss you watching people."

"I'm checking."

"What?"

"If they're lying."

"How do you know the posts aren't lying?"

She looked offended.

"I read several."

"That's not verification."

"It is if they hate each other."

Julian stopped.

"Actually, that's not terrible."

"I raised you."

"You remind me constantly."

His mother had accidentally invented adversarial source comparison.

The network did not destroy reasoning.

Sometimes it improved it.

People could find primary documents.

Watch full speeches.

Compare claims.

Access expertise.

Read foreign news.

See records previously trapped in libraries.

Julian himself had benefited.

This was why all simple critiques failed.

The same infrastructure that compressed could decompress.

The same feed that manipulated could expose.

The same camera that humiliated could protect.

The same crowd that spread rumor could correct it.

Tools were not moral classes.

Usage was not enough either.

Architecture mattered.

In late 2011, Julian attended a Civic Systems Institute event because Vane was speaking in Bellwether.

Elena came reluctantly.

"You've been arguing with this man online for four years."

"Two."

"Emotionally four."

The lecture hall at Bellwether University was full.

Vane spoke without slides.

He described the approaching problem of mediated legitimacy.

"When institutions become too large for direct observation," he said, "people rely on compression. News is compression. Credit scores are compression. Résumés are compression. Search results are compression. Reputation is compression. Law is compression. A form is compression."

Julian glanced at Elena.

She mouthed:

EVERYTHING IS COMPRESSION.

He suppressed a laugh.

Vane continued.

"Compression is necessary. Loss is inevitable. The danger begins when a compressed representation becomes authoritative while the path back to source state becomes inaccessible."

Julian stopped smiling.

Afterward there was a reception.

Julian had no intention of approaching Vane.

Elena did it for him.

"Elias Vane?"

Vane turned.

"Yes."

"This is Julian Vance with a c."

Vane looked at Julian's name badge.

"Ah. The maintenance interrogator."

Julian stared.

"You remember?"

"You ask the same question every six months."

"What question?"

"Who governs the governance."

"Still don't have an answer."

"Neither do I."

Elena said, "Good."

Vane looked at her.

"Archivist?"

"How did you know?"

"Only archivists reward uncertainty."

"Scientists used to."

"Some still do."

Julian said, "If you know the problem, why build more filtering?"

Vane took a glass of water from a tray.

"Because the alternative isn't no filtering."

"What is it?"

"Unaccounted filtering."

"That sounds like a sales pitch."

"It is. Unfortunately, I believe it."

Julian studied him.

There was no villainy available.

No smug certainty.

Vane seemed tired.

"Do you work with ranking systems?" Vane asked.

"Maintenance."

"Meaning?"

"Buildings."

"Buildings are ranking systems."

Julian groaned.

Elena smiled.

Vane laughed.

"Sorry. Occupational disease."

Julian said, "We keep finding a constant."

"What kind?"

He told him.

179971.179971.

For the first time Vane's expression became difficult to read.

Not recognition exactly.

Interruption.

"You sure?"

Julian felt Elena become still beside him.

"Why?"

Vane looked down at his water.

"Old systems use strange constants."

"That's what everyone says."

"Then everyone may be right."

"You've seen it?"

"I've seen too many numbers."

"That's not an answer."

"No."

Vane placed the untouched glass on a table.

"Send me the contexts."

Julian said nothing.

Vane handed him a card.

Then walked away before Julian could decide whether that was suspicious.

Elena took the card from Julian.

"Do not send him anything tonight."

"I wasn't going to."

"You absolutely were."

"I was going to think about sending it."

"Your thinking has attachments."

Julian looked across the room.

Vane had joined another conversation.

"What was that?"

Elena put the card in her purse.

"A predecessor."

"Of what?"

"We don't know."

Julian smiled despite himself.

"Null."

"Correct."

Outside, students moved across campus staring into phones.

One walked directly into a trash can.

Julian helped him up.

The future, he thought, remained committed to comedy.


The two political movements eventually learned to inhabit one another's nightmares.

At the downtown encampment, Julian heard warnings that corporations had captured government.

At the county gathering, he heard warnings that government had captured ordinary life.

Online, each side circulated examples proving the other's fear while treating its own as categorical error.

Julian began saving paired claims.

NOT because he thought both sides were equally correct.

That phrase—both sides—had become an insult disguised as analysis.

He was interested in mirrored mechanisms.

Emergency.

Capture.

Corruption.

Silencing.

Manipulation.

False media.

Stolen future.

The nouns differed.

The grammar often did not.

When Elena saw the folder she said, "You are building a dictionary of mutually exclusive apocalypses."

"Useful?"

"Extremely."

"Why?"

"Because if two groups use the same failure class for different targets, the failure class may be more stable than either attribution."

Julian looked at her.

"That is the most you sentence I've heard."

"Marriage has consequences."

They began calling the folder FALSE MEMBRANE, another joke that became less funny.

Julian mapped rhetorical transitions.

A rule one group called protection, the other called control.

A subsidy one called repair, the other called extraction.

A protest one called democratic, the other called mob action.

A police response one called order, the other called suppression.

Sometimes the factual disputes were resolvable.

Sometimes not.

Often the most important disagreement was over the type of the event.

Was this emergency?

Crime?

Speech?

Discrimination?

Market failure?

Moral failure?

War?

Care?

Once the category changed, the permitted actions changed with it.

This interested Julian more than ideology.

The parser determined the toolbox.

A fire alarm authorized behavior ordinary conversation did not.

A medical diagnosis authorized intervention a preference did not.

A crime authorized coercion a disagreement did not.

A public-health classification authorized rules private risk did not.

Language was not merely description.

At institutional boundaries, language opened doors.

This was the deeper reason the network mattered.

It did not only distribute claims.

It distributed classifications at speed.

By the time formal institutions reviewed the underlying event, millions of people might already be acting against a compressed type.

Julian asked Vane about this at the Bellwether lecture reception.

"How do you prevent classification from becoming authority?"

Vane looked at him.

"You don't."

"Great."

"You prevent it from becoming unreviewable authority."

"How?"

"Separate proposal from execution."

"Meaning?"

"A ranking system can propose. A human can decide."

"Humans rubber-stamp."

"Then require reason."

"Humans copy machine reason."

"Then preserve competing inputs."

"People don't read them."

Vane smiled.

"Mr. Vance, if you are waiting for an architecture that makes irresponsible people responsible, you will wait forever."

"Then what's the point?"

"Make responsibility addressable."

Julian considered.

Vane continued.

"Systems cannot guarantee virtue. They can make it harder to hide the path by which power moved."

Elena had joined them.

"Unless the path is proprietary."

Vane turned.

"Yes."

"Unless retention expires."

"Yes."

"Unless authority consolidates."

"Yes."

"Unless users have no realistic exit."

"Yes."

"Then your architecture depends on institutions voluntarily limiting themselves."

Vane looked at her.

"All constitutional systems do."

"Software is not a constitution."

"Not yet."

The word yet landed badly.

Vane noticed.

"I mean scale of dependence, not legitimacy."

"Those can converge," Julian said.

Vane nodded.

"That's why we should be doing this before they do."

"Who is they?"

Vane laughed.

"Everyone who finds convenience profitable."

That answer sounded less paranoid than any named enemy.

The threat was a business model, an administrative preference, an efficiency metric, a social habit.

Nobody needed to conspire if incentives aligned.




Julian's favorite demonstration of mirrored politics came from lawn signs.

A street near his mother's house displayed eight signs during one election season.

Four warned that freedom was disappearing.

Four warned that democracy was disappearing.

Two houses displayed both kinds because roommates disagreed.

Julian photographed the block.

Elena asked why.

"Same emergency word."

"Different noun."

"Same function."

"What function?"

"Make ordinary election feel like last gate."

Elena looked at the image.

"Maybe they believe it is."

"That's what matters."

Fear increased authorization.

People accepted measures during existential threat they would reject during ordinary disagreement.

The Long Emergency had taught institutions this structurally.

The network taught everyone else.

Every issue could be framed as terminal.

If every election was the last election, exceptional tactics never expired.

Julian wrote:

EMERGENCY HAS NO NATURAL SUNSET.

Then beneath it:

MUST BE VERSIONED.

Elena added:

AND WHO DECLARES END?

They left that unanswered.

### Chapter Eleven — Five Stars

The city began asking residents how they felt about potholes.

Julian considered this the natural endpoint of democracy.

The new service portal allowed residents to submit complaints, upload photographs, track requests, and rate completed work.

On paper, it was excellent.

Previously, a person called a municipal number, waited, explained the problem, received no reliable ticket, called again, explained it again, and eventually concluded the city existed mainly to test cardiovascular health.

Now every request generated an identifier.

Every identifier generated status.

Reported.

Assigned.

In Progress.

Resolved.

The first week, Julian was delighted.

Then he learned that Resolved did not mean resolved.

It meant somebody had closed the ticket.

He discovered this after receiving a complaint about a leaking fountain valve in a public plaza.

The ticket history showed:

REPORTED.

ASSIGNED.

IN PROGRESS.

RESOLVED.

Resident rating: ONE STAR.

Comment:

STILL LEAKING.

Julian drove there.

The fountain was still leaking.

He called the crew listed on the ticket.

"We didn't fix it," the supervisor said.

"Why closed?"

"Duplicate."

"Duplicate with what?"

"Other ticket."

Julian found the other ticket.

RESOLVED.

"Why's that closed?"

"Parts."

"Parts isn't resolved."

"In our queue it is."

"Why?"

"If we leave it open, it counts against completion time."

Julian stared at the phone.

"So you close jobs that can't be completed?"

"We close the request. Parts request is separate."

"Resident sees resolved."

"Portal translation."

"Translation from what?"

"Workflow."

There it was again.

The internal state and displayed state differed without anyone considering it a lie.

Inside the department:

REQUEST CLOSED / PART PENDING.

Outside:

RESOLVED.

Julian reopened the ticket.

His supervisor closed it again.

He escalated.

The portal vendor said the public label could be customized in the next release.

For three months the fountain remained one star.

Julian began reading ratings.

This was a mistake.

Five stars for ordinary repairs.

One star because a technician arrived after lunch.

One star because a building closed while work was performed.

Three stars because "guy was fine but city sucks."

Five stars because "nice eyes."

One star because the resident disagreed with a parking regulation the maintenance worker did not create.

The ratings were data.

They were also nonsense.

The difficulty was telling which.

A technician named Coyle consistently received lower scores than others.

Supervisors began discussing coaching.

Julian rode with him.

Coyle was technically excellent.

He was also rude.

Not abusive.

Rude.

He did not explain.

He answered yes/no questions with yes/no.

He did not apologize for delays caused elsewhere.

Residents hated him.

"Your scores suck," Julian told him.

Coyle tightened a valve.

"Scores don't fix valves."

"They affect your review."

"Then review can fix valve."

"No."

"Exactly."

Julian wanted to argue.

Instead he watched Coyle work.

A five-star system had detected something real.

Coyle's communication was bad.

It had not detected the thing the department cared about most.

His work was good.

The scalar flattened the vector.

Julian wrote that in his notes before realizing he was now using Elena's language.

At home, Mira was three years old and believed phones existed primarily to show photographs of herself.

Julian's relationship with Elena had changed status in all the ways that mattered without producing a ceremony.

She had moved in.

They shared bills.

They argued about groceries.

They coordinated childcare.

His mother lived four blocks away and exercised grandparental authority as an extralegal branch of government.

Mira had Elena's concentration and Julian's suspicion of instructions.

When told not to touch something, she asked why.

When told because, she asked what because meant.

Julian considered this promising until she applied it to bedtime.

Mira's existence had generated more digital records in three years than Julian had produced before adulthood.

Hospital registration.

Insurance.

Photographs.

Pediatric portal.

Daycare app.

Family messages.

Videos.

Backup copies.

His brother had created a private photo album online before Julian and Elena brought her home from the hospital.

Elena had nearly killed him.

Not metaphorically, Julian thought.

The argument had possessed sufficient kinetic energy.

"Delete it."

"It's private."

"Delete it."

"Family only."

"She is two days old."

"Exactly. Family wants to see."

"Then send them photographs."

"That's what this does."

"No. This creates an account relationship."

"For me."

"Containing her."

Julian intervened.

"Can we not do this while my mother is holding the baby?"

His mother said, "Leave me out of it."

"You are physically in it."

His brother deleted the album.

Or said he did.

Copies remained in relatives' downloaded folders.

Elena could not recall the distributed state.

She hated this.

Years later she would explain that Mira had taught her the difference between deletion and loss of addressability.

At the time she simply said, "Once something is distributed, control becomes negotiation."

Julian thought that sounded like motherhood.

The city faced the same problem at scale.

Departments accumulated data because storage was cheap.

Deletion became harder than retention.

Retention policies existed, but exceptions multiplied.

Litigation holds.

Audit requirements.

Historical value.

Security logs.

Fraud prevention.

Service improvement.

Training.

Every dataset acquired a reason to remain.

The public conversation about privacy became louder.

A major network service changed its policies and triggered backlash.

Users discovered that information they believed limited to certain audiences could be surfaced differently under new settings.

The company responded with controls.

Then explanations of controls.

Then simplified controls.

Then a dashboard.

Then a dashboard explaining the dashboard.

Julian opened the settings page.

It contained more decisions than his mortgage paperwork.

He called Elena from upstairs.

"Come here."

"No."

"You don't know what I want."

"Privacy settings."

"How did you know?"

"You used the voice."

"What voice?"

"The man reading a cursed contract."

She came up.

They sat beside each other.

WHO CAN SEE FUTURE POSTS?

WHO CAN LOOK YOU UP USING YOUR EMAIL?

ALLOW PARTNERS TO PERSONALIZE EXPERIENCES?

ALLOW APPLICATIONS USED BY FRIENDS TO ACCESS INFORMATION?

Julian stopped.

"Applications used by friends?"

Elena leaned in.

"Read."

He did.

"So my friend authorizes access to information about me?"

"Apparently some information."

"Which?"

"Click."

A list opened.

Julian laughed.

"This is consent designed by tax lawyers."

"Tax lawyers are more concise."

He selected restrictive settings.

The site warned that some features might not work as intended.

Elena said, "There."

"What?"

"That's the actual price."

"What is?"

"Privacy is represented as degraded functionality."

Julian looked.

"Sometimes it is degraded functionality."

"Yes."

"Then the warning isn't manipulation."

"Not necessarily."

They sat.

That was the exhausting part.

Almost nothing was pure.

A system could truthfully say data improved services.

A user could truthfully say the service was not worth the data.

The dispute occurred at the boundary.

What had been authorized?

For which purpose?

For how long?

With which successors?

Those questions sounded increasingly legalistic because ordinary life increasingly ran through contracts nobody read.

Julian began saving terms-of-service versions.

Elena approved.

Then became alarmed by how many.

"This is impossible."

"Exactly."

"No human can track this."

"Vane wins."

"Do not say that."

"He wins."

"Partial credit."

Julian created a small tool that downloaded policy pages monthly and compared changes.

The first version ran on his home computer.

It produced ugly text diffs full of navigation menus and punctuation.

He improved it.

Elena added categories.

Scope.

Retention.

Third-party sharing.

Appeal.

Deletion.

Identity.

Authorization.

They called it Terms Rat.

Mira called it Rat.

Rat began finding changes.

Most trivial.

Some meaningful.

One social service changed a sentence from:

We may use information to improve features you use.

to:

We may use information to improve and personalize services.

Elena circled personalize.

"That's large."

"It's one word."

"Exactly."

"What does personalize include?"

"We don't know."

"What evidence would tell us?"

"Later behavior."

Julian saved the predecessor.

This habit slowly became their private religion.

Not belief.

Comparison.

At work, ratings expanded.

Managers received dashboards.

Average satisfaction.

Time to resolution.

Repeat requests.

Complaint rate.

Cost.

The data improved some things.

Crews with recurring problems became visible.

Broken processes became obvious.

Departments could no longer bury piles of unprocessed complaints.

Then targets appeared.

Average resolution under forty-eight hours.

Satisfaction above 4.2.

Repeat requests below threshold.

People optimized.

Tickets closed early.

Residents nudged for ratings.

Difficult cases avoided.

Easy jobs split into multiple successes.

The metric had begun as observation.

Then became incentive.

Then behavior.

Then reality.

Julian wrote:

MEASURE -> TARGET -> GAME.

Elena added:

GAME -> NEW MEASURE.

He added:

NEW GAME.

They both stared at the page.

"This could continue forever," Julian said.

"Yes."

"That's depressing."

"Only if you believe measures can replace judgment."

"Most budgets do."

The city's annual satisfaction score rose.

The fountain still leaked.

Eventually Julian obtained the part himself through a surplus exchange with another department.

He fixed it on a Saturday.

The ticket had been closed for eighty-seven days.

The resident was an older man who came outside when he saw Julian.

"You finally fixing that?"

"Trying."

"Took long enough."

"Yeah."

"City's useless."

"Sometimes."

The man watched him.

"You want a coffee?"

"Sure."

He brought a paper cup.

Julian replaced the valve.

The leak stopped.

"How do I rate you?" the man asked.

"Please don't."

The man laughed.

"No, seriously."

"There's probably a link."

"I gave the last guy one star."

"That was me."

The man looked embarrassed.

"You weren't here."

"I was assigned."

"That's ridiculous."

"Correct."

"You want me to change it?"

Julian tightened the housing.

"No."

"Why?"

"Because it accurately describes your experience."

The man studied him.

"You're a strange city worker."

"Five stars."

The man laughed again.

Julian packed his tools.

On the drive home his phone displayed a notification.

A privacy policy had changed.

Rat had detected it.

One sentence.

One word.

He pulled over and opened the diff.
