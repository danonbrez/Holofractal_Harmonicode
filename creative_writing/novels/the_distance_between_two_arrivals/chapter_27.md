# Chapter 27: The Children of the Crossing

Eleven years was an entire childhood.

Anja heard that sentence at a town meeting, spoken by a girl whose feet did not reach the floor beneath her chair. She was nine and wore a blue arrival ribbon braided into her hair. Her grandmother had promised to help her plant an apple tree on Nacre. If the long route proceeded, the girl would be twenty when the ship returned. Her grandmother might not be alive.

"Who gives me the years back?" the girl asked.

Nobody in the room had an answer.

Anja would have preferred to face a damaged engine. A damaged engine had a list of failures and sometimes an order in which they could be repaired. The girl's lost future was not an error with a replacement part.

"I can't," Anja said. "I can tell you why the immediate maneuver is not safe, and I can show you the other path we've found. I can't give you an eleven-year childhood on a planet when the ship cannot safely go there now."

The girl looked down at the ribbon. "Then why did everyone tell us the day was certain?"

Anja could answer that. She told the room how a valid hope had been turned into a certainty by institutions that needed a schedule, how disputed observations had been compressed until they looked like finished knowledge, and how the ship's people had learned to build their lives around a date none of them controlled. She did not blame the child for trusting the adults.

The girl listened. "Will you tell the next children the truth?"

"Yes."

"Even when it makes them sad?"

"Especially then."

***

Rue and Tavi organized a meeting of younger citizens from Home and the registered habitats. It began with an argument over the use of an old recreation hall. The hall had been reserved for arrival celebrations, and a group of children had already built a paper model of Nacre's shoreline. Rue wanted to use it for a repair cooperative. The younger registered citizens accused her of stealing the only good thing they had been preparing for.

Tavi proposed dividing the time. A few people hated the compromise on principle, which meant it was probably viable.

They spent the first hour showing one another their worlds. The children from Habitat Twelve brought videos of a public pool and a tree nursery. Home's children brought photographs of their growing terraces, copper tags and a crowded school in which the same teacher taught advanced mechanics and basic reading.

One boy from the registered side asked why Home had not simply broken the wall.

"Because the other side had guns and control of the air locks," Rue said.

"Did they really?"

"At different times. And because we'd have been leaving sick people behind. And because we were scared. Pick whichever answer helps you see us as less heroic."

The boy frowned. "I wasn't calling you heroic."

"Good."

An argument followed, then an exchange of snacks. A girl from Home tasted fresh hydroponic strawberries for the first time and decided their registered neighbors could not be entirely contemptible. Another girl learned that Home had a game played by flicking copper rings along a wall and became determined to win at it.

At the end they took down the WELCOME TO NACRE banner from the hall. Not as an act of rejection. They folded it carefully and placed it in storage for a day when welcome could mean something more precise.

In its place they hung a map of all eighty-one cells. The ninth central address was not a room anyone could live in, but it was drawn. The seventy-second peripheral habitat was bright, not blank.

People argued about the color for an hour.

***

Sev met his daughter in the command kitchen that evening. Her name was Liora, she was twenty-six, and she worked in the agricultural methane reclamation plant. They had spoken little in recent months. She disagreed with his reluctance to submit the mission to a public vote.

"I voted for the long route," she said.

He nodded. "I assumed you might."

"Not because I wanted to hurt you."

"I never thought that."

"I grew up hearing your promise to Grandfather. I wanted it too. I still want a world outside a hull. But there's no way to make a promise to the dead that gives you ownership of the living."

He winced. She noticed and sat down across from him.

"You helped keep this ship alive," she said. "All my life I trusted you to know when to say no. Please don't turn that into a reason we cannot say no to you."

Sev pushed his plate aside. "I cannot accept that the lives of people who will die before our return are simply a cost on a graph."

"Neither can I."

"Then why vote for it?"

"Because the engine warning is real. Because the planet may already have inhabitants. Because you taught me that a mechanic does not force a machine past its safe load simply because the job is late."

He remembered teaching that lesson to a twelve-year-old girl who had dismantled the kitchen fan and demanded to know why it could not run twice as fast.

"Perhaps I taught too well," he said.

"No," Liora replied. "You taught me enough to disagree with you."

***

Tavi returned to Home carrying a bag of strawberry seeds. She placed them in Elsa's greenhouse beside the copper braid and the old woman's empty chair. Jori said the plants might not thrive under Home's lamps. Tavi wanted to try anyway.

She had spent the day with people who had been taught her home did not exist. Most had listened. A few had laughed at her accent. She had learned there were different levels of ignorance, and that not every correction had to become a war.

At midnight the ship's public clock displayed four days until the original capture window.

Tavi studied it from the greenhouse doorway.

The vote had been counted. The new route had a legal mandate. The scientists were still measuring. The engineers were still testing. It seemed to her that the adults had finally begun behaving as though the future were something they might share responsibility for rather than something they could deliver wrapped in a schedule.

Then a call came from Anja asking her to attend one more engineering review.

The ship was still traveling. The last four days could be as important as the past fifty-six years.
