# Chapter 8: The Ledger of Copper

Tavi did not like being called brave.

A brave person was someone older people could send to a dangerous door and then praise afterward. She had watched the council use the word on three occasions: once after a pipe repair that cost a man his fingers, once when a woman volunteered to skip medicine for her granddaughter, and once when Tavi herself carried the census braid through a tunnel during a power outage. She had not enjoyed any of those events.

She preferred precise descriptions.

For fifty-six years, the people of Home had recorded every birth, death, marriage, adoption, expulsion, return and identity change on three carriers. The first was paper. The second was copper, scored with tiny marks at three fixed intervals. The third was a memory recitation performed in groups of three elders, none of whom was permitted to be alone with the final book.

The practice had begun when the official ship refused to acknowledge their requests for storage allocation. Home had no trusted upstream archive and, for years, no spare energy to maintain one. Copper was not indestructible. People died. Paper burned. The point of three carriers was that a lie would have to cross independent witnesses before it became an ancestor.

Tavi had inherited the third generation of the braid. It was heavy, a long wire wound around wooden spools. Her grandmother had made her practice its intervals by touch in the dark.

Now she brought the oldest spool to the vestibule, where Holt waited on the other side of the glass. Anja stood beside him with a scanner that could read scratches without treating them as numbers until a human transcriber confirmed the symbols.

"There's no absolute proof," Holt said after the first hour.

Tavi did not argue. She had learned there were two kinds of people who said such things: those who wanted the truth to have limits, and those who wanted the limits to become an excuse.

"No," she said. "There's not. What is it you can prove?"

"We can prove the copper isn't a recent print. We can prove the wear tracks cross the marks in a sequence consistent with use over decades. We can compare it with paper entries and physical records. We can test whether the material came from the same manufacturing stock used before the fire. We cannot prove a person didn't lie while making a scratch."

"Then let's find a scratch with something else behind it."

She pointed to an entry dated thirty-seven years earlier. It named a woman called Hena Merrow, who had borne twins during a failed pressure cycle. One twin had lived eleven hours; the other had survived. Tavi's grandmother had taught her that the dead child was still one of them because the census could not be made smaller merely by being late.

Holt found the date on an obsolete emergency power ledger. A thirty-two-minute current draw had passed through a nominally abandoned medical line on the same night.

"Coincidence?" he asked.

"Maybe," Tavi said. "Keep going."

At another entry, a pressure loss corresponded to a registered water anomaly. At a third, a long period of hunger corresponded to a shipment of emergency flour that had gone missing from a depot in Habitat Seventy-One. The records did not prove every story. They formed a landscape of relationships too costly and irregular to invent convincingly in a week.

Anja brought the jar of reddish dust. Tavi found twenty years of collection dates and laid them beside the ship's deep-space acoustic logs. The deposits peaked when the long-range array was aimed at Nacre, even at immense distance.

"Not impossible," Anja said. "We may have tuned our instruments at those times in a way that affects the hull."

"So we're contaminated by the act of listening?" Holt asked.

"We might be."

Tavi made a note. "That's a less comforting answer than the one I expected."

"Science often is," Anja said.

***

Holt showed her a white-paper excerpt from the vessel's original archival design. He explained the distinction between a fixed physical frame that carried eighty-one machine words and a separate long textual serialization that gave every state position a durable address. The numbers interested Tavi less than the principle.

"You can change how you show a thing without changing which thing it was," she said.

"Under the right transformations, yes."

"But if you throw away where each part came from, the thing isn't recoverable."

"Exactly."

Tavi examined the braid. "Then the trouble with the old evacuation report isn't only that it lied. It changed our relationship to every event afterward."

Holt nodded. "A bad parent poisons a family of conclusions."

She looked at him sharply. "Families aren't conclusions."

"No," he said at once. "Forgive me. I meant records."

She accepted the correction because he made it without bargaining.

In the afternoon Nalin asked her to return home for a council session. Some delegates worried that sharing the full braid would allow Command to assign responsibility for every unregistered medical transfer and black-market ration. Others worried that a public census would become an evacuation order. A third group, younger and angrier, argued that all records should be published immediately, including private names, because the registered ship had no claim to privacy after denying theirs.

Tavi listened and then asked whether any of them had requested permission from the people whose histories they intended to expose.

The room fell quiet.

"Do you suggest we leave the false history standing?" asked Lera, the assembly chair.

"No. I'm saying the dead can't consent and the living still can. We can publish totals, technical evidence, and the acts of office without sending every child's medical file to eighty thousand strangers."

"Then whose authority decides what is necessary?"

"Ours," Tavi said. "In a vote with records that everybody can challenge. You taught me that."

Nalin smiled, though his eyes remained tired. The council authorized a bounded release of verified technical data and a private medical exchange. It withheld household histories until each affected family could participate. Tavi had expected the adults to find a reason to refuse. Instead they gave her the task of organizing the testimony.

It was an annoying victory.

***

That night she unrolled the first spool alone in the archives room. Near the beginning was an entry no one had ever explained fully.

SADA SOREL. PETITIONER. OUTSIDE DOOR. REQUESTS ACKNOWLEDGMENT.

It had been made on the second day after the sealing. Beneath it someone had added: HEARD.

Home's oldest stories told of a girl who shouted through a sealed intercom until her voice broke. The adults had never told the girl they could hear her. They feared she would be punished for returning. They feared, too, that acknowledging her would require a response they were not ready to give.

Tavi touched the copper with her fingertip. So much of her life had been shaped by people who tried to protect one another through silence.

She wrote a new entry in the paper book.

**SADA SOREL'S DAUGHTER CAME TO THE DOOR. HER NAME IS ANJA. SHE DID NOT OPEN IT WITHOUT ASKING.**

She could not know how the story would end. An entry was not an ending. She recorded what had happened and left the next line blank.
