# Chapter 33: The Decision That Has Parents

The last hearing lasted twenty-three minutes.

It had taken fifty-six years to acknowledge one inhabited cell, weeks to test a revised mass model, days to debate a route, and hours to repair a breach. Now the ship needed a lawful decision before the moon correction window closed.

Station Nine was a plain room with three folding chairs, a wall clock and a table scratched by years of engineering work. Ressa stood with the delegates from registered habitats and Home. Dima appeared on a secure connection from the operations desk. Holt's archive display showed the current authenticated state and the candidate trajectory. Anja waited beside the door with the damaged-spine report.

"The extraordinary vote authorized the eleven-year return route," Ressa said. "The manual-control incident has altered the structural state. We must determine whether the revised execution remains within the public mandate and the safety constraints."

Dima reviewed the changes. The engine impulse would be shorter and shifted by several hours. The moon encounter remained in the certified corridor. Life-support reserves were lower but inside the declared contingency plan after additional repairs. The changes did not require landing, atmospheric contact or a new political objective.

A Home delegate asked whether the reservoir transfer could be reversed after the burn.

"Yes," Daret answered from Station Four. "The schedule is signed and independently monitored. The tanks will return to their long-route operating levels after the load drops."

A registered delegate asked whether the ship would be able to abort if the correction failed.

"Within the declared limit," Dima said. "We have an escape-safe contingency that preserves life support, though it would require later trajectory replanning. We cannot promise every outcome is survivable."

The delegates conferred. Ressa read the statute on emergency review. The authorized objective had not changed, the revised technical route was admitted, and the engineering differences were within the boundaries for delegated execution.

"Then I certify that the existing public mandate remains valid for this correction," she said.

Each delegate signed. The receipts were preserved in separate stores. The witness chain included not only the intended state but the accident that made the revision necessary. No one was permitted to rewrite the old schedule as though the dangerous preparation had never happened.

***

Sev asked to make one statement before the engines fired.

Ressa granted him a limited witness channel. He appeared on the command screen wearing an ordinary engineer's coat. The former commander looked older than he had four days earlier.

"I supported immediate capture because I feared the cost of waiting," he said. "I remain troubled by that cost. I also accept that I exercised emergency control without the required condition and caused injury. The present route has been authorized by the people whose lives it will change, including citizens our records excluded for decades. I will assist as a technical witness. I will not issue or contest the command."

He signed a dissent note identifying the human costs he believed the long route should continue to address. He did not use the note to withhold authority. Anja was glad he had not been required to pretend his doubts had vanished. Dissent preserved under an accepted decision was not sabotage.

Dima thanked him for the testimony and closed the channel.

***

All nine station lights turned from amber to white as local witnesses confirmed their assigned conditions. Anja listened through the acoustic net. Beneath the low sounds of coolant and hull flexure, she could hear the familiar patient hum of the ship. Nothing in its voice promised success. It was a machine doing its work under the limits of its makers and maintainers.

"Station One?" Dima called.

"Pressure within limit."

"Two?"

"Phase and route mapping verified."

"Three?"

"Medical shelters ready."

"Four?"

"Water mass distribution confirmed."

"Five?"

"No atmospheric or surface contact predicted."

"Six and Seven?"

"Propulsion and load witnesses confirmed."

"Eight?"

"Predecessor, change and receipt available. No unresolved canonical discontinuity."

"Nine?"

"Public mandate confirmed. Execution authorized."

Dima gave the command.

The engine note began far below the sound people usually heard. A pressure like a change in the shape of the world passed through the hull. Cups trembled on tables. Water tilted in storage tanks. The great machine expelled a measured stream of matter into space and accepted the equal and opposite change to its motion.

Anja watched the structural readings rise, level and fall. The damaged rib held. The nine stations reported their local checks without waiting for the others to speak on their behalf.

For a few minutes Meridian was not a city traveling in the general direction of a promise. It was a vessel changing its actual course.

The burn ended on time.

The moon encounter would come later. It could still fail. The ship had survived the first necessary action.

***

Tavi watched from the public observatory with Rue, the boy who had asked about real stars and a crowd of people from several habitats. The planet filled the window, enormous and pale, and the ship's course bent away from its immediate capture.

Some people cried. A few cheered. Most watched quietly.

Tavi had expected to feel robbed when the engine changed their path. Instead she felt the peculiar ache of a door remaining open without being crossed.

"We're leaving it," Rue whispered.

"For now," Tavi said.

"Eleven years."

"Yes."

Rue looked at her. "Will we still be friends?"

Tavi laughed, then began to cry. "I hope so. I don't know whether I'll like you in eleven years."

"That's fair."

They held hands while Nacre slid toward the edge of the viewport.

***

Anja went to Habitat Thirty-Eight after the burn. Aro Fen was awake in the ward, testing a mobility brace that the medics had fitted to his remaining leg. He asked whether the lower spine had survived.

"Yes," she said.

"Then somebody finally listened to a bad joint."

"Several somebodies."

"Good. Don't put the whole thing on one hero. Heroes always forget to maintain the seals."

She promised not to.

On the way home she passed the former sealed door to Home. Its sign had been removed. The vestibule was open under the new pressure protocol, with a human steward available on either side. A group of children walked through carrying seedlings in small pots. Their teacher checked each child's name, not against a list of permitted identities but against a list of who had arrived safely.

Anja stood aside to let them pass.

The ship was going away from the planet for eleven years. It had also arrived somewhere it had refused to recognize for fifty-six.

Both things were true. Neither cancelled the other.
