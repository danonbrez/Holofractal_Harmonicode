# Chapter 1: The Difference in the Weighing

Anja Sorel had spent nineteen years listening to the ship complain, and she knew the difference between a complaint and a confession.

A complaint repeated. A bearing that needed grease sang the same high note through every rotation. A poorly trimmed air vane gave a little cough at the beginning of each pressure cycle. The Meridian had been built with so many parts that no person could know all of them, but they could be taught to say what was wrong in a language of recurrence.

A confession did not repeat. It made the rest of the machine suddenly intelligible.

At 06:18 ship time, three weeks before the arrival the children had spent their lives rehearsing, a balance test came back heavy.

Anja watched the figures settle on her pad. The discrepancy was small relative to a vessel carrying eighty thousand human beings, forests, lakes, a fusion plant, and three hundred years of spare steel. It was large relative to a maneuver calculated to within a palm-width of permissible error. The mass model showed an unaccounted sixteen hundred metric tons between the upper and lower acoustic sectors.

A defective gravimeter might have made that much of a fool of a young engineer. Anja was thirty-eight. She tested the instrument, the mounting, the reference probe and, because of a superstition that had served her better than pride, the lead she had connected with her own hands.

The error remained.

She was kneeling beneath a folded ladder in Acoustic Bay Four. Through the service grate above, people crossed the morning concourse with school bags and lunch tins, preparing for work on a ship that was preparing to become a city beneath an alien sky. A children's choir was practicing an arrival anthem somewhere in Habitat Twelve. The music reached Anja as an uneven vibration through the steel.

She turned the diagnostic order around.

The first sequence, designated XY by an engineer who had died before Anja was born, sent a low structural pulse along the axial member and then measured the tangential return. The reverse sequence, YX, began with the tangential probe and finished at the axis. Administrative manuals treated them as equivalent checks. The old phase registers did not.

The display changed. Not the magnitude of the discrepancy. Its location.

Seventy-Two.

Anja looked at the number until the loose end of the ladder strap touched her cheek.

Seventy-Two was a dead habitat. Her father had taught her that before he taught her the planets. It had gone down fifty-six years earlier in a containment fire; its surviving population had been evacuated, its atmosphere reclaimed, its name removed from the distribution rolls. Children still used the number as a dare. Tell a joke in Seventy-Two and the vacuum laughs back.

She reran the forward check. The apparent excess returned to the opposite side of the ship. Reverse. Seventy-Two.

At the top of the screen a warning appeared: EQUIVALENT PHASE PATHS — REDUNDANT CANDIDATE.

She rejected the warning. It returned.

"You're talking to the wrong thing," said a voice above her.

Miro Jass leaned over the grating, carrying two paper cups of breakfast broth. As a medical orderly he had no reason to be on this side of the hull except that the vending machine in Bay Four sold broth with actual salt.

"That is a useful feature," Anja said. "Machines rarely take offense."

"Mine does. It says my clinical report lacks empathy."

"Does it?"

"It asks if the patient felt heard. The patient was unconscious."

He passed one cup through the opening. Anja took it with both hands. The broth smelled of fungus and onions, a merciful improvement over the metal tang in her mouth.

"You're pale," Miro said.

"Bad light."

"Then everybody on Deck Four is pale."

"Get me the last uncompressed acoustic sweep from the port ribs."

"That is not a medical request."

"It will be if we miss a planet."

Miro considered her expression. "I can get you the environmental copy. The other one belongs to Command."

"Then I only want the environmental copy."

"I'll pretend I believe that." He crouched. "Anja, it's nineteen days. Whatever you have, bring it up properly. Nobody wants a surprise during braking."

She nodded. Properly was a word with corridors attached to it.

After he went, Anja brought up the ship diagram. Eighty-one cells formed a modular spine: nine central machine cells with seventy-two habitable petals arranged in nested arcs. The peripheral cells had names on the public chart except for Seventy-Two, which wore a pale rectangle marked LOST. But the acoustic schematic beneath the public chart still assigned Seventy-Two a location, a radial interface, a pressure boundary and sixty-four registered subaddresses.

A nonexistent place had rather a lot of coordinates.

She requested its pressure history. ACCESS DENIED: ARCHIVAL EXCLUSION. She requested the histories of its adjacent seals. Both showed temperature, stress and vibration. The boundary to Seventy-Two showed a half-degree rise every six hours. Metal could warm from an adjacent compartment, but the timing was wrong. It had the long, uneven interval of a regulated greenhouse.

Anja disconnected her pad. A routine automatic upload would turn a candidate into an incident, and an incident into Command property before she understood what she was holding.

The ladder rattled. This time it was not Miro.

A slim man in gray transit uniform stood above the grate, his identification visible before his face. ORREN HOLT, RECORDS. Anja had seen his signature on calibration disputes for years and had never met him.

"Engineer Sorel," he said. "Why did you withdraw your balance report?"

"Because it isn't finished."

"You filed an anomaly."

"The test process filed an anomaly. I was the witness."

"That distinction," Holt said, "is why I'm here."

She climbed out, carrying the broth. Holt was taller than she expected, an old man with his sleeves carefully darned and a small ink stain on the side of his thumb. People who trusted automation seldom still got ink on themselves.

He showed her a message on a paper slip. TEST ORDER CONFLICT. PARENT STATE PRESENT. PROJECTION WITHHELD.

"The Witness flagged your second reading," he said.

"The interface calls it redundant."

"Those are different judgments."

"What is it seeing?"

"An attempted equivalence the older records don't permit."

"Why would anyone make that equivalence?"

He looked toward the concourse. A young father was carrying a little girl on his shoulders, her arrival ribbons brushing against the ceiling handrail.

"Because," Holt said, "at some point it was simpler to have one answer."

Anja folded the slip, though it was not hers. "Sixteen hundred tons is not a simple answer."

"No," Holt said. "And neither is where you found them."

She studied him. "You know about Seventy-Two."

"I know records were closed. I don't know whether the closing was truthful."

"There were evacuations."

"There were statements of evacuation."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because the acoustic event that sent me here is a child of a record signed fifty-six years ago. That record has no verified predecessor."

Above them the choir found the anthem's refrain, eighty imperfect voices pulling the same melody into a plausible harmony. Holt glanced at his watch.

"Whatever you do next," he said, "do it in the right order."

Then he left her beneath a banner that read WELCOME TO NACRE, while the ship beneath her feet carried a mass no one had agreed to count.

***

Anja returned to the bay that night. She had eaten dinner with the other technicians, said nothing about Seventy-Two, and watched a boy spill a whole bowl of lentils on a floor polished for arrival. People were alive in the most ordinary ways. She could not reconcile that with the thought that an entire compartment might be classed as vacuum.

She ran the reverse pulse again, this time without the administrative equivalence filter.

Three soft knocks came back through the structural rib.

There was no fourth.

She waited.

The answer came in the exact rhythm she had used on the test plate, except that someone had inserted a pause before the last beat.

For the first time in her engineering life, Anja reached toward the wall not to repair it but to touch a possible hand on the other side.

# Chapter 2: A Sound That Waits

At first Anja decided the answer was interference.

Interference was a friend. It relieved her of the obligation to call anything alive until she had eliminated a thousand more modest explanations. She could blame an auxiliary pump, a returning vibration from a hydraulic joint, even the choir if it had remained in rehearsal after curfew. Those were all things she knew how to test.

She rigged three probes to an independent clock and set them against the rib. At 01:06 she tapped a code she had made at the workbench: two quick strokes, a long rest, one soft stroke. The return gave it back exactly. She repeated it. Nothing happened. She reversed the order. A new signal arrived, seven evenly spaced clicks followed by two strokes close together.

It was not the echo she had sent.

She turned the test off and sat in the dark.

On the other side of the bulkhead, the ship's cargo air hummed through a service tunnel. She could hear the bearings of a nearby centrifugal pump—one of her old companions—dragging slightly on the third turn. Life continued above her. She wondered whether a person could reach adulthood in a hidden room without ever hearing rain.

She had seen footage of terrestrial rain. The ship had mist chambers, and Nacre was an ocean world, but Earth's uncontrolled weather had acquired the quality of an inherited family story. Her father had talked about it as if he had witnessed it himself, though he had been born aboard Meridian like everyone else she knew.

In the morning, Anja invited Kala Niren to breakfast on a pretext.

Kala was a xenobiologist attached to the arrival survey. Her formal job was to decide when the first landing crews could remove their helmets. Her informal job, in Anja's estimation, was to remind Command that a planet did not become a blank sheet because they had traveled far enough to reach it.

They met beside a greenhouse window where bean vines climbed wires to the overhead light.

"Suppose an acoustic interface returns a pattern that varies when you reverse the phase order," Anja said.

Kala lifted her spoon. "Suppose someone asks for scientific advice without giving me any data."

"Would it be possible?"

"Yes. Most of biology would be impossible to understand if order didn't matter. Touch a nerve and then apply a drug; apply the drug and then touch the nerve. The results needn't match. In a mineral lattice the possibilities are narrower, but still real."

"How narrow?"

"Show me the trace."

Anja passed her a folded sheet. She had printed it on an old drafting machine and cut away the location field. Kala scanned the waveforms, her spoon held still between them.

"This is a live system," Kala said.

"Why?"

"Not proof of life. Proof of a system with state. Something hears the first event and is different by the time the second arrives. See the recovery tail?"

"Could be a valve."

"Of course. I didn't say it wasn't. Where is it?"

"Can't tell you."

"Then I can't help you."

"Could you help me make a test that rules out ordinary valves?"

Kala considered her for a long moment. "I will give you the test, Anja. But don't turn a result into a person because you'd like it to be one. It's a serious mistake on a long voyage. We have already made too many assumptions about Nacre's oceans."

"You're doing those planetary readings again?"

"Twenty-two hours every day, apparently. The target committee wants a yes-or-no classification by the capture burn. Nacre is wet, warm in bands, and its atmosphere is breathable only after a great deal of indignity to our lungs. We have signs of structure in the tidal turbulence. No confirmed metabolism. The committee thinks that means unoccupied."

"Does it?"

"It means no confirmed metabolism."

She gave Anja a diagnostic protocol that tested response history with matched amplitudes, alternating waits and an independent control region. Anja followed it exactly. The result in the forbidden direction grew stronger while the response from the control region remained ordinary steel.

She transmitted an incident request to the ship's engineering desk. Without that step, she would have to explain why she had continued a private test on an arrival-critical system. With it, someone could stop her.

They did.

Commander Elian Sev called within twelve minutes. The image that appeared above Anja's desk showed a narrow face made severe by lack of sleep. Behind him, the command deck was full of bright preparations: orbital maps, pressure graphs, departure slogans turned around to read as arrivals.

"Engineer Sorel, your request proposes opening an archival exclusion boundary."

"It proposes confirming a vibration source."

"Inside a compartment evacuated fifty-six years ago."

"The boundary has measurable thermal activity, sir."

"So do abandoned reactors."

"Not with those response tails. The sequence is state-dependent."

Sev glanced down. "You're basing this on a difference between two diagnostics our navigation software has treated as equivalent for eight decades."

"That is why I need to examine the equivalence."

"We have seventeen days left to execute the first braking maneuver. Every interface changed now introduces new uncertainty."

"The uncertainty is already there."

"An engineer can say that about every bolt on the ship. Command is obliged to choose which uncertainties matter."

He did not threaten her. He explained the consequences of the work stoppage, the reserve margins, the risk to children sleeping beneath a reactor bay. He named people not as hostages but as the population he was responsible for transporting.

"Conduct all further investigation through Records and under operational quarantine," he said. "No direct access to Cell Seventy-Two. No public alarm. If your finding is real, it will survive two days of review."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then it wasn't ready to become an order."

The connection closed.

Anja stared at the blank panel. She disliked him less than she wanted to.

***

That evening Holt met her in the Records Annex, a room with a door that still shut on hinges rather than an iris. He had placed three ledger strips on the table. Each carried seventy-two small character positions, every one visibly occupied.

"The first is the before state," he said. "The second, the change. The third, the receipt. Together they define the recorded transition. You can compress their representation for search, but not discard the identity of which lane a character belonged to."

"And the old exclusion?"

"Two hundred sixteen positions are present. The predecessor they refer to is not."

"An orphan."

"A document whose parent has been hidden, destroyed or never created. We cannot tell which from this copy."

"Can you open it?"

"I can ask for archival access. I cannot make an absent parent exist."

"Then why is the Witness holding the projection?"

"Because your reverse test touches an unresolved ancestry. The display tries to tell you both orders are the same and the underlying record refuses to certify it."

Anja laid Kala's new trace beside the strips. Holt leaned forward, watching seven clicks become two.

"That's a message," he said.

"It could be."

"Do you have any idea what it says?"

"No."

Holt looked disappointed. "Good."

"Why good?"

"Because the worst thing we could do is decide we have understood an unheard witness."

Afterward Anja made the only promise she could defend. She would preserve every raw trace, distinguish everything observed from everything inferred, and take no action that could harm an unknown person on the other side of the wall.

By morning another signal had arrived. It began before her probe fired and ended precisely at the point she had meant to start.

For the first time the thing on the other side had stopped answering.

It was asking.

# Chapter 3: The Sealed Number

The Records Annex was older than the men and women who worked in it. It had once been designed for the possibility that the computers would all fail at once. The designers had not trusted lights, voices or magnetic memory to remain loyal for a hundred and sixty years, so they had included paper presses, photographic film and shelves deep enough to stand a person in.

Most of those shelves held replacement water filters now. The paper presses were used for graduation certificates and anniversary poems. The preserved manual ledgers slept behind a climate door on the lowest floor.

Holt opened that door for Anja with two keys.

"Why two?" she asked.

"Because there used to be a third."

The air inside smelled of machine oil and dry wood. A wall clock ticked over a table made of material imported from Earth. Anja had never felt wood that old beneath her fingers. It was scarred by cups, burns and knife marks, small human violations the wood had survived.

Holt spread an accident log before her. INCIDENT E-72. PRESSURE AND FIRE LOSS. Fifty-six years and four months earlier, during a solar-particle event, a ventilation-control failure had caused fire and rapid decompression. The entry listed three hundred fourteen dead, eight hundred sixty evacuated, and a permanent sealing order. The signatures belonged to the captain, the chief engineer and the civic recorder.

"That is what we teach," Anja said.

"Yes."

"Is any of it wrong?"

"The fire happened. Some people died. The rest is not one kind of statement."

He pointed to a field beside EVACUATED: COMPLETE. There was no destination list. No receiving shelter, no medical roll, no transit count, only the word COMPLETE.

"A completion judgment," Holt said, "should have children. An arrival manifest. A ration addition. A treatment register. People who go somewhere continue to make records."

"Maybe the archives were damaged."

"Some were. I searched the uncompressed copies held by thirteen independent departments. I found eighty-seven arrivals, all before the seal order. Not eight hundred sixty."

"Then the missing were killed."

"Possibly. But the ship's death ledger names three hundred fourteen. It doesn't name another seven hundred seventy-three."

He left the arithmetic between them. Anja thought of the mass model. Sixteen hundred tons included plants, water and steel. Human bodies were a small fraction of it. A society could be physically hidden behind a blank administrative category.

"Who closed the ledger?"

Holt's eyes moved to the signatures. "Your father was a junior engineer on the seal team."

Anja had known that. She had known it the way a person knows a place name from an old map. When she was eight her father had told her the doors were dangerous and that good engineers kept their hands away from dangerous doors. He had never described the people inside.

"His name is on the technical supplement," Holt said.

He opened the supplement. It contained pressure measurements, flame-front predictions and a recommendation to isolate the cell for six minutes. Six minutes. The seal had lasted fifty-six years.

Anja saw a note in handwriting she recognized from her father's small repair cards: TEMPORARY HOLD PENDING MANUAL ACCOUNTING.

There was no later accounting.

"Where did the six minutes go?" she asked.

"That's the question I've been trying to ask for twelve years."

He handed her another sheet. It was an appeal filed by someone named Sada Sorel, aged fourteen. The date was one day after the accident. The petition requested reopening of a family passage and independent census certification. Its final line read: I HAVE NOT RECEIVED MY MOTHER'S BODY. I HAVE NOT RECEIVED MY MOTHER'S VOICE. DO NOT SAY SHE IS ABSENT FROM THE SHIP.

Anja sat down.

"My mother."

"Yes."

"She never told me."

"That can be true and this can be true."

Anja stared at the name. Her mother had a way of arranging grief into domestic prohibitions. There were foods they never ate, songs she refused to sing, corridors she would walk miles to avoid. Anja had treated those refusals as habits acquired by someone who had never liked the world outside their household. She had not considered they might be directions on a map.

"What happened to the appeal?"

"Rejected as procedurally redundant. The evacuation had been certified complete."

The phrase made Anja feel cold despite the climate controls.

***

She found her mother tending mushrooms in Habitat Twenty. Sada had made a business of coaxing food from materials other people threw away: sawdust, bitter paper, the leaves clipped from municipal vegetables. She wore a green apron and old glasses repaired with copper wire. Seeing her inside the humid glow of the growing racks, Anja wondered how much of her life she had inherited without knowing the invoice.

Sada greeted her by asking whether she had eaten. Anja said yes. It was a lie too ordinary to count.

"I found your appeal," Anja said.

Sada's knife stopped halfway through a stem.

"Then you went looking."

"I was looking at the accident."

"That would bring you to me sooner or later."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Which part?"

"The part where your mother was trapped behind a door that everyone says leads to vacuum."

Sada lowered the knife. "You were my daughter. Not a public hearing."

"I've been an engineer on the same ship for nineteen years. I work three cells away."

"You have been an engineer. That made me more afraid, not less."

"Afraid of what?"

"That you would open something because the numbers told you it ought to be open."

Anja looked at the mushrooms. Tiny pale heads pressed up from old soil in a place where nothing had grown naturally for generations.

"Is there someone alive in there?"

Sada would not look up. The silence lasted long enough to become an answer, then long enough to become something more deliberate.

"I need you to understand," Sada said, "that the door was not always locked from this side."

"Then from which side?"

Sada covered the growing tray with a clean cloth. "If you found the appeal, find what was filed after it. Not in the public annex. In the technical rolls your father kept."

"Where?"

"Under the floor of our old kitchen. The panel with the ugly yellow screw."

Anja almost smiled. Her father had always refused to replace that screw, even when she brought home the right part from the workshop.

"Why make it so hard?"

"Because he couldn't make it easy without bringing Command to the same place. He had to decide which way to fail."

Before Anja left, Sada touched her arm.

"One more thing. If you reach the door, do not tell the people behind it that you're rescuing them."

"Why not?"

"Because they know the history of being rescued by us."

***

The box beneath the kitchen contained three brass fasteners, a cracked photograph and a narrow roll of copper ribbon. The photograph showed her father as a young man beside a group of exhausted workers. One girl stood in front of him, filthy with soot, clutching a younger child's hand. The girl's face was her mother's face before adulthood made it guarded.

On the back, in her father's careful script: WE HAVE NOT FINISHED COUNTING.

Anja unrolled the ribbon.

Numbers had been hammered into it by hand, not in a uniform machine typeface but at different depths, some almost invisible. Beside each number was a single word. Alive. Born. Dead. Alive. Born. Alive.

The entries continued through fifty-six years of ship time.

When she reached the last line her vision blurred. The date was from the previous morning.

Alive: 3,108.

The uncounted had been sending evidence all the time.

Someone had been receiving it.

# Chapter 4: Orbital Window

Anja had expected the first thing Commander Sev would do was arrest her. Instead he offered her a chair and a cup of coffee.

There was something intolerable about the courtesy. A guard stood beside the exit and another by the viewport, but both were pretending to examine weather reports. The command deck made the planet look like a promise: Nacre's blue crescent hovered above the navigation table, pale continental shelves showing through its shallow seas. Seventeen days remained before the first capture maneuver. Anja could see a counter at the edge of every screen.

"Your work is excellent," Sev said.

She left the coffee untouched. "Then approve an inspection."

"I approved an independent acoustic survey."

"You barred inspection of the source."

"The source is a sealed catastrophe area, outside the present life-support network."

"Except its boundary is warmer than a sealed catastrophe area should be."

"That does not make it safe."

He sat, careful not to put himself between her and the door. Sev had the restrained physical grace of someone who never wasted a gesture. His family had maintained the ship's central reaction wheels for six generations. He was no aristocrat born to command; the position had been bestowed on him by a legislature of neighbors, engineers and frightened practical people. Anja had voted for him nine years earlier, when he promised never to conceal maneuver risks.

"I am going to show you what concerns me," he said.

The navigation table changed. A path curved beneath Nacre's northern moon, cleared a capture arc by fractions of a degree, and settled into a temporary orbit. The difference between success and failure was so narrow that Anja felt it in her stomach.

"The engine banks have one full braking sequence left at certified efficiency. With margins, two short corrections. If the burn is late, we escape the system. If we overcorrect, the atmosphere takes us. We have fuel to survive either outcome only under models that account for mass and thrust."

"Which ours don't."

"We are investigating whether they don't."

"Sixteen hundred tons is a measurement."

"A discrepancy. It may be a sensor calibration artifact. It may be stored water in the wrong place. It may be a structural resonance no one has described adequately. Until confirmed, it is a candidate. You know the difference."

"Then let me confirm it."

He stood and placed the pad between them. "If Cell Seventy-Two is pressurized, opening the interface without understanding its occupants or atmosphere would be murderously reckless. If it is decompressed, we risk contaminating an occupied corridor. The archive's sealing order is still in force. I am not signing a demolition order because someone answered a knock."

"Then sign a protected inspection with no entry."

"You'll have eight hours. Holt will supervise. I want the witness stream sealed until we establish what it is."

Anja felt a little of the fight leave her. The decision was not the one she expected. It cost him something; she could see the officers studying their screens and pretending not to hear.

"What made you change your mind?"

"The mass problem. And your father's name."

"Did you know him?"

"He helped rebuild my mother's respiratory ward after the accident. He never took payment for it."

Anja looked down. Her father had never mentioned that either.

Sev's expression hardened. "I knew him as a man who understood consequences. Don't make me regret trusting you with the same."

***

In Engineering, Anja found her inspection authorization already attached to a map. Two adjacent pressure locks were available, with no internal hatch operations permitted. The outer corridor had not been entered since a maintenance robot passed through thirty-one years before. The robot's final report showed a failed thermal camera and a shadow whose dimensions had been classified as sensor corruption.

Miro arrived with insulated gloves, a medical kit and two packets of dried pears.

"You didn't ask me," he said before Anja could object. "But if a person is alive in there, an engineer is not enough. And if a person isn't alive, someone should help you cope with what you find."

"You assume I intend to enter."

"I assume you intend to learn something the map says you can't."

Holt came last, carrying a portable recorder that worked without a network. He was visibly unhappy to find Miro there.

"This is an evidence inspection."

"Then I'm evidence of the ship employing medics," Miro said.

Anja checked their suits. Atmospheric integrity, pressure transducer, tether length, supply capacity. She did not intend a heroic crossing into vacuum; she intended a slow approach to a wall, and she had a deep respect for how quickly slow approaches turned bad.

They walked past an unused transit line. Arrival posters had been pasted over the warning stripes, as if a change of scenery would persuade the ship to forget where the trains once ran. At the second bulkhead a child's drawing was visible beneath flaking paint. It showed seventy-two houses circling a yellow sun. One house had been scratched through, and someone had drawn a flower beside the scratch.

"How long ago?" Miro asked.

Holt shook his head. "We don't know."

At the final seal Anja attached the acoustic probes. She announced the operation into the recorder and placed her palm against the steel.

Three taps returned.

"That could be structural," Holt said, though his voice had changed.

Anja sent the matched pulse Kala had designed. The wall answered with seven clicks, then two together.

Anja spoke through the old emergency intercom. It had not been certified in decades. "This is Engineer Anja Sorel. We are outside Habitat Seventy-Two. We will not open the pressure boundary. If someone hears me, please answer with two knocks."

Silence.

Then a voice, barely audible beneath static: "Stop using the number."

The three of them looked at each other. The voice sounded young, impatient and thoroughly human.

Anja pressed the intercom. "What would you prefer?"

"Home," the voice said. "For a start."

No one laughed. The word had passed through fifty-six years of official vacuum and reached them with all its complications intact.

"I want to make sure no one is in danger," Anja said.

"We are in danger every time you remember us."

Holt closed his eyes.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"You first."

Anja had already named herself. She understood the demand only after a few seconds.

"I am a person who has never crossed this door," she said. "And I want to understand what happened on both sides of it."

There was a long pause. A metal latch clicked somewhere distant, followed by footsteps.

Then the voice said, "Bring your recorder. Not your commander."

# Chapter 5: The Door Marked Vacuum

The authorization allowed them to stand outside the door for eight hours. It did not allow the people inside to open it.

Anja read the order three times. The person beyond the intercom read it once and laughed with the sharp economy of someone who had learned to ration amusement.

"So you're allowed to approach us," the voice said, "but we're not allowed to approach you?"

"That is the wording."

"Is it also the thinking?"

"We're trying to change the thinking."

"That's usually the dangerous part."

The speaker's name was Tavi Merrow. She said she was seventeen, had never seen Nacre except as a colored point on a contraband chart, and was speaking through a line maintained by people who would lose their meal allotment if she wore out the coil. She had no difficulty putting Anja on the defensive.

"How many people are there?" Anja asked.

"How many people are where?"

"In your habitat."

"How many do you count as people?"

Miro stepped close. "I'm a medic. Is anybody hurt?"

"Every day," Tavi said. "Be specific."

"Anybody in immediate need of care?"

Tavi hesitated. It was the first unguarded thing she had done.

"Our oldest gardener can't breathe lying down. We have medicine for fluid in the lungs but not enough to keep treating her. The adults say we're saving the last course for when it becomes urgent."

"It sounds urgent now," Miro said.

"Then you can tell the adults."

"Can I speak with her?"

"Not by command channel."

"Can I bring a doctor?"

"Not by command channel."

Anja stared at the sealed hatch. All her training had been built around the hazards of crossing boundaries without permission. Now she understood permission was not a word with only one direction.

She withdrew her tool kit, switched off the powered probe, and placed the inspection order flat against the bulkhead camera so that anyone inside could see it. "We will not open this hatch. We won't transmit images from inside. If you wish, we can leave medicine in the outer service locker and have an independent witness watch us do it."

Holt made a small noise of protest. "The locker seal is listed as lost."

"Then a mechanical pass-through might need an engineering survey. Not an unauthorized opening."

"Anja," he said.

"Can you read that as a lawful inspection?"

He studied the order. "Barely. With an addendum that the transfer is at the request of the occupants."

"Ask them," she said.

Tavi disappeared from the line for eleven minutes. In that time the three of them waited beside the hatch and listened to the ship's ordinary voice. Anja heard the transit fans, an elevator belt and the barely perceptible deep pulse of the engine preparation cycle. The world outside the wall was busy becoming something else.

Tavi returned with an older woman named Jori and an offer. The medicine could be passed through a manual decontamination cassette one meter from the corridor floor. The cassette had been welded shut decades ago from the official side; the internal handle remained operable. If Anja broke the external weld without cutting the pressure shell, the women inside would open the cassette and take the medicine. Anja could inspect and film the weld, but she would not record faces or voices without consent.

Miro whispered, "That's a medical request."

Holt answered, "And an engineering defect report."

Anja filed both while everyone watched. The response from Command arrived after four minutes: REQUEST HELD PENDING SECURITY REVIEW.

Tavi was still on the line when the answer appeared.

"That's what your help sounds like," she said.

Miro knelt by the hatch. His voice changed, becoming the particular calm voice medics use when they are telling the truth without making the truth frightening.

"Jori, I need you to listen. If that woman has difficulty breathing lying down, she may not have four more minutes of certainty to spare. I can tell you what to do from here. Sit her upright. Check whether her ankles are swollen. If you have a pulse monitor, tell me her saturation."

Anja watched the security hold remain on the pad. The Witness, which validated technical transitions, had no jurisdiction over an emergency transfer. The hold came from human command. She could break the weld and accept the disciplinary consequence. Or she could keep her license and hear a woman gasp for air behind a legal definition.

She selected the manual medical emergency clause. It allowed removal of a non-pressure-bearing obstruction when the source of the obstruction was an unverified barrier to care. The clause had never been designed for a compartment removed from the census. It did not need to have been. Anja recorded her interpretation and the people it could affect.

"Are you asking us to transfer medicine?" she asked Tavi.

"Yes."

"Are you consenting to opening the cassette only?"

"Yes."

"Then we will open the cassette only."

She cut the weld.

The metal separated in a shower of harmless sparks. A square sleeve moved inward an inch, stopped, and shivered as someone on the other side worked a stiff handle. A narrow hand appeared through the opening. It was not a spectral hand or a sign sent by fate. It had a scar across the knuckle and a torn cuticle. It pulled Miro's medicine case into the dark.

Tavi said, "Thank you."

Then, almost at once: "Don't make this into a story about how kind you are."

Anja smiled despite everything. "I'll try not to."

***

Command suspended the inspection license before the medicine reached Jori's kitchen.

Sev called while Anja was removing the tool cable. His voice was controlled. "You overrode an operational hold."

"I acted under medical necessity with the request of the occupants."

"You presumed the occupants possessed authority to request it."

"They have bodies, Commander."

"They may possess authority, Engineer. But the status of that compartment has not been verified."

"If it takes verification to be alive, that is a problem with the register."

Sev went silent, not from shame, Anja thought, but because he was calculating the next hazard.

"The press will hear about this," he said.

"Not from us. We have their names and no consent to publish them."

"Their names?"

"Some. Enough."

He rubbed his forehead. "You have made an excluded area into a negotiation I cannot control."

"Perhaps it always was one."

"Come to the command deck at noon. You and Holt. Bring your evidence, not your conclusions."

Anja agreed and disconnected.

Holt was recording the wound in the weld. Miro had gone back to his ward with the difficult look of a man remembering other patients he had not been able to help.

A folded slip came back through the cassette before they left. Tavi had written on rough paper, in a schoolroom hand unlike any font Anja had seen:

**There are three thousand one hundred and eight of us. Some are ill. All are alive.**

Underneath was a second line.

**You can call us the people of Home. You cannot call us recovered cargo.**

Anja put the slip inside her suit, above the pocket where her father's old repair card had lived for years.

She did not feel triumphant. The hatch was still locked. The medicines would run out. A commander had reason to worry about the integrity of the entire ship. Behind the wall a girl had asked, clearly and for the first time in Anja's life, not to be turned into a number.

The simplest parts of engineering, she thought, were the ones that did not require the part to consent.

# Chapter 6: The People Behind the Number

On the sixth day of the investigation, Anja learned that a door can be opened without admitting anybody has crossed it.

Commander Sev authorized a controlled meeting in the old customs vestibule between Habitats Seventy-One and Seventy-Two. Its inner bulkhead had been sealed after the fire, but the vestibule's scrubbers and independent pressure gauges still worked. Each side could enter its own lock, and no air would pass between them until the mechanisms had been tested by both engineering staffs.

It was the first time Anja had thought to use the word staffs in connection with the people of Home. She wondered what else she had been unable to imagine simply because the ship's paperwork gave it no category.

Sev named Holt, Miro and Anja to the registered delegation. He placed two security officers beyond the outer lock. Home sent Tavi, Jori and a man who introduced himself as Nalin Deh, caretaker of the vestibule.

Nalin wore a coat whose fabric had been pieced from several generations of uniforms. The insignia had all been removed, leaving squares of unfaded cloth. His beard was braided in short, practical sections so that it would not catch in machinery. He was smaller than Anja expected, and he held his chin high with the peculiar dignity of people who have practiced being overlooked.

The glass between them was scratched. Anja could see Tavi clearly only when she leaned toward the center.

She was short, broad-shouldered, and wore her dark hair tied with a copper strip. She had a deliberate expression and a bruise under her left eye. Anja would later learn that the bruise came from a fallen irrigation pipe, not a romantic adventure. For now she registered the evidence as an injury without a story and forced herself not to invent one.

Miro spoke first. "How is the gardener?"

"Better," Jori said. "She slept flat for a few hours. It has been a long time since she could do that."

"Her name?" he asked.

"Elsa," Jori answered. "Elsa Bener. She was born here. Her mother was born here too."

Miro wrote it down. "I'll need a medical history."

"You can ask her," Jori said. "She's not a condition. She's a person with a history."

He looked genuinely chastened. "You're right."

Tavi stepped forward. "Are you going to put our names in the census now?"

"No," Holt said. "Not without an authority process and your consent."

"Are you going to send us through the cargo sorting tunnels?"

"No."

"Will you turn our power off if we don't?"

Sev's audio line, open from Command, crackled. "No such order has been issued."

Tavi looked toward the ceiling speaker. "That's not an answer."

Sev did not reply.

Nalin placed a rolled plan against the glass. "We have pressure seals, crops, hospitals, classrooms and a water treatment plant. Our people have built a working habitat out of a failure no one would help us repair. We need access to medicine and materials. We do not need a parade."

Anja matched the plan against her ship map. The handwritten additions were competent and precise. Several sections had been rerouted around a long scar from the original fire. The water system recaptured condensation from the hull instead of drawing on registered stores. The electrical supply came from leakage power and maintenance transfers that the ship's model called unallocated loss.

"Do you have an engineering authority?" she asked.

Nalin raised an eyebrow. "I am one."

"I meant—"

"I know what you meant."

She flushed. "I need to compare our safety plans. I'd like to do it engineer to engineer."

Nalin studied her for a moment. "Better."

"Your population contributes to ship mass," she said. "That changes our orbital braking calculation."

"Perhaps it changes the arithmetic of the people who were wrong about our population. We have always contributed to the ship's mass."

Tavi laughed outright. Anja felt the relief of being corrected by someone with a better sentence.

The discussion ran two hours. Tavi presented a ledger covering three generations of births and deaths. Holt asked about missing pages. Jori asked why a man who represented a system that had erased them thought it was the excluded people's obligation to be complete. Holt accepted the question as evidence of his own bad premise.

Sev joined by live image near the end. His face appeared above the delegation like a public monument.

"I can offer protected reentry into the common registry," he said. "Independent medical support, ration normalization, provisional transit rights, and a review of historical claims after orbital arrival."

Nalin listened. "Reentry into what?"

"The lawful civic body."

"We have never left it."

"The registry has treated you as absent."

"The registry is not the body."

Sev breathed out. "We cannot reconcile fifty-six years of disputed documents in sixteen days."

"Then you should have begun earlier," Jori said.

Anja saw that neither side could simply take the other's words as a grant of authority. They would have to build a shared record without allowing the state that had done the harm to be the sole interpreter.

Tavi leaned near the glass. "I have a condition."

"Name it," Sev said.

"Your people must stop saying we're being rescued. We grew crops, buried our dead, and kept children alive when your records called us vacuum. You can help. You can also stop hurting us. But you don't get to say we waited here to be found."

Sev stared at her. "I can agree to the wording."

"The wording is the work," Tavi said.

There was a silence long enough for the scrubber pumps to become audible.

Then Nalin folded his map. "We are prepared to share a witness chain. Not authority. Not yet."

Sev said he would consider it.

The meeting ended with the pressure boundary intact.

***

Anja remained in the vestibule after the others left. Tavi was on the far side, unrolling the copper braid from her hair. She seemed reluctant to end a conversation neither of them had begun on equal terms.

"Your mother came here once," Tavi said.

Anja looked up. "When?"

"Long before I was born. My grandmother used to tell a story about a girl named Sada who shouted at a locked door until the adults carried her away. She thought the people inside could hear her, even when the intercom was dead."

"They could?"

"Some of them." Tavi touched the glass. "My grandmother said there are different kinds of answer. One is what you hear. Another is what somebody does because you spoke."

"My mother never told me that story."

"Maybe she thought she had failed."

Anja touched the other side of the glass, palm to palm with the girl she had been taught could not exist. The surface was cold.

"Are you afraid of us?" Anja asked.

Tavi considered it seriously. "I am afraid of what your arrival celebrations will do to people who haven't been invited. I don't know if I'm afraid of you."

"What would make you less afraid?"

"A door that opens both ways," Tavi said. "And a rule that keeps it that way after you're gone."

When she turned away, Anja noticed the scarred steel beneath the glass, a scratch made by some long-ago hand. Three words had been carved with a blunt tool.

**WE ARE HERE.**

Anja left the vestibule without touching the inscription. It was already enough.

# Chapter 7: Bread in the Dark

Home smelled of bread.

Anja had expected the first crossing to be a procession through wreckage. She had dreamed of exposed conduits, ice in corridors and bodies moving in the dark with lamp beams for faces. Fifty-six years of official reports described Cell Seventy-Two as a hazard. She knew how stubbornly official descriptions could enter the nerves.

Instead a woman opened the inner vestibule and the smell of rising grain reached Anja before the room did.

The crossing had required six hours of negotiation and two teams of inspectors. Nalin had read the pressure certificate aloud, objected to a clause giving registered security indefinite emergency entry, and demanded that it be struck. Holt amended the permit without calling Command. The Witness accepted the version with both signatures as a bounded inspection record; it did not thereby certify the political agreement.

Anja entered with Miro and a portable instrument case. She wore ordinary work clothes. Nalin had insisted on that. "Suits make you look as if our air is an insult," he said. The pressure gauges were within tolerance. The air smelled of people, yeast and an unfamiliar vegetable soap.

The central corridor was too narrow for the population it served. Children ran along a strip of blue paint marking the safe side of a repaired floor. Farther down, an older woman carried a basket of vegetables beneath grow lamps that cast red shadows on her arms. The lamps had been salvaged from obsolete surgical theaters. Home had perfected the art of asking one piece of equipment to do three incompatible jobs without forgetting which job it had been designed for.

Nalin brought them to a public kitchen. Four heavy ovens had been built out of fire-brick, old ceramic insulation and panels stamped with the insignia of a factory that no longer existed. A baker stood at a table kneading dough with both forearms. He looked at Anja once, then returned to work.

"Are we supposed to have a tour?" Anja asked.

"You're supposed to assess safety," Nalin said. "But I thought you'd ask different questions after breakfast."

Tavi was there, seated on a low stool with a notebook on her knees. She had an expression of theatrical boredom.

"Visitors," she called to the baker, "are here to find out whether we can be people in a narrow room."

The baker lifted a doughy hand. "Tell them narrow rooms use less heating."

Someone laughed. A little boy with a gap in his front teeth asked whether the visitors had seen real stars. Miro said yes, and admitted that from inside the ship they looked mostly like pinholes. The boy found that disappointing.

Anja ate flatbread with a paste made from bitter algae and toasted seeds. It was better than the breakfast she had left unfinished in Habitat Twenty. She could not tell whether the taste was objectively better or whether her senses were rearranging the world because it contradicted the chart.

Home had made its own school, an infirmary, water purification terraces and a council elected from households and work crews. It had also made black markets, a faction opposed to any opening, a disciplinary committee with more authority than Nalin liked, and a dangerous habit of delaying repairs when doing them required asking the registered ship for materials.

"This panel was due for replacement thirteen years ago," Anja said, kneeling beside a buckled rib.

"Then you're late," said the technician working beside her.

His name was Daret. He was thirty-two and had been born two rooms down. He wore his hair shaved close so it would not catch in fans. He did not conceal his distrust.

"I can splice a new member in three shifts," Anja said.

"If you have steel."

"We have steel."

"You've always had steel. Do you have a way of giving it to us without taking something else?"

Anja touched the corroded seam. "I don't know."

"Then write that down. It's more useful than promising a bridge."

She did.

***

Miro spent the morning with Elsa Bener. She sat propped on a chair by a small window that looked into a hydroponic room, and she took his questions with dry impatience.

"Everybody asks whether I can breathe," she told him. "Nobody asks whether I can cook."

"Can you?"

"Of course. I don't want to. I've cooked for seventy-four years."

Miro laughed. "That is valuable clinical information."

"Write it exactly."

Anja met Tavi by the school after lunch. Seventy-two copper tags hung from a ceiling wire, one for each unit of a year-long study the children called the Crossing Calendar. The school had built its own world history from remembered transmissions. There was a large gap where news of Earth ought to have been. The registered ship had received records from the departing solar system for sixty years. Home had intercepted only fragments.

"We thought the old country was a place with blue roofs," Tavi said. "There was a diagram once. It showed the Earth from above, and the sea made everything blue. A teacher thought those were roofs."

"Did you believe it?"

"When I was little. Then I learned the difference between a map and what the map leaves out."

"That sounds like something an archivist would say."

"I am an archivist."

Anja glanced at her. "You said you were an apprentice."

"Is there a distinction in your records?"

"A certification."

"Is certification what makes a person able to remember?"

Anja did not answer. Tavi smiled slightly. She was not always trying to wound, Anja realized. Sometimes she was trying to find out whether the other person could stand a question.

They passed a wall mural showing Meridian surrounded by the stars, not arriving anywhere, simply traveling. Home's children had painted the hull as eighty-one bright rectangles. A little girl had drawn a face inside the rectangle everybody else had left blank.

"That's me," Tavi said.

"Why your face?"

"Because I was seven and the school had run out of blue paint."

For the first time Anja could picture fifty-six years of children making things rather than waiting.

***

Near the departure hatch Nalin stopped her. "There is something you should take back to your engineers."

He held out a jar filled with reddish dust.

"From the outer ribs," he said. "We have collected it for twenty years. Your calibration model calls it corrosion. Our plants respond to it. The filters grow warmer when the ship's long-range instruments listen toward Nacre."

Anja examined the dust. It was not crystalline at a glance, but under the lens it formed delicate branching clusters. It might have been a local electrostatic deposit. There were too many possible sources to call it anything else.

"Have you kept the dates?" she asked.

"Every collection. Tavi has the books."

At the final door Tavi asked a question with less armor than usual.

"Does your sky really look that blue?"

"Nacre?"

"Yes."

"Sometimes. Sometimes it's white, or gray, or almost black. We only see the part of it facing us."

"That's reassuring."

"Why?"

"Because everything I know of the outside comes in a picture someone else made. I was starting to think the planet might be incapable of changing its mind."

Anja stepped into the vestibule with the jar in her hand. Home's bread scent followed her as the pressure doors closed.

On the other side, the registered ship smelled of disinfectant and clean plastic.

She had always called that smell normal.

# Chapter 8: The Ledger of Copper

Tavi did not like being called brave.

A brave person was someone older people could send to a dangerous door and then praise afterward. She had watched the council use the word on three occasions: once after a pipe repair that cost a man his fingers, once when a woman volunteered to skip medicine for her granddaughter, and once when Tavi herself carried the census braid through a tunnel during a power outage. She had not enjoyed any of those events.

She preferred precise descriptions.

For fifty-six years, the people of Home had recorded every birth, death, marriage, adoption, expulsion, return and identity change on three carriers. The first was paper. The second was copper, scored with tiny marks at three fixed intervals. The third was a memory recitation performed in groups of three elders, none of whom was permitted to be alone with the final book.

The practice had begun when the official ship refused to acknowledge their requests for storage allocation. Home had no trusted upstream archive and, for years, no spare energy to maintain one. Copper was not indestructible. People died. Paper burned. The point of three carriers was that a lie would have to cross independent witnesses before it became an ancestor.

Tavi had inherited the third generation of the braid. It was heavy, a long wire wound around wooden spools. Her grandmother had made her practice its intervals by touch in the dark.

Now she brought the oldest spool to the vestibule, where Holt waited on the other side of the glass. Anja stood beside him with a scanner that could read scratches without treating them as numbers until a human transcriber confirmed the symbols.

"There's no absolute proof," Holt said after the first hour.

Tavi did not argue. She had learned there were two kinds of people who said such things: those who wanted the truth to have limits, and those who wanted the limits to become an excuse.

"No," she said. "There's not. What is it you can prove?"

"We can prove the copper isn't a recent print. We can prove the wear tracks cross the marks in a sequence consistent with use over decades. We can compare it with paper entries and physical records. We can test whether the material came from the same manufacturing stock used before the fire. We cannot prove a person didn't lie while making a scratch."

"Then let's find a scratch with something else behind it."

She pointed to an entry dated thirty-seven years earlier. It named a woman called Hena Merrow, who had borne twins during a failed pressure cycle. One twin had lived eleven hours; the other had survived. Tavi's grandmother had taught her that the dead child was still one of them because the census could not be made smaller merely by being late.

Holt found the date on an obsolete emergency power ledger. A thirty-two-minute current draw had passed through a nominally abandoned medical line on the same night.

"Coincidence?" he asked.

"Maybe," Tavi said. "Keep going."

At another entry, a pressure loss corresponded to a registered water anomaly. At a third, a long period of hunger corresponded to a shipment of emergency flour that had gone missing from a depot in Habitat Seventy-One. The records did not prove every story. They formed a landscape of relationships too costly and irregular to invent convincingly in a week.

Anja brought the jar of reddish dust. Tavi found twenty years of collection dates and laid them beside the ship's deep-space acoustic logs. The deposits peaked when the long-range array was aimed at Nacre, even at immense distance.

"Not impossible," Anja said. "We may have tuned our instruments at those times in a way that affects the hull."

"So we're contaminated by the act of listening?" Holt asked.

"We might be."

Tavi made a note. "That's a less comforting answer than the one I expected."

"Science often is," Anja said.

***

Holt showed her a white-paper excerpt from the vessel's original archival design. He explained the distinction between a fixed physical frame that carried eighty-one machine words and a separate long textual serialization that gave every state position a durable address. The numbers interested Tavi less than the principle.

"You can change how you show a thing without changing which thing it was," she said.

"Under the right transformations, yes."

"But if you throw away where each part came from, the thing isn't recoverable."

"Exactly."

Tavi examined the braid. "Then the trouble with the old evacuation report isn't only that it lied. It changed our relationship to every event afterward."

Holt nodded. "A bad parent poisons a family of conclusions."

She looked at him sharply. "Families aren't conclusions."

"No," he said at once. "Forgive me. I meant records."

She accepted the correction because he made it without bargaining.

In the afternoon Nalin asked her to return home for a council session. Some delegates worried that sharing the full braid would allow Command to assign responsibility for every unregistered medical transfer and black-market ration. Others worried that a public census would become an evacuation order. A third group, younger and angrier, argued that all records should be published immediately, including private names, because the registered ship had no claim to privacy after denying theirs.

Tavi listened and then asked whether any of them had requested permission from the people whose histories they intended to expose.

The room fell quiet.

"Do you suggest we leave the false history standing?" asked Lera, the assembly chair.

"No. I'm saying the dead can't consent and the living still can. We can publish totals, technical evidence, and the acts of office without sending every child's medical file to eighty thousand strangers."

"Then whose authority decides what is necessary?"

"Ours," Tavi said. "In a vote with records that everybody can challenge. You taught me that."

Nalin smiled, though his eyes remained tired. The council authorized a bounded release of verified technical data and a private medical exchange. It withheld household histories until each affected family could participate. Tavi had expected the adults to find a reason to refuse. Instead they gave her the task of organizing the testimony.

It was an annoying victory.

***

That night she unrolled the first spool alone in the archives room. Near the beginning was an entry no one had ever explained fully.

SADA SOREL. PETITIONER. OUTSIDE DOOR. REQUESTS ACKNOWLEDGMENT.

It had been made on the second day after the sealing. Beneath it someone had added: HEARD.

Home's oldest stories told of a girl who shouted through a sealed intercom until her voice broke. The adults had never told the girl they could hear her. They feared she would be punished for returning. They feared, too, that acknowledging her would require a response they were not ready to give.

Tavi touched the copper with her fingertip. So much of her life had been shaped by people who tried to protect one another through silence.

She wrote a new entry in the paper book.

**SADA SOREL'S DAUGHTER CAME TO THE DOOR. HER NAME IS ANJA. SHE DID NOT OPEN IT WITHOUT ASKING.**

She could not know how the story would end. An entry was not an ending. She recorded what had happened and left the next line blank.

# Chapter 9: The Nine Empty Cells

The original Meridian schematic was printed in a language no one on the current ship spoke fluently.

It was not a human language. The notation consisted of relation marks, ordered phase labels and a repeating rectangular array. Its first layer counted eighty-one structural cells. The outer seventy-two contained habitation and transit paths. Nine central cells held control systems that were called empty because they began each authenticated cycle without an assigned payload.

The blueprint lay on the floor of the engineering observatory, projected ten meters across so Anja could walk through it. Holt stood near the center with his hands behind his back. Kala watched from a chair beside the cooling console.

"The public display shows seventy-one habitable cells," Anja said. "The old diagram shows seventy-two."

"That alone doesn't prove the missing one is inhabited," Holt replied.

"No. It proves the map must preserve its address even if the contents change. The current admission planner actually maps its neighbors across the empty boundary, as though one of the cells ceased to exist."

"How?"

"An administrative adapter. It merges both phase orders before building the navigation state. If a response comes from Seventy-Two, the adapter assigns it to Seventy-One or the lower spine depending on which way we probe. That was my first reading."

Kala crouched beside the projected grid. "So the same physical thing appears in different places because of the order in which the system asks."

"Right. The carrier knows that the sequence matters. A reporting layer has been taught to forget it."

Holt tapped a square at the center. "And the nine?"

"They are not nine extra habitats. They're addressable control positions. Their payload can be at local zero, but the addresses still exist. It's the difference between an empty room and a room that has been removed from the building."

"Then why would the adapter conflate them?"

Anja summoned the revision record. It had been written forty-nine years earlier. A software committee had classified Seventy-Two as a permanently failed asset, removed it from the active routing domain, and compressed its diagnostic outputs into a neighboring channel. The committee's report praised the result for eliminating spurious alarms.

One signature belonged to a man whose face Anja remembered from her childhood. He had visited her parents on an autumn feast day and brought a carved wooden bird.

"Old maintenance engineer," she said. "He fixed the children’s bicycles."

"Perhaps he thought he was fixing the alarms," Holt said.

Kala rose. "Then your discovery may be a lossless data problem with lethal consequences. That's a particular kind of tragedy."

Anja knew the technical remedy. They could construct a shadow planner using the entire registered geometry, then compare every result with the inherited route. But proving a state correct and convincing a command officer to maneuver a ship by it were different jobs.

"Will the Witness admit the old map?" she asked Holt.

"The Witness can validate a candidate against the original schema, preserve the received lineage and tell us where an unlicensed adapter changed an address. It cannot choose to replace an active navigation plan."

"Good," Anja said.

Kala looked at her. "Why good?"

"Because the only thing worse than an engineer making a life-and-death decision without authority is a machine doing it and pretending authority came from its ability to calculate."

They spent six hours reconstructing the mapping. The canonical transport frame had eighty-one 64-bit words; the text state used a different fixed-length carrier of five thousand one hundred eighty-four character positions. They could view the same relationship as seventy-two by seventy-two or eighty-one by sixty-four, but the distinct carriers were not interchangeable physical objects. The mapping was an address language that prevented them from losing the origin of a reading.

At the end, the shadow planner returned the same anomalous mass estimate Anja had found in Bay Four. This time it assigned the mass to one exact habitat and its stored water, soil, air and people.

Seventy-Two.

Anja printed the result. She liked paper at moments when a computer could make its own work look inevitable.

***

She brought the new model to Sev, who listened without interruption. The commander had the decency to look frightened.

"If we add the mass," he said, "the burn window changes."

"Yes."

"And if you are wrong, changing the model may kill us."

"Yes."

"If we leave the model as it is and you are right, we may also die."

"That is the problem."

Sev crossed to the viewport. Nacre filled half the sky now. Its illuminated atmosphere looked soft enough to enter by hand. Anja tried to imagine standing in a valley beneath that light and decided the effort was a dangerous comfort.

"I have fourteen days," he said. "You have until the next navigation board. Produce an independent mass proof, not merely a plausible model. And produce a resource ledger. We have given you space to investigate. The ship cannot become a debate without a maneuver plan."

"Will you restore Cell Seventy-Two to the public map?"

"Not yet."

"They've given us names."

"Names are not engineering verification."

"Names are civic evidence."

"Then the civic body will have to decide what they mean."

Anja almost thanked him, then remembered he was still withholding something that belonged to its owners.

As she turned to leave he said, "Do not misunderstand me. I want those people alive. I also want the eighty-three thousand whose names are on my screens alive. I cannot let either wish destroy the other."

Anja studied the commander. He had never told her he enjoyed power. That made the power more difficult to challenge.

***

She returned to Habitat Twenty late. Her mother was sitting at the kitchen table, not waiting exactly, but not engaged in anything that might disguise waiting.

"I found the original map," Anja said.

Sada looked at the folded sheet. "Your father wanted you to."

"He could have told me."

"He wanted to. Every year he said he would, then thought of what Command would do if you believed him."

"Did you agree?"

"No."

"Did you stop him?"

Sada watched the table for a while. "I didn't stop him. I let him carry a fear I shared because I was angry with him for being afraid."

Anja placed the map between them. "I can correct the model."

"Can you correct the years?"

"No."

Sada traced the outline of the seventy-second cell without touching the paper.

"Then begin with what you can repair," she said, "and don't call the rest of it erased."

# Chapter 10: The Cost of an Address

Two kinds of power ran through the ship. One moved air and water. The other decided who had a right to them.

Anja learned the second kind could be measured too, though no instrument in her kit had been designed for it.

She was standing in the Distribution Office when a clerk showed her the consequence of adding Home's population to the common register. The system accepted the total, calculated a revised daily allowance, and produced a list of necessary transfers. Habitat Seventy-Two would receive medical oxygen, dietary supplements and water permits. Twelve registered neighborhoods would immediately lose reserve allotments. Habitat Forty, which housed children with respiratory disorders, would lose the protected oxygen buffer intended for its annual infection season.

"That can't be right," Anja said.

The clerk, a tired man named Peren, shifted a cup of cold tea aside. "It is right under the current distribution rule. Every new recipient is covered by the existing pool. If you add recipients, the per-person reserve falls."

"These people weren't newly born yesterday. They already use air."

"They use air outside the measured network."

"But the ship carries that air whether you count it or not."

"Yes. The system's common capacity estimate is wrong, and its existing promises are already overcommitted."

Anja stared at the transfer list. Her discovery had not created a new demand. It had exposed a deficit disguised as generosity to a smaller population.

"Can we correct the model before applying the cuts?"

"Not under an automatic census admission. The registered population is the control parameter. Full reconstruction requires a deliberative resource order."

"Then don't use automatic admission."

Peren spread his hands. "I don't make the rules."

"You understand them."

He looked at her for a long time, then pulled a chair over.

They spent the afternoon discovering how many reports were children of the absent category. The unused power assigned to a lost cell had been sold as margin. Its condensation capture had been counted as central-system efficiency. Its food production had appeared as a reduction in shipwide consumption. Medical models had treated missing deaths as proof of declining disease. A fiction had become a resource plan, and a resource plan had become a source of comfort for people who would now feel harmed by its correction.

Anja left with a packet of calculations that would have made a worse headline than a thousand secretly living people. It meant somebody would have to give something up.

***

Home's council was divided. The elders wanted recognition and protected autonomy. A younger faction wanted immediate common ration rights and open transit. Others insisted the outer door should be welded shut again before the registered ship could relocate them to a safer but supervised compartment.

Tavi stood in front of an old heating panel and read the Distribution Office's projected cuts. She had expected fury. What she heard was fear.

"If we take the medicine, children outside will lose air," said a teacher named Bel.

"No," Tavi replied. "If the distribution policy continues to pretend our consumption does not exist, the office will transfer shortages onto visible people. That isn't the same thing as us taking their air."

"Are you sure they will understand the distinction?"

"No. I'm not sure we do."

Nalin asked whether the entire ship could share the discrepancy through a small temporary reduction instead of concentrating losses in a dozen neighborhoods. Daret said they could increase Home's condensate output by modifying an old heat exchanger, but doing so risked exposing a water conduit with failing seals.

"We don't have a free solution," he said.

"Then we have a choice," Tavi replied. "It might be the first one we've ever been allowed to participate in."

She did not win the vote. The council postponed registration for forty-eight hours while requesting a resource reconstruction. She hated the delay. Then she helped deliver bread to three elderly neighbors who could not leave their rooms, and found herself grateful the meeting had ended without anyone being ordered out.

***

Anja visited Miro in Habitat Eleven that night. He was sorting emergency medical stock under a lamp too dim for the work.

"I learned something today," she told him. "A census can injure people even when it's truthful."

"Not the census," Miro said. "The rule that acts on it without asking what was actually there."

"Patients will lose reserve oxygen if we register Home."

"Then don't let that happen."

"I have no authority to rewrite allocation law."

"Neither do I. But I can sign a clinical risk assessment. So can every ward head. We can demand that the transfers stop until they account for the hidden production they also excluded."

"You make it sound simple."

"It isn't. I've already had three patients ask why I'm giving supplies to people who never paid into the common system."

"What did you say?"

"I asked when they began paying for the air they were born into."

Anja smiled. Miro closed the drawer.

"Don't romanticize this, Anja. Registered families are frightened for their children too. Some of them will listen, some won't, and some will hurt people before they learn the difference. You'll need a better plan than being right."

Outside the infirmary, arrival bells rang the end of another ship day. Fourteen days remained. Anja walked home with the packet of calculations beneath her arm and realized there was no version of the truth that would spare everyone discomfort.

She could only refuse the version that spared the decision-makers.

# Chapter 11: The Original Closing

Sada Sorel told the story with her hands wrapped around a cup of water.

Anja had asked for the account on three evenings before her mother agreed. On the fourth she arrived with no recorder, no notes and no demand that anything be confessed in a useful order. They sat at the same kitchen table where Anja had learned to solve simple circuit problems.

"There was a radiation alarm," Sada began. "Everyone remembers the fire, but the alarm came first. A solar storm was expected. We were told to close vents and shelter near the inner ribs. My mother worked in the Seventy-Two clinic. She stayed to help the children move."

Sada had been fourteen. She recalled the taste of the emergency breakfast, an awful sweet paste served in foil. She recalled a man from Water Maintenance promising to return with masks. She recalled her older brother carrying a girl with burned arms down the central hall. Memory had kept the small details precise while eroding the sequence between them.

The fire started in an oxygen-scrubbing substation. It crossed three ventilation trunks before the automated shutters closed. A pressure front threatened adjacent habitats. The captain ordered a six-minute isolation while crews moved survivors through the emergency passage. The local crew disagreed about whether venting would carry flame away or send it into a medical ward.

"There was an argument," Sada said. "I didn't hear the words. I remember the sound of men's boots running."

"My father was there?"

"Yes. He was eighteen and had only begun working in engineering. He helped turn the mechanical wheel. The shutter dropped. Everybody thought it would open after the pressure equalized."

The fire was suppressed in eleven minutes. The radiation alarm lasted seven hours. The local habitat, cut off from the common circulation loop, had independent life support. It had been designed that way for exactly this kind of event. The surviving crew kept its own oxygen and water until the main system could be repaired.

"Then why didn't they reopen?" Anja asked.

"Because the pressure readings were terrible. Some instruments were burned. Command didn't know what gases were inside. There were wounded people on both sides. The doctors ordered isolation and the captain signed a temporary quarantine."

That, Sada said, was the part she had always been able to forgive. People had been afraid and incomplete information could kill.

Then the quarantine acquired a new owner. Emergency authority became budget authority. The medical department wanted to move supplies into the unaffected wards. The security office wanted a single controlled breach, then no breach at all. A civic committee declared all remaining residents either evacuated or presumed dead because the alternative required a costly long-term quarantine and a second set of ration ledgers.

"My mother was inside," Sada said. "I knew she was. I heard her on the intercom on the third day. She told me to stop shouting because my throat sounded sore. Then someone disconnected the line."

"Who?"

"One of the security officers. He told me the signal was an audio loop."

"Was it?"

"No. She said a thing about our kitchen only she knew. The officer didn't care. He was doing what he'd been told."

Sada set down her cup.

"Your father helped me file an appeal. He kept writing them after we were married. At first he thought the truth would be enough. Later he learned the truth had to pass through every office that benefited from a contrary version. He came to the wall at night. He made small repairs outside the seal. The men and women inside made repairs too. Slowly, quietly, they arranged a way to pass paper and medicine and some power without making a public record."

Anja swallowed. "So you knew people were alive?"

"Some, at first. Then names. There were times we lost contact for years. We didn't know how many remained. We told ourselves that admitting the whole truth would cause the commander to evacuate the cell by force. It became a story we used to explain the silence that frightened us."

"Did you ever see your mother again?"

Sada shook her head. "No."

"Did she die inside?"

"Yes. The people inside sent me a note. She died of an infection six years after the fire. There might have been medicine in the common wards. I don't know if it would have saved her."

"Why didn't you tell me any of this when I joined Engineering?"

"Because I was ashamed. And because the lie had grown so large that truth looked like a new danger every time I approached it."

Anja wanted to be angry with her. She was angry. But she had no clear shape for the anger. Her mother had been a child when the door first closed and an adult during all the years she had allowed it to remain closed. Those were both true.

"Your father died believing you would fix the ship," Sada said.

"Did he think I would find Home?"

"He hoped you wouldn't have to. He was wrong to think that would absolve us."

***

Later Sada brought out a recording wafer hidden inside a sewing machine. It contained six minutes of distorted voices from the first day after the fire. Anja heard alarms, people coughing, somebody crying out for water. Then her father's young voice, shaking but intelligible: "Temporary isolation. We will open again when the pressure is safe."

Another voice asked, "Who has authority to decide it's safe?"

No one answered in the recording.

Anja replayed that silence until it became unbearable.

The question had never been resolved. The seal could have been opened by the people inside, but they could not certify the common system. The people outside held the key and refused to acknowledge the living residents as parties to the decision. A technical emergency had become a constitutional trap.

Sada had left the room when Anja finally switched the wafer off. On the kitchen counter lay a loaf wrapped in cloth and a note in her mother's uneven handwriting:

**I can't ask you to forgive me. I can ask you to stop me if I try to hide again.**

Anja placed the note in her pocket beside the folded slip from Tavi. She had spent years thinking the story of her parents was one of absence and reticence. Now she saw a more difficult inheritance: two people who had loved each other while allowing fear to do work that love should have forbidden.

She did not know whether to forgive them.

She did know the door would not stay closed.

# Chapter 12: Terms of Witness

The agreement was signed in a room without any official flag.

Nalin had insisted on the location. The meeting took place in a maintenance classroom where neither side owned the walls, and where the chalkboard still carried a lesson on coolant pressure from the previous week. The table was borrowed from the ship's agricultural union. Two people from Home and two from the registered habitats inspected it for hidden equipment before the talks began, an absurd procedure that made everyone feel a little better.

Anja sat between Holt and Tavi. Across from them Commander Sev had brought Peren from Distribution, Miro from Medicine and a civilian legal adviser named Ressa An.

The offer was narrow. Home would release raw mass and resource data, engineering diagrams, and the nonprivate parts of its lineage records to a jointly witnessed technical team. The registered ship would recognize Home's residents as persons for urgent medical care, food safety, and emergency transit while an independent body assessed full civic participation. Neither side would receive unilateral control over the other's internal systems.

"You're making our existence provisional," Tavi said.

Ressa shook her head. "Your right to emergency care is unconditional. It's the mechanism of civic admission we're drafting."

"Then say that plainly."

Ressa changed the wording.

Sev wanted exclusive custody of the shared logs until the navigation board confirmed their integrity. Holt refused. "That would recreate the failure we're investigating," he said. "One institution would both hold the evidence and define what counts as evidence."

"We need to avoid panic," Sev said.

"Then tell the ship what is known, what is unknown and what action is authorized," Holt replied. "Don't replace the problem with a performance of certainty."

Nalin proposed parallel evidence stores. The first would remain inside Home. The second would be held by Records in a protected civic archive. The third would be a cryptographically witnessed summary available to anyone aboard, including Command. Private medical and household records would remain private unless their owners consented or a narrowly defined emergency required access.

The arrangement took two hours to draft and another hour to read back. Anja found herself moved by the way the adults took care with verbs. Provide. Retain. Decline. Challenge. Suspend. They were trying to build a door out of words before making another one out of steel.

Then Ressa reached the clause on dissemination.

"All jointly confirmed technical findings will be published in full within twenty-four hours of admission," she read.

Sev stiffened. "That is too fast."

"It is fast enough to matter," Tavi said.

"We may need to change the capture plan. Dissemination before the board's review could provoke a reaction that makes the ship less safe."

"Sev," Anja said, "the missing mass changes the plan whether people hear about it or not."

"People react to information," he replied. "Reactions have mass, pressure and consequences too."

Anja could not dispute that. She offered a safety annotation: publish raw findings with explicit candidate status, delay any operational instruction until the appropriate admission authority acted, and forbid the use of public evidence as a command to open pressure boundaries. Sev agreed to that. He did not agree to let the population see the navigational consequences before his board.

Tavi placed her hand on the paper. "Then we have no agreement."

The room went quiet.

Sev looked at her, not as a child who could be told to behave but as an adversary who had found a decisive pressure point.

"You are asking me to risk the maneuver," he said.

"I'm asking you not to hide the risk from the people who will bear it."

"There isn't time to teach eighty thousand people orbital mechanics."

"They don't all have to agree on the equation," Tavi said. "They have to know someone is choosing on their behalf."

Ressa proposed a compromise: publish the discrepancy, the methods, the independent reviewer names and the range of operational consequences immediately after the technical team confirmed them. The final maneuver options would go to the navigation board and then a special civic assembly within forty-eight hours. Emergency action remained available but required a contemporaneous written justification and independent retrospective review.

Sev looked toward the window. Nacre's crescent had become a broad white arc.

"Forty-eight hours," he said. "And no unauthorized publicity before the certification."

Nalin asked Tavi if the condition was acceptable. She disliked how close it was to the commander's original demand. Still, the timetable was now written and jointly enforceable.

"Yes," she said. "With the amendment that our council receives the same report at the same time."

Ressa wrote it.

***

When the agreement was complete, Holt placed three copies on the table and began the careful process of making each one refer to the same original text. He explained that the compact receipt did not itself establish whether the treaty was fair. It established which people had signed which exact terms and in what sequence. The distinction mattered: an authentic injustice was still an injustice.

Miro gave Tavi a package of medical supplies with her council's permission. She took it without ceremony.

"Thank you," she said. "Elsa will be pleased. She already thinks your first visit was too full of doctors."

"That's a kind of good news I rarely receive," he answered.

Anja watched Sev sign last. His pen hovered over the line, then descended.

She had expected triumph. Instead she felt the gravity of the next problem gathering in the room. They had constructed a means to discover more truth, and truth on a ship preparing for a capture burn was not a passive object. It would change schedules, supplies and people who had imagined those things belonged to them.

Tavi walked beside her to the transit junction.

"Do you trust him?" she asked.

"Sev? I trust him to believe he's saving us."

"That's not what I meant."

"No. I don't trust his judgment to include people he's never had to count."

"Do you trust yours?"

Anja thought about the first acoustic report she had almost let a convenience filter erase.

"Not without witnesses," she said.

Tavi nodded. "That's probably the best answer anybody could give."

Before they parted, the two women stood in the doorway of the shuttle and watched the ship's service lights recede down the spine. Somewhere ahead, beyond steel and synthetic air, lay Nacre's ocean, making waves beneath a foreign moon. Neither woman knew what those waves meant.

For the first time, Anja had agreed in writing not to decide alone.

The next morning Kala sent her a new signal trace.

It had the same ordered signature as the wall in Seventy-Two.

It came from the planet.
