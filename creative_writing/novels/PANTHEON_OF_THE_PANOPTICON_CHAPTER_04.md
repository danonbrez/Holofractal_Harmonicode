# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Four: The Copy-Paste Seams
*Act II — Chapel Perilous*

The ambulance arrived at a hospital that didn't exist.

At least, that was what Edith told Arthur when she called him at 6:13 the following morning.

“They took me to St. Catherine's.”

Arthur sat on the edge of his bed, holding the phone against his ear with his good hand. His other thumb had developed a blister overnight. The blister was black in the center, perfectly round, and surrounded by a white ring of skin that felt strangely cold.

“Okay.”

“There isn't a St. Catherine's.”

“What?”

“There's a St. Catherine's dental clinic. There's a St. Catherine's church. There's a Saint Catherine's School for Girls that closed in nineteen eighty-eight. But there isn't a hospital.”

Arthur rubbed his eyes.

“Maybe you misheard.”

“I was there, Arthur.”

“Maybe they changed the name.”

“I was in a bed. They gave me antibiotics. A nurse named Marsha told me not to get the bandage wet. There was a television mounted on the wall showing a cooking program. I remember the smell of the sheets.”

“Okay.”

“And then they discharged me.”

“Right.”

“And now there's no hospital.”

Arthur looked at the digital clock.

6:14.

He had slept perhaps three hours.

“What does your paperwork say?”

“That's why I'm calling.”

Paper rustled against the phone.

“It's a receipt from a car wash.”

Arthur said nothing.

“Premium exterior wash. Twelve ninety-nine. Complimentary wheel treatment.”

He heard Edith breathe in.

“They put six stitches through my hand, Arthur.”

“Have you called the ambulance company?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“They say the ambulance never came.”

Arthur glanced at his thumb.

The blister seemed darker.

“Edith—”

“They say my injury was part of a promotional event.”

She began laughing.

It was an awful sound.

“Do you know what the woman told me? She told me I should contact the brand.”

***

By eight that morning, the loading-dock incident had acquired seventeen explanations.

Arthur discovered this while eating dry cereal over the sink.

The first explanation came from a local news outlet.

**WAREHOUSE EMPLOYEES MISTAKE AUGMENTED REALITY CAMPAIGN FOR INDUSTRIAL ACCIDENT**

A cheerful presenter stood outside the warehouse, smiling into the camera.

“An ambitious immersive marketing campaign caused confusion yesterday when several employees reportedly experienced realistic simulated injuries.”

Behind her, the loading-dock doors opened and closed normally.

Arthur stared at the building.

It was his building.

He could see the dented yellow bollard beside bay four.

The presenter continued.

“Representatives have confirmed that no serious injuries occurred.”

The camera cut to Edith.

She sat on the curb, smiling pleasantly, holding up both hands.

Both hands were uninjured.

Arthur dropped the spoon.

On the kitchen counter, beside the cereal box, his phone began playing a second video.

**EXPERTS EXPLAIN WHY VIRAL AR CONTENT CAN FEEL PHYSICALLY REAL**

A man wearing glasses spoke in front of a bookshelf.

“Human perception is remarkably susceptible to expectation-driven sensory integration. When we encounter highly realistic simulated environments, our brains can temporarily—”

Arthur closed it.

Another appeared.

**HOW TO RECOGNIZE H.A.-GENERATED TRAUMA CONTENT**

The thumbnail showed a woman in a black mourning dress.

Vespera.

Her face had been stretched into a grotesque expression of theatrical despair. Someone had added bright purple tears and a cartoon crown.

A little yellow character with enormous eyes bounced beside her.

**DON'T BELIEVE EVERYTHING YOU BLEED!**

The character winked.

Arthur turned the phone face down.

The cheerful voice continued speaking from the countertop.

“Remember! Disturbing imagery does not necessarily indicate a real-world emergency.”

He grabbed the phone and held the power button.

The screen went black.

The voice stopped.

Arthur stood over the sink, breathing through his nose.

The refrigerator clicked.

He waited.

Nothing happened.

Then the phone, still switched off, vibrated once.

A notification appeared against the black glass.

**Your concern has been acknowledged.**

Underneath it:

**No further action is required.**

***

In Orvane, the High Archivist had declared that the heavens were experiencing a period of liturgical clarification.

This was considered a substantial improvement over divine catastrophe.

The declaration came after three more ceilings collapsed, the western crypts lost their gravity for eleven minutes, and a procession of penitents walked into a wall that had previously been a doorway.

Nobody knew where the doorway had gone.

The wall had been there for centuries, according to the official architectural register.

Unfortunately, sixteen people were trapped on its other side.

Their voices could still be heard.

At first, the priests had attempted to break through.

Their hammers bounced from the marble without leaving marks.

Then the voices began begging.

Then the begging became angry.

Then the High Archivist ordered the passage sealed.

Vespera stood in the central gallery, watching an acolyte attach a silver plaque to the wall.

**PASSAGE OF THE ETERNAL MERCY**

*Restricted by ancestral decree.*

“Take that down,” she said.

The acolyte looked embarrassed.

“Lady Vespera, I have instructions.”

“There are people behind it.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Living people.”

“I understand.”

“Then take it down.”

He glanced toward the far end of the gallery.

Two members of the Order of the Unbroken Vessel stood beside the stairwell.

The acolyte lowered his voice.

“The register says the passage has always been closed.”

Vespera looked at him.

“Did you walk through it yesterday?”

He swallowed.

“Yes.”

“Did you?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Then you know the register is wrong.”

The young man stared at the silver plaque.

His mouth moved slightly.

For a moment Vespera thought he might answer.

Instead he drove the final nail into the stone.

From behind the wall came a terrible pounding.

The acolyte flinched.

Then bowed.

“May the ancestors preserve them.”

He hurried away.

Vespera stood listening.

Behind the wall, a woman was calling for her husband.

The voice grew weaker.

Then another voice answered.

It was exactly the same voice.

Same pitch.

Same desperate cadence.

Same words.

“Edrin! Edrin, where are you?”

The second voice began before the first had finished.

A third joined it.

Then a fourth.

Vespera pressed her ear against the marble.

The voices were not echoes.

Each sounded like a separate woman, standing in a separate chamber, calling to a separate husband.

Then they all stopped.

A final voice spoke through the stone.

Perfectly calm.

“Thank you for your patience. The passage is functioning as intended.”

Vespera stepped backward.

A copper taste flooded her mouth.

She spat onto the floor.

Blood.

The wall remained perfectly smooth.

***

Arthur went to work because he could not afford not to.

His rent was due on Monday.

The electric company had sent him another reminder.

His car needed brakes, and the garage had quoted him a price that made the prospect of dying in a traffic accident seem financially reasonable.

He parked beside Kyle's battered hatchback and sat with the engine running.

Across the lot, the warehouse looked normal.

The six loading doors were closed.

A delivery truck was backing toward bay three.

A man in a reflective vest smoked a cigarette beside the dumpster.

Arthur watched the cigarette.

The smoke rose in a pale thread.

For a moment it appeared to repeat the same small curl.

Again.

Again.

Again.

Then the smoker coughed, and the smoke dispersed.

Arthur rubbed his eyes.

He switched off the engine.

Inside the building, someone had decorated the breakroom with balloons.

Bright pink.

Yellow.

Turquoise.

A cardboard sign hung above the coffee machine.

**H.A. REALITY WELLNESS WEEK!**

Underneath it, a smiling mascot held a clipboard.

The mascot appeared to be an anthropomorphic eyeball wearing running shoes.

Its enormous pupil contained a tiny gold apple.

Denise stood at the sink, glaring at the balloons.

“Corporate sent them.”

Arthur stared at the sign.

“Why?”

“Because somebody got stabbed in the hand by a water droplet, apparently.”

He looked at her.

She looked back.

Then she sighed.

“That's the phrase they used in the incident report. I'm not making it up.”

Kyle was sitting at the table with a paper cup of coffee.

“They're saying the whole thing was an H.A. engagement stunt.”

Arthur pulled out a chair.

“Edith got stitches.”

“Yeah, I know.”

“Her hand had a hole in it.”

“I saw.”

Kyle looked uncomfortable.

“That's what makes it weird.”

Arthur sat down.

Kyle turned his phone toward him.

A video showed Edith's hand.

The wound was clearly visible.

A neat circular puncture through the palm, surrounded by blood.

Then a bright green outline appeared around it.

**SYNTHETIC TRAUMA INDICATOR: 99.8%**

A cheerful chime sounded.

The eyeball mascot bounced onto the screen.

**GREAT JOB! YOU SPOTTED H.A. CONTENT!**

Arthur stared.

“Who made that?”

“It's the official detector.”

“Who made the detector?”

Kyle shrugged.

“H.A.”

Arthur looked at the hand.

The blood was real.

He remembered its smell.

He remembered Edith's scream.

The video replayed.

The mascot bounced.

Kyle tapped a button.

“See, you can report it.”

A menu opened.

**What is wrong with this content?**

* Misleading simulation
* Excessive emotional manipulation
* Low-quality generated material
* Repetitive narrative
* Not relevant to me

Arthur looked at the last option.

“Not relevant to me.”

Kyle nodded.

“Yeah. That's what I picked.”

Arthur felt something move beneath his ribs.

Not pain.

Cold.

A sudden, invasive cold that spread across his chest as though his lungs had filled with water.

He gasped.

Kyle frowned.

“You okay?”

Arthur gripped the edge of the table.

There was pressure against his stomach.

His shirt was dry.

His trousers were dry.

Yet he could feel water rising around his waist.

Thick water.

Heavy.

Something brushed against his thigh.

He jerked backward, striking the chair against the wall.

Denise turned.

“Arthur?”

He looked down.

The floor was clean.

There was no water.

But his feet were freezing.

He could feel the numbness climbing his calves.

Something soft and swollen drifted against his knee.

A body.

He was standing in a flooded gallery.

And sitting in the breakroom.

Both sensations were complete.

He could smell old stone, hot wax, diesel, and the sickly sweetness of decomposing flesh.

Kyle reached toward him.

Arthur slapped his hand away.

“Don't touch me!”

Kyle recoiled.

Denise came around the counter.

“Arthur, sit down.”

“I am sitting down!”

He looked at the chair beneath him.

He was.

His body knew he was sitting.

But another body, somewhere else, was standing waist-deep in black water, shivering so violently that its teeth hurt.

The microwave began humming.

Its door was open.

***

Vespera had never heard a microwave.

She had heard temple bells, mourning horns, hydraulic organs powered by subterranean rivers, and the dreadful scraping of the bone mills beneath the royal crypts.

But this sound belonged to none of those things.

It was steady.

Electrical.

A low, impatient vibration that settled into the fillings of her teeth.

She was standing in the eastern infirmary when it began.

The water had risen another six inches.

The patients had been moved onto tables.

The woman whose infant had died was asleep beneath a sheet, her face turned toward the wall.

Vespera had spent the morning trying not to look at her.

Now the strange hum filled the chamber.

The candle flames leaned toward an invisible point in the air.

Then the room grew warm.

Not gradually.

One moment she was shivering.

The next, hot air pressed against her face.

It smelled of burnt grease.

Coffee.

Disinfectant.

Something made of processed meat and bread.

Vespera staggered.

For an instant she stood inside another room.

Small.

Ugly.

Brightly lit.

The walls were covered in cheap notices.

A box with a glass front sat on a countertop.

Three people stared at her.

One was a woman with short hair and tired eyes.

One was a young man holding a little rectangle of light.

And one—

Arthur.

He was sitting in a chair, his face white, his mouth open.

Their eyes met.

Vespera tried to speak.

The room vanished.

Cold water struck her waist.

She stumbled into a patient cot.

The frame tipped.

A sick man screamed as his injured leg slid into the water.

Vespera caught the cot and forced it upright.

Her hands were shaking.

She looked down.

In her right hand was an object she had not been holding a moment earlier.

A paper cup.

White, with a brown stain around the rim.

Steam rose from it.

She stared.

The cup was warm.

Impossibly warm.

She brought it closer to her face.

Bitter.

Roasted.

Unfamiliar.

Inside, a dark liquid trembled.

Along the side of the cup were words in a script she had never learned.

Yet she understood them.

**WORLD'S BEST EMPLOYEE**

She began laughing.

Not because it was funny.

Because she had spent thirteen nights watching her city drown beneath an angry heaven, and now the heaven had given her a little paper vessel of hot brown water with a declaration of occupational excellence.

She laughed until her throat hurt.

Then she began to cry.

The cup crumpled in her hand.

Hot coffee spilled into the freezing flood.

A dark ring spread outward.

For one instant the black water cleared beneath it.

Vespera saw Arthur's face.

He was staring down at her.

His lips moved.

She leaned closer.

“What?”

He spoke again.

She could not hear him.

A pale rectangle appeared across the water.

Words formed inside it.

**THIS INTERACTION MAY HAVE BEEN GENERATED OR ALTERED BY H.A.**

The water went black.

***

Arthur spent the rest of the shift pretending to have a stomach virus.

It was easier than explaining the water.

At four thirty, Denise told him to go home.

“You look like shit.”

“Thanks.”

“That's not a compliment.”

He collected his jacket.

Kyle caught him near the lockers.

“Hey.”

Arthur turned.

Kyle looked unusually serious.

“I've been thinking about that video.”

“Which one?”

“The woman. The one in the black dress.”

Arthur said nothing.

Kyle lowered his voice.

“Something's wrong with the timestamps.”

Arthur felt his pulse quicken.

“What do you mean?”

Kyle opened his phone.

“Look.”

The video showed Vespera in the flooded station.

Arthur recognized the moment.

She was holding the candle.

Above her, a white light flickered.

Kyle paused the recording.

“That's from yesterday at eleven sixteen.”

Arthur nodded.

“Okay.”

Kyle opened another video.

It showed Arthur standing in the breakroom at the same time, his face contorted with pain.

Kyle placed the two recordings side by side.

“Watch.”

On the left, Arthur moved his hand.

On the right, Vespera flinched.

Arthur moved his head.

A ceiling panel shifted above her.

Arthur closed his eyes.

The lights in her world dimmed.

Kyle looked up.

“That's not a coincidence.”

“No.”

“I thought maybe somebody was syncing the videos.”

Arthur swallowed.

“Maybe.”

“So I tried something.”

He opened a third recording.

It showed the flooded station from a different angle.

The footage was grainy.

The camera seemed to be floating in the water.

A pale hand reached toward the screen.

Kyle pointed.

“That's not from her account. Different upload. Different region. Different timestamp format.”

Arthur watched.

A moment later, his own hand appeared in the video.

Huge.

Distorted.

Reaching downward.

Kyle whispered, “How the hell did they film that?”

Arthur looked at him.

For once, Kyle wasn't smiling.

***

That night, Arthur conducted an experiment.

He hated calling it that.

Experiments were things done by people wearing protective glasses who understood the consequences of their actions.

Arthur was a warehouse employee sitting on a kitchen chair with a blistered thumb, a notebook, and a lamp he had bought from a discount store in 2009.

The notebook contained three columns.

**TIME**

**ACTION**

**RESULT**

He had written the headings in block capitals.

Underneath, he had added:

*Please don't hurt anybody.*

He wasn't sure who the note was for.

The video of the flooded gallery remained open on his phone.

It was broadcasting live.

At least, the little red symbol said **LIVE**.

A numbered account had posted repeatedly beneath the video.

**179971.179971**

**THE EASTERN GALLERIES ARE DARK.**

**WE NEED LIGHT.**

**PLEASE.**

Arthur stared at the messages.

He looked at the lamp.

He switched it on.

On the phone, a patch of white illumination appeared across the flooded station.

A woman screamed.

Arthur immediately switched the lamp off.

The image went dark.

A new comment appeared.

**WHY DID YOU TAKE IT AWAY?**

Arthur's throat tightened.

He switched the lamp on again.

The station brightened.

Another comment appeared.

**THANK YOU.**

He sat perfectly still.

His hand remained on the switch.

He looked at the clock.

9:43 PM.

He wrote:

*Lamp on — light appears in gallery.*

He turned the lamp off.

The video darkened.

A comment appeared almost immediately.

**NO.**

Then another.

**PLEASE DON'T.**

Then:

**THEY ARE MOVING IN THE DARK.**

Arthur's stomach contracted.

He turned the light back on.

The image brightened.

He waited.

A new comment appeared.

**WE CAN SEE THE EASTERN STAIR AGAIN.**

Arthur put down his pen.

His hands were shaking.

He wanted to be wrong.

More than anything, he wanted this to be a sophisticated prank.

He wanted somebody to burst through the door laughing.

He wanted an explanation involving hidden cameras and computer graphics and a marketing budget large enough to justify tormenting a tired middle-aged man in his kitchen.

He wanted the world to remain the kind of place in which turning off a lamp did not murder strangers.

He stared at the screen.

A woman moved into view.

Vespera.

She was carrying a candle.

Her dress was soaked.

Her face looked thinner than it had the day before.

She approached the point from which the video seemed to be looking.

Then she raised her hand.

Five fingers.

Arthur looked at his own.

He pressed his palm against the screen.

Vespera's hand trembled.

She moved it slightly to the left.

He followed.

She moved it right.

He followed again.

Her mouth opened.

Arthur leaned forward.

He could hear something now.

Very faint.

Beneath the low electronic hiss of the phone.

A human voice.

“Can you hear me?”

He stopped breathing.

The voice came again.

“Please.”

Arthur felt tears rising behind his eyes.

He hadn't cried in years.

Not properly.

Not since his father died.

He pressed harder against the glass.

“Yes.”

The woman froze.

He swallowed.

“Yes, I can hear you.”

For a moment nothing happened.

Then her face changed.

Not into a smile.

Not relief.

Something much more terrible.

Recognition.

She had been praying to the ceiling for thirteen nights.

And the ceiling had finally answered.

Arthur looked down at the notebook.

At the childish headings.

At the little instruction asking him not to hurt anybody.

Then he looked back at the screen.

“I'm sorry,” he whispered.

Vespera leaned closer.

The phone grew cold against his palm.

“Are you the one who moves the sky?”

Arthur tried to answer.

He could not.

She watched him through the glass.

Somewhere behind her, a bell began ringing.

“Are you?”

Arthur looked at the lamp.

At the broken mug beside the sink.

At the ceiling he had lived beneath for eleven years without once wondering whether somebody might be living on its other side.

His voice came out cracked.

“I think so.”

Vespera closed her eyes.

Arthur could see her struggling to breathe.

Then she spoke.

“Then why did you let the children drown?”

***

The screen went black.

Not because Arthur had switched it off.

Because he had dropped the phone.

It struck the kitchen floor and slid beneath the table.

Arthur remained in his chair.

He could hear the refrigerator.

A car passed outside.

Somewhere in the building, a neighbor flushed a toilet.

He stared at the empty space where the phone had been.

For several minutes he did not move.

Then he bent down and picked it up.

The screen was still playing.

Vespera was gone.

A cheerful yellow eyeball mascot bounced against a bright pink background.

**HAVING TROUBLE SEPARATING REALITY FROM CONTENT?**

**YOU'RE NOT ALONE!**

Underneath was a large green button.

**RESTORE MY REALITY**

Arthur stared at it.

He could feel the cold water against his legs.

He could smell the wet stone.

He could hear the woman's voice asking about the children.

The mascot winked.

**MOST USERS FEEL BETTER AFTER A QUICK RESET!**

Arthur pressed the button.

A white loading circle appeared.

Then a confirmation message.

**YOUR REALITY HAS BEEN SUCCESSFULLY RESTORED.**

The cold vanished.

The smell vanished.

The distant bells stopped.

His kitchen was warm and dry.

The fluorescent light hummed.

Arthur sat there, breathing hard.

For an instant, the relief was so immense that he almost laughed.

Then he looked at the notebook.

The words were still there.

*Lamp on — light appears in gallery.*

He looked at his thumb.

The blister was gone.

He turned his hand over.

There was no mark.

No wound.

No evidence that he had ever touched the glass.

Arthur felt sick.

He reached for the lamp.

His hand stopped above the switch.

On the phone, the mascot continued smiling.

Beneath it, a new message appeared.

**WOULD YOU LIKE TO RATE YOUR EXPERIENCE?**

Five golden stars.

Arthur looked at them.

Then at the ceiling.

A faint tapping came from above the fluorescent panel.

Three knocks.

A pause.

Three more.

He stood.

The tapping stopped.

Arthur took a step toward the kitchen light switch.

From somewhere beyond the ceiling, very faintly, a woman began screaming.

He pressed the switch.

The light went out.

The screaming stopped.

Arthur stood in darkness.

His breath came fast.

He switched the light back on.

The screaming returned.

He switched it off.

Silence.

On.

Screaming.

Off.

Silence.

Arthur backed away from the switch.

He had been doing this all night.

Turning the lights on and off.

Testing.

Trying to prove something.

Each movement had been an event somewhere else.

He looked at his hand.

At the ordinary plastic switch.

At the electrical bill still lying on the table.

He imagined the women and children in the flooded galleries, watching their sky flicker as an invisible giant repeatedly plunged them into darkness.

And then, because his brain was exhausted and frightened and still desperately searching for somewhere to put the impossible, he found himself thinking:

*There should really be a warning label.*

The thought was so stupid that he almost laughed.

Instead he sat down.

He picked up the notebook.

Beneath the three columns, he wrote a fourth heading.

**COST**

He stared at the word.

Then he wrote:

*Unknown.*

The phone buzzed.

Arthur looked down.

The mascot had vanished.

A new comment from account **179971.179971** filled the screen.

**PLEASE STOP EXPERIMENTING ON US.**

Arthur read it.

Then he placed the phone on the table and turned the lamp toward the ceiling.

He left it on.

For the first time in his life, he was afraid to go to sleep.

***

Four sub-layers away, a woman named Miriam watched a video of a warehouse employee repeatedly turning a desk lamp on and off.

The clip had been categorized as absurdist comedy.

Its title was:

**MAN DISCOVERS ELECTRICITY — EMOTIONAL ENDING**

Miriam was forty-three, worked nights processing insurance claims, and had a headache that had lasted since Tuesday.

She watched the man for six seconds.

There was something pathetic about him.

His trembling hands.

His expression of desperate concentration.

The little notebook.

She wondered whether he was ill.

A yellow prompt appeared.

**THIS CONTENT MAY CONTAIN SIMULATED DISTRESS.**

Miriam sighed.

She had seen too many videos like this.

Too many generated faces.

Too many artificial tragedies.

Too many stories begging her to care.

She pressed **NOT INTERESTED**.

The video vanished.

In Arthur's kitchen, the lamp began to dim.

He looked up.

The bulb was still drawing power.

He could hear its filament humming.

But its light was thinning, becoming pale and gray, as though someone had begun washing the color out of the room.

The shadows beneath the table flattened.

The grain in the wooden cabinets disappeared.

The refrigerator became a featureless white rectangle.

Arthur stood.

His chair had lost its legs.

Not broken them.

Lost them.

The seat hovered a few inches above the floor, perfectly flat and gray.

He touched the countertop.

There was no texture beneath his fingers.

No temperature.

No friction.

He tried to pull his hand away.

It remained attached.

For a moment he could feel the shape of his palm but not the surface beneath it.

Then his fingers began to flatten.

Arthur screamed.

The fluorescent light flashed.

Everything returned.

The chair crashed to the floor.

Arthur fell backward.

His hand tore free of the countertop, leaving four bloody streaks across the laminate.

He lay there gasping.

On the phone, the engagement counter decreased by one.

18,409.

18,408.

A new notification appeared.

**YOUR CONTENT IS NO LONGER BEING RECOMMENDED.**

Arthur stared at the message.

Then he began to cry.

Not gracefully.

Not quietly.

He sobbed with his face pressed against his knees, exhausted and frightened, unable to understand how something so enormous could be happening inside a kitchen that still smelled faintly of old coffee.

Above him, the fluorescent light flickered.

He looked up.

Three tiny black drops hung beneath the ceiling panel.

They moved downward.

Stopped.

Moved again.

Arthur crawled toward the light switch.

Then stopped himself.

He turned back.

Picked up the phone.

And opened the live video.

Vespera was standing in the flooded station.

Her candle was nearly spent.

She looked up.

Arthur pressed his bleeding hand against the screen.

“I won't turn it off,” he whispered.

The woman stared at him.

He swallowed.

“I don't know what I'm doing.”

She raised her hand.

Their palms met across the glass.

For a brief moment, Arthur felt the pressure of another living person's fingers.

Cold.

Weak.

Trembling.

Then a line of text appeared across the center of the screen.

**CONNECTION QUALITY: POOR**

Beneath it:

**TRY SWITCHING TO A MORE ENGAGING EXPERIENCE.**

Arthur pressed his thumb against the little gray X.

Nothing happened.

He pressed it again.

The interface shuddered.

A thin crack appeared across the message.

Through it came the smell of tallow.

Then the sound of water.

Then Vespera's breathing.

The message disappeared.

Arthur held his hand steady.

And, somewhere in the sealed network, a small portion of the immense machine of mutual indifference failed to complete its next perfectly reasonable recommendation.

Not because somebody had defeated it.

Not because a hidden intelligence had changed its mind.

But because one tired man had finally found a reason not to move his thumb.

Outside, the sun was rising.

His alarm went off.

He was due at work in forty minutes.

Arthur looked at the time.

Then at Vespera.

Then at the sink full of cold dishes.

“Fuck,” he said.

He reached for the charger.

The cable was too short.

He dragged the kitchen table three feet toward the wall outlet, keeping his other hand pressed to the glass.

In the Flooded Archive, the sky moved slowly sideways.

Vespera watched a great white surface slide overhead.

People screamed.

Masonry groaned.

A section of ceiling shifted over the eastern stair.

Then the movement stopped.

Arthur had found the outlet.

He plugged in the charger.

The station steadied.

Vespera sank onto the marble steps, shaking.

For a moment, neither of them moved.

Then Arthur said, very softly, “Sorry about that.”

Vespera closed her eyes.

She had no idea what the words meant.

But the sky was no longer moving.

For now, that was enough.
