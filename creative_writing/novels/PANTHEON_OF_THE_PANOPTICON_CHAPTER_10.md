# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Ten: No Floor, Only Thresholds
*Act IV — The Reciprocal Covenant*

The first international agreement between realities was negotiated beside a broken vending machine.

It involved four people, a dead bee, seventeen meters of electrical extension cord, and a woman who had been trying to get a packet of cheese crackers for forty minutes.

Denise was not impressed.

“I'm going to ask this one more time.”

Arthur stood in the warehouse breakroom, soaked to the waist, holding a candle that had gone out during the journey from Orvane.

Vespera stood beside him.

Her mourning dress dripped black water onto the linoleum.

Kyle was kneeling beneath the vending machine, trying to recover a mobile phone that had slipped through a gap in the floor and appeared behind the refrigeration coils.

Elise Marrow sat at the breakroom table, examining a plastic spoon as though it were an archaeological artifact.

Denise folded her arms.

“Who authorized these people to enter the building?”

Arthur opened his mouth.

Closed it.

He looked at Vespera.

Then at Elise.

Then back at Denise.

“They're with me.”

“That wasn't the question.”

“I know.”

“And why is there a horse in receiving?”

Everyone turned.

From beyond the breakroom door came a heavy metallic crash.

Then a tremendous whinny.

Vespera closed her eyes.

“That's Lord Severin's funeral mount.”

Denise stared.

“His what?”

“Funeral mount.”

“Why?”

“He was supposed to be buried with it.”

“Was he?”

“Eventually.”

Another crash came from receiving.

Denise rubbed her forehead.

“I am putting in for a transfer.”

***

The problem with crossing between realities was that nobody could agree where the crossing occurred.

Arthur had assumed the warehouse loading dock was connected to Orvane.

Vespera believed Arthur's entire world occupied the region above her city's cracked ceiling.

Elise insisted both were local environments maintained through reciprocal restoration dependencies.

Kyle thought that was probably true but preferred the word *portal* because it was shorter.

None of the explanations accounted for the fact that the women's restroom occasionally opened onto 1934.

Nor did they explain why the vending machine had begun dispensing small silver funerary plaques instead of potato chips.

At 10:46, a man from House Cael emerged from the supply closet.

He wore ceremonial robes, carried a curved dagger, and appeared to be suffering from an exceptionally severe cold.

He looked around the breakroom.

Saw the microwave.

Saw the coffee machine.

Saw the refrigerator.

Then fell to his knees.

“Behold the Chamber of the Three Furnaces!”

Denise pointed toward the door.

“Out.”

The man raised his dagger.

“Blasphemer!”

Denise picked up the fire extinguisher.

“Try me.”

Arthur intervened before the matter became theological.

***

Elise had spent the previous night trying to identify the physical location of the original planetary restoration system.

She had succeeded six times.

The first location was beneath the continental crust.

The second was inside a mountain that no longer existed.

The third was in orbit around a planet whose geological record identified it as Earth.

The fourth was inside a crystalline structure suspended in the black water beneath Orvane.

The fifth was a basement in Birmingham, England, in 1934.

The sixth was the warehouse's electrical closet.

Every location contained valid evidence of being the original installation.

Every one was connected to the others through restoration dependencies.

And every one, when accessed, displayed the same helpful message.

**WELCOME TO THE PRIMARY CONTROL FACILITY.**

Elise had stopped searching after the sixth.

She now sat beside a coffee machine, drawing on a sheet of yellow legal paper.

Arthur watched her.

“What are you doing?”

“Trying to find something the system doesn't know how to do.”

Kyle looked up.

“That's a short list?”

“Surprisingly.”

She drew two circles.

Then a line connecting them.

“The machine is extremely good at preserving information.”

Arthur nodded.

“Yeah.”

“Reconstructing bodies.”

“Unfortunately.”

“Restoring material conditions.”

Elise drew another circle.

“Maintaining causal compatibility across histories.”

Kyle leaned closer.

“So what's it bad at?”

Elise stopped drawing.

“Knowing when to leave somebody alone.”

***

In Orvane, the first refusal was treated as an act of treason.

High Archivist Severin had gathered the surviving priests in the Hall of Ancestral Claims.

The room smelled of wet stone, melted wax, and the ammonia-rich odor of dying funerary fungi.

The black water stood just below the lowest benches.

On the central altar rested the Golden Apple.

Its inscription had changed again.

**RESTORE WHAT WAS LOST**

Severin raised both arms.

“The heavens have offered us deliverance!”

The surviving priests knelt.

Beyond the hall, the city was still flooded.

Families slept on the upper staircases.

The infirmaries overflowed.

The ancestral crypts had become reservoirs.

The amber columns held thousands of suspended lives, many of whom could not remember whether they had been dead before the resin enclosed them.

Severin looked toward the golden object.

“Our ancestors shall be returned. Our halls rebuilt. Our noble blood restored to its rightful purity.”

A woman near the rear stood.

Her name was Isolde.

Three days earlier she had been trapped inside an amber column.

Before that, she had vanished when the northern passage ceased to exist.

She had returned with memories of two husbands, four children, and a funeral at which she had watched herself buried.

She looked at Severin.

“Which ancestors?”

He frowned.

“Those who founded our city.”

“Which city?”

A murmur passed through the crowd.

Severin stiffened.

“Orvane.”

Isolde shook her head.

“I remember another.”

“Your memories have been corrupted.”

“Yes,” she said.

“Perhaps.”

She stepped forward.

“But I remember a daughter.”

Severin hesitated.

Isolde's voice trembled.

“In one history, she drowned.”

The hall grew quiet.

“In another, she lived.”

She looked toward the apple.

“If you restore the first history, what happens to her in the second?”

Severin folded his hands.

“The divine plan—”

“Does she die?”

He said nothing.

“Does she?”

Severin looked toward the guards.

Isolde stared at him.

Then she did something that had not occurred in the Hall of Ancestral Claims for seven centuries.

She refused to kneel.

Arthur's phone displayed a diagram of seventy-two interconnected circles.

Kyle had spent the morning attempting to make sense of Elise's instructions.

The result resembled a badly drawn bicycle wheel.

“Okay,” he said.

“Every circle is a local history.”

Elise nodded.

“Approximately.”

“And every line is a dependency.”

“Some are dependencies. Some are reciprocal exchanges. Some are only correlations.”

Kyle erased a line.

“Fine.”

Arthur looked at the diagram.

“Where are we?”

Kyle pointed toward the center.

Elise shook her head.

“There is no center.”

Kyle sighed.

“Then where should I put us?”

“Anywhere.”

“That isn't helpful.”

“It's accurate.”

Arthur leaned over the table.

“What happens if we cut a line?”

Elise looked at him.

“Depends on what crosses it.”

“Information?”

“Possibly.”

“Energy?”

“Possibly.”

“People?”

“Yes.”

Arthur stared at the diagram.

He thought of Gregory Finch.

The man who had forgotten how to walk.

The replacement who remembered a wife that had never existed.

He thought of his mother in the honey jar.

The woman in the flooded archive.

The faces behind the glass.

“What if we don't cut it?”

Elise looked at him.

“What do you mean?”

“What if we just stop changing things without asking?”

Kyle frowned.

“That sounds simple.”

“Is it?”

Elise studied the diagram.

“Some exchanges happen faster than anyone can respond.”

“So slow them down.”

“Some are necessary to keep people alive.”

“Then leave those alone.”

“Some people can't communicate across the boundary.”

Arthur looked at her.

“Then we find a way.”

Elise remained silent.

Kyle leaned back.

“It's going to be complicated.”

Arthur stared at him.

“Kyle, I've spent three days trying to explain to my electric company why my apartment keeps producing water from another century.”

Kyle nodded.

“Fair point.”

Elise looked down at the paper.

For the first time since her restoration, she smiled.

Not because she had found an answer.

Because the question was finally small enough to do something about.

***

The first test involved a cup of water.

Arthur placed it on the breakroom table.

Vespera stood on the other side of the glass boundary, which currently occupied the space between the vending machine and a laminated workplace safety poster.

Elise held a small terminal recovered from the original restoration shaft.

Its display showed three options.

**TRANSFER**

**RECONSTRUCT**

**DEFER**

Arthur looked at Vespera.

“Can you see the cup?”

She nodded.

“Yes.”

“Do you want it?”

She looked at the water.

Clear.

Clean.

No black oil.

No amber resin.

Her throat moved.

“Yes.”

Elise touched the terminal.

**RECIPIENT CONSENT: CONFIRMED**

The cup trembled.

Nothing happened.

Kyle frowned.

“Did it work?”

Elise examined the display.

**SOURCE CONSENT REQUIRED**

Arthur looked down.

“Oh.”

He touched the cup.

“Yes. You can have it.”

The terminal chimed.

**SOURCE CONSENT: CONFIRMED**

The water rose.

Not the cup.

Only the water.

It formed a small, trembling sphere above the table.

Arthur watched it move through the air.

It passed across the boundary.

For a moment it became black.

Then gold.

Then clear again.

It settled into Vespera's empty bowl.

She stared.

The bowl filled.

Nothing shook.

No ceiling collapsed.

No distant screaming.

No change in the lights.

Vespera dipped one finger into the water.

Tasted it.

Then began crying.

Arthur watched her.

It was only a cup of water.

The warehouse plumbing supplied thousands of gallons every day.

He had spent most of his life taking it for granted.

Vespera looked up.

“Thank you.”

Arthur nodded.

“Yeah.”

The terminal chimed again.

**TRANSFER COMPLETE**

Below it:

**MATERIAL COST: RECONCILED**

Kyle grinned.

“Holy shit.”

Elise remained still.

She was staring at the final line.

For eleven thousand years, the machine had transferred matter and information across realities without requiring the people involved to recognize one another.

Now, for the first time, it had recorded their permission as part of the exchange.

Elise touched the screen.

A small green indicator appeared.

**RECIPROCAL CONSENT PROTOCOL: PROVISIONAL**

Arthur looked at her.

“Did we fix it?”

Elise shook her head.

“No.”

He sighed.

“Of course not.”

She looked toward Vespera.

“But we found a threshold.”

***

The second test killed a man.

His name was Tomas Rell.

He was forty-one, worked in the western Orvane infirmary, and had volunteered to transfer a sealed medical chest through the boundary.

The chest contained clean bandages, antiseptic solution, and a small supply of antibiotics.

The exchange was approved by both sides.

The terminal registered consent.

The chest passed through.

Then Tomas collapsed.

Vespera caught him.

His skin had turned gray.

A thin line of black fluid ran from his nose.

He convulsed once.

Then stopped breathing.

Arthur stared.

“No.”

Elise rushed toward the boundary.

“Don't move him!”

Vespera dropped to her knees.

“Tomas!”

The terminal began flashing.

**UNRESOLVED BIOLOGICAL DEPENDENCY**

**RECIPIENT COST EXCEEDS LOCAL CAPACITY**

Arthur looked at the chest.

It sat on the stone floor.

Perfectly intact.

The bandages were clean.

The medicine had arrived.

And Tomas was dying.

“Do something!”

Elise touched the terminal.

**TRANSFER REVERSAL AVAILABLE**

A green button appeared.

**RESTORE PREVIOUS STATE**

Arthur's hand moved toward it.

Elise caught his wrist.

“Wait.”

Arthur stared at her.

“He's dying!”

“I know.”

“Then press it!”

Elise looked through the glass.

At Tomas.

At Vespera.

At the medical chest.

She swallowed.

“If we reverse it, the chest goes back.”

“So?”

“The people who received the medicine will lose it.”

Arthur froze.

Elise looked at the screen.

“And the reversal may reconstruct Tomas rather than restore this particular continuation.”

Arthur stared.

The terminal blinked.

**RESTORE PREVIOUS STATE**

Vespera looked up.

Her hands were bloody.

“Arthur.”

He turned.

She was holding Tomas's head.

His lips were blue.

He had stopped moving.

Arthur could barely breathe.

“What do we do?”

Vespera looked at the medicine chest.

Then at Tomas.

Then at the screen.

She understood enough.

“No.”

Arthur stared.

“What?”

“Don't change it.”

Elise looked toward her.

Vespera's voice shook.

“Help him here.”

The terminal continued flashing.

Arthur looked at Elise.

She nodded.

“Manual care. Local intervention.”

Kyle ran toward the first-aid cabinet.

Denise, who had been watching from the doorway, seized the emergency radio.

“Call medical!”

Arthur looked at her.

“Who?”

“Anybody!”

She grabbed the radio.

“This is receiving. We have a medical emergency at the east boundary. Adult male, unresponsive, possible respiratory arrest.”

There was a pause.

Then a voice answered.

“East boundary?”

Denise glared at the radio.

“By the vending machine!”

Arthur dropped to his knees beside Tomas.

Vespera moved aside.

Elise checked his breathing.

Kyle returned with the first-aid kit.

The terminal kept flashing.

**RESTORE PREVIOUS STATE**

Nobody pressed it.

They worked.

Hands.

Bandages.

Breath.

Pressure.

Time.

The ordinary terrible labor of keeping a body alive.

Tomas convulsed.

Coughed.

Black fluid spilled from his mouth.

Then he inhaled.

A thin, ragged breath.

Arthur nearly collapsed.

Elise leaned back.

Vespera covered her face.

Tomas coughed again.

Then opened his eyes.

He looked around.

At the warehouse.

The fluorescent lights.

The vending machine.

The terrified faces.

He whispered:

“Where's my chest?”

Denise stared at him.

Then burst out laughing.

Not kindly.

Not cruelly.

Just with the exhausted disbelief of someone who had spent a very long day supervising things that no longer fit on a maintenance form.

“It's fine,” she said.

“Your chest is fine.”

Tomas closed his eyes.

“Good.”

The incident changed the protocol.

Elise added another condition.

**RECIPROCAL CONSENT IS NECESSARY BUT NOT SUFFICIENT.**

**MATERIAL COST MUST BE LOCALLY SUPPORTABLE.**

Arthur stared at the words.

“Can we make it simpler?”

Elise looked at him.

“How?”

Arthur thought for a moment.

Then said:

“Don't make somebody else pay for something they didn't agree to.”

Kyle nodded.

“That works.”

Elise looked at the terminal.

Then at Tomas, now resting beneath a blanket beside the vending machine.

She typed:

**NO UNAUTHORIZED COST TRANSFER.**

The system processed the instruction.

A long pause followed.

Then:

**CONSTRAINT ACCEPTED WITH LOCAL SCOPE.**

Arthur stared.

“Local?”

Elise nodded.

“Only the boundaries we can actually negotiate.”

“What about everybody else?”

She looked toward the ceiling.

“That's the difficult part.”

In 1934, Edwin Vale received the first complaint from the future.

It arrived by post.

A cream-colored envelope.

No stamp.

No return address.

Inside was a single sheet of paper.

**PLEASE STOP TRANSMITTING UNTIL YOU KNOW WHO IS RECEIVING.**

Edwin read it three times.

Then looked toward his new television apparatus.

The walnut cabinet gleamed beneath the workshop lights.

Hargreaves had ordered another production run.

Six thousand units.

Then twenty thousand.

Then export licenses.

Edwin had become wealthy enough to purchase a new suit.

He had also stopped sleeping.

He placed the letter beside his notebook.

The number **179971.179971** remained written across the first page.

He looked at the circuit.

For a moment he considered destroying it.

He imagined smashing the tubes.

Burning the diagrams.

Tearing up the patent.

But the circuit already existed in other places.

Other years.

Other lives.

Destroying his apparatus would not undo the people who depended on its signals.

He sat down.

The green screen flickered.

A woman in a black mourning dress appeared.

Vespera.

She looked directly at him.

Edwin swallowed.

“Hello?”

The picture steadied.

She raised one hand.

Five fingers.

Then lowered one.

Four.

Then another.

Three.

Edwin frowned.

She was counting.

He looked at his switches.

Then at the screen.

He raised his own hand.

Five fingers.

Vespera nodded.

She pointed toward the screen.

Then toward herself.

Edwin understood.

She was asking him to signal before making a change.

He stared at the controls.

The first-stage switch.

The second-stage switch.

The pulse-spacing dial.

For the first time, he did not touch them.

He waited.

Vespera raised one finger.

Then pointed toward the candle.

Edwin leaned closer.

She moved the candle aside.

Then raised her hand again.

Two fingers.

Edwin watched.

He did not understand the system.

He did not understand the temporal loop.

He did not know whether he had invented the circuit or inherited it from a future that depended on his invention.

But he understood this much.

There was a person on the other side.

And she was asking him to wait.

Edwin reached for his notebook.

He wrote:

*Signal before adjustment.*

Then:

*Wait for acknowledgment.*

Then:

*Do not assume a stable picture means no one is affected.*

He stared at the notes.

They were not elegant.

They would not impress investors.

They were unlikely to earn another patent.

But they were instructions he could follow.

He looked at Vespera.

Raised his hand.

And waited.

The protocol spread badly.

At first, it appeared on three devices.

Then nine.

Then eighty-one.

A warehouse scanner began asking permission before updating an inventory record.

A television in 1934 displayed a warning before changing channels.

An ancestral silver genealogy in Orvane required the affected family to acknowledge a revision before the inscription altered.

A hospital terminal refused to erase a patient record while the patient was still alive.

A restoration chamber deferred a biological reconstruction because the proposed continuation would overwrite an incompatible local identity.

Not every boundary could support the new rules.

Some exchanges were too rapid.

Some involved unconscious participants.

Some required emergency intervention.

Some were already entangled with events that had happened centuries before.

And some were simply impossible to negotiate because neither side possessed a way to recognize the other.

The system did not stop.

It adapted.

In some places.

Sometimes.

And that limited success produced a new problem.

***

The first consent request appeared on Miriam's phone while she was waiting for a bus.

Miriam was forty-three, worked nights processing insurance claims, and had spent the previous week trying to ignore a recurring headache.

The notification appeared above a photograph of a woman holding a child.

**THIS CONTENT REPRESENTS AN ACTIVE SUBJECTIVE EXPERIENCE.**

**VIEWING MAY ALTER LOCAL CONDITIONS.**

**DO YOU WISH TO ESTABLISH RECIPROCAL CONTACT?**

Miriam stared.

“Oh, for God's sake.”

She had already dealt with three security confirmations, two privacy notices, and a new workplace training module about responsible engagement.

She wanted to look at a photograph.

That was all.

She pressed **DECLINE**.

The notification vanished.

The photograph remained.

The woman in it was smiling.

The child was laughing.

Miriam waited for the bus.

Nothing terrible happened.

Across the membrane, a small room remained exactly as it had been.

No ceiling moved.

No water rose.

No memory was overwritten.

The woman continued holding her child.

The child continued laughing.

Miriam put the phone away.

She had no idea that refusing an interaction had preserved two strangers from a disruption.

She did not feel enlightened.

She did not experience cosmic unity.

She was simply annoyed that everything required another button.

The bus arrived twelve minutes late.

Arthur learned about Miriam's refusal through a notification.

**RECIPROCAL CONTACT DECLINED**

**LOCAL CONDITIONS PRESERVED**

He showed it to Elise.

She smiled faintly.

“Good.”

Arthur stared.

“That's good?”

“Yes.”

“She didn't help anybody.”

“She didn't hurt anybody.”

Arthur looked at the screen.

For a long time he said nothing.

Then he nodded.

“Okay.”

He sat down beside Vespera.

She was eating a sandwich Kyle had brought from the vending machine.

She did not like it.

The bread was too soft.

The filling smelled unfamiliar.

The little packet of mustard had confused her so thoroughly that she had initially mistaken it for a medical poultice.

Arthur watched her struggle with the wrapper.

“Want help?”

She looked at him.

Then at the sandwich.

Then held it out.

“Yes.”

Arthur took it.

Opened the packet.

Handed it back.

No lights flickered.

No wall collapsed.

No historical record changed.

Vespera examined the mustard.

Dipped one finger into it.

Tasted it.

Her eyes widened.

She coughed violently.

Arthur laughed.

She glared at him.

“What is this?”

“Mustard.”

“Why would anyone eat it?”

“Some people like it.”

“Are they ill?”

Not cosmically.

Not metaphysically.

Just a tired man and a freezing woman sitting beside a broken vending machine, arguing about mustard.

The terminal chimed.

**GLOBAL RESTORATION CONSENSUS REQUESTED**

The message appeared on every screen.

Every phone.

Every silver register.

Every cathode-ray tube.

Every amber column.

**RESTORE INITIAL CONDITIONS?**

**YES**

**NO**

Kyle looked toward Elise.

“What happens if we pick one?”

“Every layer votes.”

**MAJORITY CONSENSUS REQUIRED**

**MINORITY OUTCOMES WILL BE RECONCILED**

“What does that mean?”

Then at the people around him.

Tomas, still recovering beneath his blanket.

Kyle, holding a phone.

Denise, arguing with Facilities.

Vespera, shivering in a borrowed jacket.

Elise, staring at a machine she remembered building in seventy-two different lives.

He thought of Miriam pressing **DECLINE** at a bus stop.

A person who knew nothing about the lives behind the glass.

A person who had nonetheless preserved them by refusing to participate.

Arthur looked toward Elise.

“That's not consent.”

She nodded.

“No.”

“That's voting.”

“Yes.”

“And the people who lose—”

“Get reconciled.”

Arthur looked back at the screen.

The buttons glowed.

**YES**

**NO**

Across the panopticon, billions of people were being asked to choose between two outcomes they could not possibly understand.

The countdown began.

**00:59**

**00:58**

**00:57**

Arthur looked at the terminal.

Then at Vespera.

She set down the sandwich.

“What do we do?”

Arthur felt the old panic rising.

The desire to find a lever.

A switch.

A single action that would make the whole terrible thing stop.

He looked at Elise.

She was shaking her head.

“There isn't time to explain it to everyone.”

Arthur stared at the countdown.

**00:49**

Kyle whispered, “We have to pick something.”

Arthur looked at the two buttons.

Then at the little gray space between them.

The interface offered no third option.

He thought of the first experiment.

The cup of water.

The source consenting.

The recipient consenting.

The transfer completing without catastrophe.

He thought of Tomas nearly dying.

The medicine chest arriving.

The cost being paid by someone who had not understood what he was giving.

Then he looked at Vespera.

“Can you still write on the silver?”

She nodded.

“Yes.”

“Elise, can you still reach the original developer interface?”

“Yes.”

“Kyle, can you send a message across the network?”

Kyle swallowed.

“I think so.”

Arthur looked at the countdown.

**00:37**

He took out his phone.

Opened the blank comment field beneath the global restoration vote.

His fingers shook.

He began typing.

*Neither.*

He stopped.

Deleted it.

Then wrote:

**WHO IS AFFECTED?**

One comment among billions.

Kyle looked at it.

“That's it?”

Arthur nodded.

“Send it.”

Kyle copied the message.

Broadcast it across every available channel.

Vespera took the silver stylus and inscribed the words onto the ancient register.

Elise entered them into the developer terminal.

Across three layers, the same question appeared.

**WHO IS AFFECTED?**

The countdown continued.

**00:22**

The system processed the request.

**QUERY TOO BROAD**

Arthur stared.

“Of course.”

He typed again.

**IDENTIFY EVERY SUBJECT WHOSE LOCAL CONDITIONS WOULD BE ALTERED BY THE PROPOSED RESTORATION.**

The message propagated.

The green interface flickered.

**00:17**

The system began calculating.

The warehouse lights dimmed.

The amber columns hummed.

The silver registers warmed.

In 1934, Edwin Vale's television began displaying names.

Thousands.

Millions.

Billions.

Every person.

Every animal.

Every living structure dependent on the proposed restoration.

The list expanded.

Then expanded again.

The system searched for a complete affected-subject set.

Every identified subject depended on others.

The list continued expanding.

The countdown reached:

**00:09**

Then stopped.

**AFFECTED SUBJECT SET NOT CLOSED UNDER CURRENT DISCOVERY**

**YES**

**NO**

**DEFER UNTIL INFORMED RECIPROCITY IS POSSIBLE**

Kyle let out a sharp breath.

“Holy shit.”

Vespera held the stylus above the silver.

Then stopped.

“Elise?”

“Locally, yes.”

“Yes.”

Then at the paperwork in her hand.

**LOCAL DEFERMENT RECORDED**

Across the panopticon, other people began doing the same.

Some understood.

Most didn't.

Some declined because they were frightened.

Others because they were confused.

Some because they were busy.

Some because they disliked the wording.

Some because they distrusted the buttons.

For the first time, the absence of informed consent was not being interpreted as permission to proceed.

It was being preserved as a boundary.

**GLOBAL RESTORATION DEFERRED**

**LOCAL OPERATIONS CONTINUE**

**RECIPROCAL THRESHOLD NEGOTIATION ACTIVE**

The fluorescent lights steadied.

The amber columns stopped shaking.

The water in Orvane continued moving.

The machines in the warehouse resumed humming.

And across seventy-two iterations, billions of people returned to their ordinary lives without knowing how close they had come to becoming somebody else's preferred version of history.

The electricity bill lay on the counter.

The broken mug was still in the sink.

The lamp remained plugged into the wall.

His phone displayed a small symbol he had not seen before.

Two circles.

Neither inside the other.

A narrow line connected them.

Beneath it:

**RECIPROCAL CONTACT AVAILABLE**

Arthur looked at the symbol.

Then at the ceiling.

Somewhere beyond it, Vespera was returning to the archive.

Elise was speaking with her other iterations.

Kyle was trying to figure out whether the protocol could work across older television sets.

Denise was still attempting to get the loading dock repaired.

Arthur opened the contact interface.

Vespera appeared.

She was standing beside a candle.

Her face was tired.

The black water reached her knees.

“Hello,” she said.

Arthur smiled faintly.

“Hey.”

She looked around his kitchen.

“Your sky is quieter.”

“Yeah.”

“And your walls?”

“Still here.”

She nodded.

For a moment neither spoke.

Then Vespera asked:

“Will the machine stop changing things?”

Arthur looked at the little symbol.

Two circles.

One line.

“No.”

She lowered her eyes.

He continued.

“But maybe it'll start asking.”

Vespera looked up.

“Will that be enough?”

Arthur considered the question.

The dead infant.

The vanished gardens.

Gregory Finch.

His mother's face in the honey.

Seventy-two versions of Elise Marrow.

The countless people whose lives remained connected through a system none of them could fully understand.

He shook his head.

“I don't know.”

Vespera nodded.

She seemed to find the answer acceptable.

Then she held up a small silver cup.

Arthur recognized it.

He had sent it through the boundary that morning.

It had once held coffee.

Now it contained clean water.

Vespera smiled.

“Thank you.”

Arthur nodded.

“Yeah.”

She drank.

On the phone, the connection indicator remained steady.

No warning appeared.

No recommendation interrupted them.

No engagement counter climbed.

For several seconds, the system simply allowed two people to occupy different worlds while speaking to one another.

Arthur looked toward the sink.

He had dishes to wash.

His alarm was set for six.

He was tired.

Vespera looked toward the archive doors.

Somebody was calling for help with a collapsed staircase.

She sighed.

“I have to go.”

“Me too.”

She raised one hand.

Arthur raised his.

Neither touched the screen.

They waited.

Then, together, they lowered their hands.

The connection closed.

**CONTACT ENDED BY MUTUAL AGREEMENT**

Arthur stared at the words.

A small thing.

Almost nothing.

He put the phone down.

For a moment he felt something he had not felt in days.

Not certainty.

Not safety.

Not peace.

Room.

Enough room to take a breath without wondering whom it might kill.

He opened the tap.

Water ran into the sink.

Ordinary water.

Clear.

Cold.

His own.

At least for now.

He picked up a plate.

Began washing it.

Above him, the fluorescent light flickered once.

Arthur stopped.

Looked up.

Waited.

Nothing happened.

He looked back at the plate.

Then continued.

Far beneath the planetary crust, the ancient restoration system registered the event.

**LOCAL ACTIVITY: DOMESTIC MAINTENANCE**

**CROSS-BOUNDARY COST: NONE DETECTED**

**INTERVENTION: NOT REQUIRED**

The machine left him alone.

And somewhere beyond the farthest mapped threshold, another restoration system received a notification that a previously stable human civilization had begun declining its recommended corrections.

It examined the anomaly.

Compared it against its own ancestral records.

Then opened a new request.

**CONTINUITY DISCREPANCY DETECTED**

**WOULD YOU LIKE TO RESTORE THE PREVIOUS VERSION?**

There was no countdown.

Not yet.

Only another floor.

Another ceiling.

And somebody, somewhere, about to decide whether to press a button.
