# Chapter 7: Bread in the Dark

Home smelled of bread.

Anja had expected the first crossing to be a procession through wreckage. She had dreamed of exposed conduits, ice in corridors and bodies moving in the dark with lamp beams for faces. Fifty-six years of official reports described Cell Seventy-Two as a hazard. She knew how stubbornly official descriptions could enter the nerves.

Instead a woman opened the inner vestibule and the smell of rising grain reached Anja before the room did.

The crossing had required six hours of negotiation and two teams of inspectors. Nalin had read the pressure certificate aloud, objected to a clause giving registered security indefinite emergency entry, and demanded that it be struck. Holt amended the permit without calling Command. The Witness accepted the version with both signatures as a bounded inspection record; it did not thereby certify the political agreement.

Anja entered with Miro and a portable instrument case. She wore ordinary work clothes. Nalin had insisted on that. "Suits make you look as if our air is an insult," he said. The pressure gauges were within tolerance. The air smelled of people, yeast and an unfamiliar vegetable soap.

The central corridor was too narrow for the population it served. Children ran along a strip of blue paint marking the safe side of a repaired floor. Farther down, an older woman carried a basket of vegetables beneath grow lamps that cast red shadows on her arms. The lamps had been salvaged from obsolete surgical theaters. Home had perfected the art of asking one piece of equipment to do three incompatible jobs without forgetting which job it had been designed for.

Nalin brought them to a public kitchen. Four heavy ovens had been built out of fire-brick, old ceramic insulation and panels stamped with the insignia of a factory that no longer existed. A baker stood at a table kneading dough with both forearms. He looked at Anja once, then returned to work.

"Are we supposed to have a tour?" Anja asked.

"You're supposed to assess safety," Nalin said. "But I thought you'd ask different questions after breakfast."

Tavi was there, seated on a low stool with a notebook on her knees. She had an expression of theatrical boredom.

"Visitors," she called to the baker, "are here to find out whether we can be people in a narrow room."

The baker lifted a doughy hand. "Tell them narrow rooms use less heating."

Someone laughed. A little boy with a gap in his front teeth asked whether the visitors had seen real stars. Miro said yes, and admitted that from inside the ship they looked mostly like pinholes. The boy found that disappointing.

Anja ate flatbread with a paste made from bitter algae and toasted seeds. It was better than the breakfast she had left unfinished in Habitat Twenty. She could not tell whether the taste was objectively better or whether her senses were rearranging the world because it contradicted the chart.

Home had made its own school, an infirmary, water purification terraces and a council elected from households and work crews. It had also made black markets, a faction opposed to any opening, a disciplinary committee with more authority than Nalin liked, and a dangerous habit of delaying repairs when doing them required asking the registered ship for materials.

"This panel was due for replacement thirteen years ago," Anja said, kneeling beside a buckled rib.

"Then you're late," said the technician working beside her.

His name was Daret. He was thirty-two and had been born two rooms down. He wore his hair shaved close so it would not catch in fans. He did not conceal his distrust.

"I can splice a new member in three shifts," Anja said.

"If you have steel."

"We have steel."

"You've always had steel. Do you have a way of giving it to us without taking something else?"

Anja touched the corroded seam. "I don't know."

"Then write that down. It's more useful than promising a bridge."

She did.

***

Miro spent the morning with Elsa Bener. She sat propped on a chair by a small window that looked into a hydroponic room, and she took his questions with dry impatience.

"Everybody asks whether I can breathe," she told him. "Nobody asks whether I can cook."

"Can you?"

"Of course. I don't want to. I've cooked for seventy-four years."

Miro laughed. "That is valuable clinical information."

"Write it exactly."

Anja met Tavi by the school after lunch. Seventy-two copper tags hung from a ceiling wire, one for each unit of a year-long study the children called the Crossing Calendar. The school had built its own world history from remembered transmissions. There was a large gap where news of Earth ought to have been. The registered ship had received records from the departing solar system for sixty years. Home had intercepted only fragments.

"We thought the old country was a place with blue roofs," Tavi said. "There was a diagram once. It showed the Earth from above, and the sea made everything blue. A teacher thought those were roofs."

"Did you believe it?"

"When I was little. Then I learned the difference between a map and what the map leaves out."

"That sounds like something an archivist would say."

"I am an archivist."

Anja glanced at her. "You said you were an apprentice."

"Is there a distinction in your records?"

"A certification."

"Is certification what makes a person able to remember?"

Anja did not answer. Tavi smiled slightly. She was not always trying to wound, Anja realized. Sometimes she was trying to find out whether the other person could stand a question.

They passed a wall mural showing Meridian surrounded by the stars, not arriving anywhere, simply traveling. Home's children had painted the hull as eighty-one bright rectangles. A little girl had drawn a face inside the rectangle everybody else had left blank.

"That's me," Tavi said.

"Why your face?"

"Because I was seven and the school had run out of blue paint."

For the first time Anja could picture fifty-six years of children making things rather than waiting.

***

Near the departure hatch Nalin stopped her. "There is something you should take back to your engineers."

He held out a jar filled with reddish dust.

"From the outer ribs," he said. "We have collected it for twenty years. Your calibration model calls it corrosion. Our plants respond to it. The filters grow warmer when the ship's long-range instruments listen toward Nacre."

Anja examined the dust. It was not crystalline at a glance, but under the lens it formed delicate branching clusters. It might have been a local electrostatic deposit. There were too many possible sources to call it anything else.

"Have you kept the dates?" she asked.

"Every collection. Tavi has the books."

At the final door Tavi asked a question with less armor than usual.

"Does your sky really look that blue?"

"Nacre?"

"Yes."

"Sometimes. Sometimes it's white, or gray, or almost black. We only see the part of it facing us."

"That's reassuring."

"Why?"

"Because everything I know of the outside comes in a picture someone else made. I was starting to think the planet might be incapable of changing its mind."

Anja stepped into the vestibule with the jar in her hand. Home's bread scent followed her as the pressure doors closed.

On the other side, the registered ship smelled of disinfectant and clean plastic.

She had always called that smell normal.
