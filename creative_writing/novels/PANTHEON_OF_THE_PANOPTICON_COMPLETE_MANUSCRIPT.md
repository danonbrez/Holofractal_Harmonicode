# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter One: The B.S. Filter

Arthur woke with his cheek stuck to the kitchen floor.

For several seconds he couldn't remember why he was there. His left arm had gone numb beneath him, his shoulder hurt, and something sharp was pressing against his ribs. He shifted and heard the brittle crunch of a broken coffee mug.

The fluorescent light was still on.

He stared at it.

Nothing happened.

No screaming woman. No flooded subway. No impossible black water rising through the table. Just a yellowing plastic diffuser containing three dead flies and a moth that had apparently died trying to escape the same way it came in.

Arthur closed his eyes.

“Fuck.”

He rolled onto his back, immediately regretted it, and lay staring at the ceiling while sensation returned to his arm in a swarm of needles.

The microwave said 5:47.

He had to be at work at seven.

His phone was under the table, face down. The chair lay beside him. There was a dent in the drywall where his shoulder had hit it.

That last detail bothered him.

He could explain the mug. Cheap ceramic. Thermal stress. He could explain the chair. He'd fallen asleep sitting up, probably jerked awake, knocked things over.

He could explain the hallucination.

Sleep deprivation. Three nights of four-hour sleep. Too much caffeine. Too much garbage on his phone.

It was almost reassuring how many explanations there were.

Arthur sat up.

The coffee had dried into an irregular brown stain across the laminate. A piece of paper was glued to it. He peeled the electricity bill loose and discovered that the account number had transferred backward onto the table.

He rubbed at the numbers with his thumb.

They didn't come off.

He stood, filled a glass at the sink, and drank half of it before noticing the smell.

Diesel.

Faint, but unmistakable.

He sniffed the glass. Nothing. The tap water smelled like chlorine and old pipes. He sniffed his hand.

Diesel.

Arthur put the glass down.

The ordinary explanations began rearranging themselves in his head, competing for position.

He had been dreaming.

He had spilled something.

He had a gas leak.

He was having a stroke.

His phone buzzed beneath the table.

Arthur flinched so violently that he knocked the glass into the sink.

He crouched and retrieved it.

A notification from H.A.

**Your recent activity is getting attention!**

Beneath it was a thumbnail.

A middle-aged man in underwear and one sock, sprawled on a kitchen floor.

Arthur stared at his own face.

The thumbnail had 18,406 views.

He tapped it.

A loading circle appeared, spun twice, and disappeared.

**This content is no longer available.**

He tapped again.

**This content may have been generated or altered by H.A.**

Arthur sat down very slowly.

For a long time he looked at the message.

Then he said, “Of course it fucking was.”

And because he needed that to be true more than he needed anything else that morning, he got dressed and went to work.

***

The breakroom smelled of burnt popcorn and disinfectant.

Somebody had written **PLEASE CLEAN UP AFTER YOURSELF** on a sheet of printer paper and taped it above a sink filled with mugs.

The irony had apparently survived three management changes.

Arthur stood in front of the microwave waiting for a breakfast sandwich whose packaging had promised *homestyle sausage* and whose ingredients suggested a complicated legal dispute over the definition of both words.

His supervisor, Denise, was talking to a younger employee named Kyle.

“H.A. is getting scary good,” Kyle said.

Denise was stirring powdered creamer into a cup of coffee. “It's been scary good for five years.”

“No, I mean the new stuff. It's making whole environments now. Like entire historical simulations. You can walk around in them.”

“Can it make the loading dock work?”

“I'm serious.”

“So am I.”

Kyle held out his phone.

“Look. This woman is completely generated. They even simulated hypothermia. Like, down to the capillaries.”

Arthur looked before he could stop himself.

Black dress.

White tiles.

Water up to the woman's chest.

His stomach dropped so abruptly that he thought he might lose control of his bowels.

The microwave beeped.

Kyle turned the screen toward Denise.

“See? Her lips are actually changing color. And the water physics—”

Arthur reached across him and grabbed the phone.

Kyle recoiled.

“Dude. What the hell?”

On the screen, the woman was trying to lift something out of the water. Her hands were raw. One fingernail had peeled backward, exposing a bloody crescent beneath it.

The video had no sound.

Arthur pressed the volume button.

Nothing.

He tapped the speaker icon.

**AUDIO REMOVED FOR COMMUNITY SAFETY.**

He watched her mouth moving.

He knew those movements.

*Hold it.*

*Hold the sky.*

Arthur's breakfast sandwich began revolving again inside the microwave.

He hadn't pressed anything.

“Arthur?”

Denise was watching him.

He realized he was gripping Kyle's phone hard enough to bend the cheap protective case.

“Where did you get this?”

Kyle frowned. “It's trending.”

“Where?”

“Everywhere.”

“No. Who posted it?”

“Jesus, man. I don't know. It's H.A.”

Arthur looked at the account name.

**179971.179971**

His mouth went dry.

Underneath the video, a small gray caption read:

**A dramatic reconstruction of events that may never have occurred.**

Arthur read it twice.

Then he handed the phone back.

“Sorry.”

Kyle looked at the screen, checking for damage.

“Are you okay?”

“Didn't sleep.”

Denise gave a short, humorless laugh.

“Welcome to the club.”

The microwave beeped again.

Arthur opened it.

Inside, his breakfast sandwich sat in its plastic wrapper, hot enough to blister his fingers.

Behind it, on the back wall of the microwave, was a wet handprint.

Five narrow fingers.

Black residue running downward from the fingertips.

Arthur shut the door.

Denise glanced over.

“You not eating that?”

He could hear his pulse in his ears.

“Lost my appetite.”

“Then don't leave it in there. People have to use that.”

Arthur opened the microwave again.

The handprint was gone.

He took out the sandwich, burned his thumb, dropped it onto the counter, and stood there looking at it.

Kyle was still scrolling.

“Man, the comments on this are insane.”

Denise sighed.

“What now?”

“People think she's real.”

Arthur turned toward him.

Kyle laughed.

“Like, actually real. Some guy says his aunt works in that subway station.”

“Which station?” Arthur asked.

Kyle shrugged.

“Doesn't exist. That's the point.”

He read another comment.

“Listen to this. ‘If you can see this, please don't turn off the lights. We have already lost the eastern galleries.’”

Arthur felt cold spread through his chest.

Kyle snorted.

“That's dedication. People are roleplaying in the comments now.”

He swiped.

The fluorescent lights flickered.

Arthur looked up.

For one second, between the long white fixtures and the stained ceiling panels, he thought he saw black water.

Not pouring through.

Not dripping.

Just sitting there.

An immense, still body of water held against the other side of the ceiling, with something pale moving slowly through it.

Then the lights steadied.

Denise slapped the side of the coffee machine.

“Maintenance needs to fix this place.”

Kyle put his phone away.

Arthur stood in the breakroom with his burned thumb pressed against his palm, wondering how many people had to be drowning before someone stopped calling it content.

On the noticeboard behind Denise was a new laminated memo.

**H.A. WELLNESS AND PRODUCTIVITY GUIDANCE**

*Employees experiencing persistent immersion, narrative bleed, simulated distress, or difficulty distinguishing generated content from workplace reality should contact Human Resources.*

Underneath, in smaller type:

*Please do not contact Facilities regarding perceived dimensional irregularities unless accompanied by a verifiable maintenance defect.*

Arthur read the memo.

Then he looked at the water stain spreading across the ceiling above it.

A single black drop formed at the center.

It fell.

Nobody else seemed to notice.

It struck the floor beside his shoe with the weight and sound of a distant hammer.

Arthur watched it spread between the tiles.

Deep inside the stain, impossibly far below the breakroom floor, a tiny woman in a ruined black dress was looking up.

She was holding a candle.

Arthur gripped the counter.

Behind him, Denise said, “Are you clocking in or what?”

The woman raised the candle higher.

Arthur could see her lips moving.

This time he didn't need sound.

She was saying his name.

# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Two: The Flooded Archive

The dead had begun floating out of their graves on Tuesday.

By Thursday, the undertakers were charging extra to put them back.

Vespera stood waist-deep in the eastern gallery, holding a candle above her head while Brother Malrec tried to force a swollen corpse through a doorway that had narrowed sometime during the night.

“Turn him sideways.”

“He won't fit sideways.”

“Then take his arms off.”

“He's consecrated.”

“He's blocking the door.”

Malrec looked at her with the patient disgust of a man whose religious convictions had become inconvenient to someone else's practical responsibilities.

“The dead of House Verrain are interred whole.”

“Then perhaps House Verrain should have built wider doors.”

The corpse rolled gently in the black water, its pale face turning upward.

A second pair of hands emerged from beneath the surface and caught its ankles.

Vespera nearly dropped the candle.

“Mother preserve us—”

“It's me,” a voice gurgled.

Toman surfaced beneath the body, coughing oil from his mouth.

“There's something caught under him.”

“Get out of there.”

“I can't. My boot's stuck.”

Malrec made the sign of the Ninefold Mercy.

Vespera seized the dead man's collar and pulled him aside. He was heavier than he ought to have been. Something inside his chest knocked softly against his ribs.

Toman jerked free.

A pale hand came with him.

Not his own.

It was attached to a length of forearm, the flesh softened and translucent from prolonged immersion. A copper funerary chain had wound around his boot.

Toman stared at it.

Then he began screaming.

Vespera slapped him.

He stopped.

“Take off the boot.”

“But—”

“Take it off.”

He obeyed, hopping in water too deep for hopping, and almost went under again.

Vespera caught his collar.

The candle guttered.

Above them, somewhere beyond sixty feet of masonry and a ceiling of white glazed tiles, the heavens made a terrible grinding noise.

All three looked up.

The tiles moved.

Not cracked.

Not shifted.

Moved, as a single vast surface, from east to west.

The water surged toward the doorway.

Malrec dropped the corpse.

“Down!”

Vespera caught the edge of the stone arch as a wave struck her back. Her candle went out. Toman vanished beneath the surface.

A sound came through the ceiling.

It was enormous, wet, and strangely intimate.

A ceramic crack.

Then the sky split.

***

Long before the waters rose, the city had been called Orvane.

Its kings had been buried beneath it, their kings beneath them, and beneath those kings lay the bones of things whose names had been forbidden before the first human language was properly finished.

The city had descended through its own history.

Each generation built downward because the surface was considered impure, and each buried generation became the foundation of the next. Ancestry was architecture. Houses occupied depth according to age, and marriage contracts specified not merely blood but the precise elevation at which children might legally be conceived, born, and interred.

There were families so old that their ancestral crypts had become load-bearing structures.

Removing a dead patriarch could bring down an entire neighborhood.

House Verrain claimed the eastern galleries.

House Cael occupied the western vaults.

Between them lay the Great Archive, where the genealogy of every recognized bloodline was inscribed upon sheets of beaten silver and stored in cabinets taller than cathedral towers.

The archive had survived seven dynastic wars, four plagues, the Burning of the Lesser Saints, and the disappearance of the Third King.

It was not surviving the water particularly well.

The black flood had arrived thirteen nights earlier, seeping upward through the lowest burial chambers. It carried no ordinary smell of sewage. It smelled of hot metal, old flowers, and the oily interior of a freshly opened animal.

It also behaved badly.

Sometimes it flowed downhill.

Sometimes it climbed walls.

In certain chambers it formed columns that stood unsupported in the air, twisting slowly as though listening.

And it reflected things that were not present.

Vespera had seen a vast white room in it, hung with rectangular suns. She had seen a metal beast with a single black eye. She had seen a man whose enormous face filled an entire vault, his features contorted in an expression of terror.

She had seen him fall.

The priests called him the God of the Broken Chalice.

Vespera called him the man.

She had not yet made the mistake of saying so aloud.

***

The sky split at the second hour after midnight.

It happened above the central station, where the tiled ceiling had always been considered the lowest visible portion of the divine firmament.

A black line appeared across the white.

From it came light.

Not candlelight.

Not the soft phosphorescence of funeral fungi or the blue combustion of alchemical salt.

This light was white and pitiless. It revealed every stain in the stone, every worm beneath translucent skin, every human face stripped of the flattering softness of flame.

It hurt to look at.

The priests threw themselves into the water.

Malrec began reciting the Litany of the First Descent.

Vespera stood with her mouth open.

Beyond the crack she could see the lower half of something immense.

A cup.

A broken, white, ceramic cup.

Its handle hung over the precipice like an arch from a ruined city.

Then a hand appeared.

Five vast fingers pressed against the boundary of the world.

Toman began sobbing.

Malrec raised both arms.

“BEHOLD THE CHALICE-BEARER!”

The fingers withdrew.

The ceiling lurched.

A thunderous crash followed, and the entire eastern gallery dropped several inches.

People screamed from the passages behind them.

Stone shattered.

The water rose.

Vespera caught Toman by his shirt and dragged him beneath the archway just as a section of tiled ceiling fell into the station.

It struck the water with a force that extinguished every candle still burning.

Darkness swallowed the gallery.

For several seconds there was only the noise of moving water and the thick, frightened breathing of three people trying not to drown.

Then the light returned.

Not gradually.

The great rectangular suns snapped on.

A shadow crossed them.

Something large moved above the fractured heavens, accompanied by a sound like furniture being dragged across an enormous floor.

Malrec was laughing and crying simultaneously.

“The god has heard us.”

Vespera coughed water from her lungs.

“Then ask him to stop.”

***

By the following morning, the priests had divided into three factions.

The Chalice-Bearers believed the giant had come to save them.

The Penitents believed he had come to punish them.

The Order of the Unbroken Vessel insisted there had never been a giant at all.

Their doctrine required an uninterrupted ceiling, and the enormous crack visible above the station was causing them considerable administrative difficulty.

At the emergency council, High Archivist Severin presented a theological solution.

“The fracture is a manifestation of collective impurity.”

A woman from the lower burial wards raised her hand.

“Does impurity explain why my husband was crushed by a tile?”

Severin folded his hands.

“Individual suffering must be understood in the context of the greater design.”

“Can the greater design help me pull his legs out of the wall?”

There was an uncomfortable silence.

The council had assembled in the Hall of Ancestral Claims, a chamber whose marble floor was now submerged beneath eighteen inches of oil-black water.

Acolytes moved between the benches with wooden poles, pushing away floating debris.

One retrieved a severed ear.

He hesitated, unsure whether it belonged in the medical basin or the relic basin.

Severin continued.

“The Chalice-Bearer has revealed himself at a moment of great spiritual necessity. The eastern galleries have suffered because the people have strayed from the covenant.”

Vespera sat near the back, wrapped in a soaked mourning cloak.

She had slept for perhaps twenty minutes.

Her hands were shaking beneath the table.

“What covenant?” she asked.

Severin regarded her.

“The covenant of our ancestors.”

“Which one?”

A few heads turned.

“There are forty-seven recognized covenantal revisions,” Vespera continued. “The oldest prohibits the use of silver in funerary instruments. The newest requires it. If the ceiling has a preference, perhaps it could indicate which revision applies.”

Malrec glared at her.

Severin smiled faintly.

“Lady Vespera, grief has made you irreverent.”

“Cold has made me impatient.”

“Your family's losses are known to us.”

“My family's losses are floating in the archive.”

The smile vanished.

Severin looked toward the guards.

Vespera looked back at him.

For a moment neither spoke.

Beneath the table, the black water touched the hem of her dress and began climbing.

She stood.

“Something is wrong with the water.”

Nobody moved.

It crawled upward in slender threads, following the embroidery of her mourning gown.

The threads converged at her waist.

And then, with the careful deliberation of a hand arranging flowers, they formed five black letters.

**A R T H U R**

Vespera stared.

The letters broke apart.

The water fell back into the hall.

Severin had seen them.

So had Malrec.

For a long moment, everyone remained silent.

Then Severin whispered, “The divine name.”

***

The first execution took place before noon.

The condemned man was a mason from the western galleries who had suggested that Arthur might be the name of a tradesman rather than a god.

He was accused of reducing the sacred revelation to common labor.

Vespera heard about his sentence while helping drain the eastern infirmary.

By then the water had risen to the knees of the patients lying on their cots.

Several were too weak to move.

One had developed a rash of pale, circular lesions that opened and closed in sequence along his spine. Another kept coughing up small translucent membranes that quivered when exposed to candlelight.

The physicians had stopped collecting samples.

There were too many.

A woman in the nearest cot was clutching a bundle of blankets to her chest.

“My baby,” she said.

Vespera approached.

The bundle moved.

The woman's eyes were sunken, her cheeks flushed with fever.

“She's been cold since the lights went away.”

Vespera touched the blankets.

Something inside them was breathing.

Very fast.

She peeled back the fabric.

The child couldn't have been more than three months old.

Her lips were blue.

Vespera looked toward the physician.

He was occupied with a man whose left hand had fused to a bronze bedframe.

“Bring hot water,” she told Toman.

“We haven't any.”

“Then bring blankets.”

“They're all wet.”

“Bring something dry.”

Toman stood helplessly.

There was nothing dry left in the infirmary.

The baby made a small, clicking sound.

Vespera wrapped her inside her own cloak and pressed the child against her chest.

The tiny body was frighteningly light.

Above them, the ceiling lights flickered.

The mother began praying.

“Blessed Chalice-Bearer, warm the child. Blessed Chalice-Bearer, restore the light.”

Vespera looked up.

The lights went out.

The entire eastern infirmary disappeared.

Someone screamed.

A cot overturned.

From somewhere behind her came the sickening noise of a body falling into deep water.

Vespera crouched, shielding the infant with both arms.

“Stay still!”

In the darkness, she could hear the mother whispering.

“Please.”

The prayer became a sob.

“Please.”

Then a sound came from above.

A distant click.

The lights returned.

The woman seized Vespera's wrist.

“He heard us.”

Vespera looked down at the child.

The infant had stopped breathing.

For a moment she could not understand what she was seeing.

She touched the small cheek.

Then the mouth.

Then the chest.

Nothing.

The mother looked at her.

“She's sleeping?”

Vespera opened her mouth.

No words came.

The physician was still trying to separate the man's hand from the bedframe.

Somewhere in the western galleries, a mason was being led to his execution for saying that a god might have a job.

The baby lay still against Vespera's skin.

Above them, the white lights continued to burn.

***

The Golden Apple arrived that evening.

It came without thunder.

Without proclamation.

Without any of the ceremonies the priesthood had spent centuries constructing to accommodate divine intervention.

A small object dropped through the crack in the station ceiling and struck a marble stair.

It made a sound like a spoon falling onto a plate.

A passing acolyte picked it up.

He was seventeen, recently initiated, and had spent most of the day carrying buckets of water from one flooded chamber into another flooded chamber because no superior had thought to revise the instructions.

The object was round.

Gold-colored.

Perfectly smooth.

It weighed less than he expected.

On its surface were three words.

He could read them, though he could not explain the language.

**TO THE FAIREST**

He turned the object over.

The words remained facing him.

He frowned.

Then he took it to Malrec.

Within an hour, the Chalice-Bearers had declared it a divine gift.

The Penitents insisted it was a test.

The Order of the Unbroken Vessel, whose doctrine had already suffered greatly that week, declared that the object had not fallen through the ceiling because the ceiling was demonstrably unbroken.

The argument became violent when House Verrain claimed that *fairest* referred to purity of blood.

House Cael countered that the phrase obviously meant purity of devotion.

The archivists produced seventeen genealogical volumes in support of the first interpretation and nine theological commentaries in support of the second.

By midnight, the volumes were burning.

Three men had been stabbed.

A fourth had fallen into an oil shaft.

Nobody could agree who owned the apple.

Nobody had asked what it was for.

***

At approximately the same hour, Arthur was trying to finish an inventory discrepancy report.

The loading dock computer had frozen.

Again.

The warehouse software displayed an error message that offered three possible solutions, none of which involved the actual problem.

He clicked **RETRY**.

The screen went white.

Then gold.

A small, shining sphere rotated in the center.

Below it, three words appeared.

**TO THE FAIREST**

Arthur blinked.

“What the hell?”

He clicked the close button.

Nothing.

He pressed Escape.

Nothing.

He held down the power button.

The monitor went black.

For half a second he saw his own reflection.

Behind it, a woman in a black mourning dress stood waist-deep in oily water.

Then the monitor turned itself back on.

**TO THE FAIREST**

From the next workstation, Kyle said, “Oh, you're getting it too?”

Arthur slowly turned.

Kyle was laughing at his screen.

“It's everywhere, man. Some viral campaign. Nobody knows who's behind it.”

“Can you turn it off?”

“Nope. That's why it's funny.”

Arthur looked toward the loading dock.

Every monitor was gold.

Every handheld scanner.

Every employee phone.

Even the little digital thermometer on the wall had begun displaying the same three words, scrolling across a screen barely wide enough for seven letters.

Denise came out of her office holding a clipboard.

“Why is nothing working?”

Kyle pointed at the screens.

“H.A. trend.”

Denise stared at the golden apple.

“Can the trend unload the truck?”

“No.”

“Then tell it to get the hell off my equipment.”

Arthur barely heard them.

Something had changed in the image.

The apple was no longer rotating.

It was sitting on a marble stair.

Behind it stood a woman in a soaked black dress.

Vespera.

She was staring directly at him.

There was blood on her cheek.

She lifted one hand.

Not in prayer.

Not in worship.

She pointed at the apple.

Then at Arthur.

Her lips moved.

He leaned closer.

The warehouse lights dimmed.

A cold draft touched his face.

For an instant he smelled tallow, iron, and rotting flowers.

Then the sound came through.

Small.

Hoarse.

Terribly human.

“Don't touch it.”

Arthur stepped backward.

The monitor flickered.

The woman vanished.

Across the warehouse, every screen displayed a new message.

**YOU HAVE BEEN SELECTED.**

Kyle whistled.

“Damn. Lucky you.”

Arthur felt his stomach contract.

The loading dock doors began opening.

All six of them.

Outside was not the parking lot.

There were white tiled walls, black water, and a marble staircase descending into darkness.

At the bottom of the staircase lay a golden apple.

Somewhere very far below, bells began ringing.

Denise dropped her clipboard.

For the first time since Arthur had known her, she had nothing to say.

# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Three: Kallisti
### The Spaces Between

The first person to notice the missing second was a woman named Edith Bell, who worked in receiving and had been counting boxes for nineteen years.

She was standing beside loading bay four when the doors opened onto the flooded staircase.

She watched a drop of black water fall from the upper door rail.

It stopped halfway down.

Edith blinked.

The drop appeared six inches lower.

Then it hit the floor.

She stared at the little black stain.

“Arthur?”

He was standing at the nearest terminal, staring past the loading dock into a place that had no business existing.

“Arthur, did you see that?”

He didn't answer.

Edith found another drop hanging from the rail.

This time she watched carefully.

It fell.

Stopped.

Moved sideways.

Stopped.

Fell again.

There was no blur between the movements. The water simply occupied one position and then another.

She held out her hand.

“Don't,” Arthur said.

She touched it anyway.

The drop struck her palm with such force that she cried out.

There was a neat circular hole through the fleshy part of her hand.

For a second, she didn't understand why she could see the yellow paint of the loading dock through her skin.

Then the blood came.

Edith screamed.

Denise grabbed a roll of packing tape and a clean rag from the maintenance cabinet. She pressed the rag against Edith's hand while trying to remember whether the first-aid procedure for puncture wounds involved elevation.

“Call an ambulance.”

Kyle had his phone out.

“I am.”

“Then why are you filming?”

“I'm not!”

He turned the screen around.

It was recording.

He hadn't opened the camera.

On the display Edith was standing beside the loading dock, holding out her uninjured hand.

The drop had not fallen yet.

Kyle looked from the screen to Edith.

Then back again.

On the phone, her finger touched the black water.

There was a sharp cracking sound.

Edith screamed a second time, perfectly synchronized with her own voice coming from the screen.

Denise dropped the tape.

“Turn that fucking thing off.”

Kyle pressed the power button.

The screen went black.

Then it displayed the golden apple.

**TO THE FAIREST**

***

In the Flooded Archive, Vespera was watching a man burn without fire.

He was one of the archivists from House Cael, a thin gentleman with an extraordinary collection of rings and the unpleasant habit of addressing servants as though they were poorly trained furniture.

His name was Osric.

An hour earlier he had seized the golden apple from the hands of a Verrain priest, declared it evidence of his house's ancestral supremacy, and attempted to carry it into the western galleries.

Now he was standing in the central aisle with his hands outstretched.

His skin was changing.

Not blistering.

Not melting.

Changing.

The first layer of his face remained where it was. Another appeared slightly to the left. Then a third, overlapping both.

Each face had a different expression.

One was shouting.

One was looking down at his hands.

One appeared to be laughing.

All three belonged to Osric.

“Help me,” he said.

The words came from different positions around his head.

Vespera backed away.

Osric took a step.

His body followed in three distinct movements, each separated by an instant of perfect stillness. The first displaced his legs. The second moved his torso. The third brought his head into alignment.

Something cracked inside his spine.

He fell to his knees.

The three faces became one.

Osric vomited a thick ribbon of blood.

Then the golden apple rolled from his fingers, struck the marble floor, and came to rest against Vespera's boot.

She looked down.

The inscription had changed.

**FOR THE ONE WHO SEES**

Malrec pushed through the crowd.

“Do not touch it!”

Vespera moved her foot.

The apple remained beside her.

Osric was trying to breathe. His rings had sunk into his fingers as if the bones beneath them had shortened.

“Bring a physician,” she said.

Malrec ignored him.

“The relic has chosen.”

“He needs help.”

“The relic has chosen *you*.”

Vespera looked at the apple.

Then at Osric.

Then at the crowd of priests who were already beginning to bow.

She was suddenly, horribly certain that somebody was going to die because of three words on a piece of metal.

And she was equally certain that no two people in the room had read the same three words.

***

At the warehouse, Arthur had found an ordinary analog clock.

It was mounted above the personnel office, a cheap plastic thing with a white face, a red second hand, and a battery someone had been meaning to replace since September.

He watched it.

Tick.

Tick.

The second hand moved.

Tick.

Then it moved backward.

Tick.

Arthur took a step closer.

The clock showed 11:16.

He looked at his phone.

11:16.

The warehouse computer.

11:16.

Everything agreed.

The second hand moved backward again.

The minute hand did not.

Arthur felt the pressure building behind his eyes.

He watched until the red hand reached the twelve.

It stopped.

He waited.

A second passed.

Then another.

Then another.

The second hand remained still.

From behind him came the low mechanical rumble of a forklift engine.

Arthur turned.

The forklift was moving toward an open loading bay.

There was nobody driving it.

Its front wheels advanced several inches.

Stopped.

Advanced again.

The machine made no sound between movements.

Arthur ran toward the emergency stop.

His body did not behave correctly.

He took one step and found himself farther along the floor than the step should have carried him. His shoulder struck a steel shelf before he had seen the shelf move into his path.

Pain exploded across his collarbone.

He stumbled.

The forklift advanced.

Stopped.

Advanced.

He could see the individual rotations of its front wheels, each arriving without the motion that should have connected it to the last.

He reached the emergency stop.

His hand struck the red button.

Nothing happened.

Then, several moments later, the button depressed beneath his palm.

The forklift stopped.

Arthur stood hunched over, panting.

The clock above the personnel office resumed ticking.

Behind him, Denise said, “We are closing the building.”

Nobody argued.

### 1934 — The Man Who Sold Tomorrow

The first moving picture of the future appeared on a Wednesday afternoon in a room that smelled of boiled cabbage, hot dust, and electrical insulation.

Edwin Vale was trying to get a refund on a defective vacuum tube.

He had purchased six of them from a surplus dealer in Birmingham, and four had failed within the week. The fifth produced an intermittent oscillation that had ruined two days of experiments. The sixth was currently glowing an unpleasant shade of orange.

He tapped the glass with a pencil.

“Come on, you bastard.”

The tube flickered.

A small crack appeared around one of its pins.

Edwin swore.

His workshop occupied the back of a former cobbler's shop, between a tobacconist and a public laundry. The landlord charged eleven shillings a week and had recently begun leaving increasingly unpleasant notes about the electricity bill.

On the workbench stood a homemade television receiver.

It was a dreadful-looking contraption.

Copper coils hung from wooden brackets. Resistors were soldered into irregular chains beneath a sheet of bakelite. A rotating scanning disk sat inside a metal housing scavenged from a broken ventilation fan. Beside it, Edwin had mounted a small cathode-ray tube purchased from a laboratory that had apparently decided electricity was less profitable than bankruptcy.

The tube's viewing surface was barely larger than a postcard.

It glowed green.

Mostly.

When it worked.

Which it generally didn't.

Edwin wiped his hands on a rag and examined the oscilloscope.

The trace was wrong.

He had spent three months attempting to eliminate an intermittent disturbance in the visual signal transmitted from a university laboratory several miles away.

The ordinary carrier produced the expected line noise.

The disturbance did not.

It appeared as a series of abrupt, evenly spaced interruptions, too regular to be atmospheric interference and too sharp to resemble any defect in the power supply.

He adjusted the tuning capacitor.

The interruptions remained.

He switched to the secondary coil.

Still there.

He disconnected the aerial.

The interruptions became stronger.

Edwin stared.

“That,” he said to the empty workshop, “is bloody interesting.”

He leaned closer.

The oscilloscope trace formed three narrow pulses.

A pause.

Then three more.

Then nine.

Then a longer pause.

He reached for his notebook.

A hot drop of solder fell onto the back of his hand.

He yelped, knocked over his pencil cup, and spent the next minute with his wrist beneath the cold tap, inventing increasingly elaborate obscenities.

When he returned, the trace was still there.

Perfectly steady.

Waiting.

***

Edwin had not begun the project to revolutionize telecommunications.

He had begun it because he was thirty-two, unmarried, four months behind on his rent, and tired of repairing wireless sets for customers who believed that opening the cabinet and shaking the valves constituted a legitimate diagnostic procedure.

His father had worked in a foundry.

His grandfather had worked in a foundry.

Edwin had worked in the foundry for six years before discovering that the smoke made him cough blood.

Electricity offered a cleaner future.

So far it had merely offered a different smell.

He sat down at the bench.

The irregular pulses continued crossing the display.

# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Eleven: Remembering How to Forget
*Act IV — The Climax*

At 5:58 in the morning, Arthur's alarm went off in seventy-two kitchens.

In one, he was forty-six and late for work.

In another, he was eight and supposed to be getting dressed for school.

In a third, he had been dead for eleven years, and the alarm was ringing beside an empty bed.

In the seventieth kitchen, nobody knew what an alarm clock was. A bronze bell hung above a stone basin. When it rang, a woman in mourning clothes looked up from a genealogy and whispered his name.

In the seventy-first, the kitchen had no floor.

In the seventy-second, Arthur's mother was frying eggs.

The sound reached all of them.

Arthur sat upright in his own bed and slapped the clock.

The ringing stopped.

He stared at the dark window.

His name was Arthur Bellamy. He was forty-six. He worked in receiving. His electric bill was overdue. There was a blister on his thumb, and his left knee hurt when it rained.

He repeated the facts until the other kitchens began to fade.

Then the telephone rang.

It was Denise.

“Don't come in.”

Arthur sat still.

“Why?”

“Because the loading dock has been restored.”

“Isn't that what you wanted?”

“The loading dock is now a forest.”

Arthur rubbed his eyes.

“A forest.”

“Yes.”

“With trees?”

“No, Arthur. A forest of shelving units. Of course with trees.”

A bird called through the telephone.

Denise lowered her voice.

“There's a man here insisting it's his ancestral woodland. He's threatening to shoot anybody who moves the oak by bay three.”

“Does he have a gun?”

“He has a crossbow.”

Arthur swung his legs out of bed.

“I'm coming.”

“I just told you not to.”

“I know.”

He heard Denise sigh.

“Bring boots.”

***

The seventy-two versions of Elise Marrow had reached a decision.

Not an agreement.

Not a consensus.

A decision to stop asking each other to agree.

They occupied seventy-two rooms, some linked by computer screens, some by silver mirrors, some by pools of black water. One Elise sat inside a hospital ward. Another was aboard a vessel with no visible planet below it. One stood in an orchard where fruit ripened and rotted in the time it took to speak a sentence.

Their faces appeared together on a shared display.

At its center was a question.

**RESTORE INITIAL CONDITIONS?**

The options had changed.

**RESTORE A SINGLE AUTHORITATIVE HISTORY**

**RELEASE ALL RESTORATION DEPENDENCIES**

The first would overwrite incompatible lives.

The second would terminate support for every life whose continued existence depended upon a neighboring layer.

The machine offered a comparison.

**ESTIMATED HARM: UNRESOLVED**

**ESTIMATED HARM: UNRESOLVED**

A younger Elise, no more than twenty, began to laugh.

“That's helpful.”

An older Elise said, “We should have finished the appendix.”

“You did,” said another.

The display rearranged.

A document appeared.

**SUBJECTIVE WELFARE REQUIREMENTS — FINAL**

Elise stared at it.

She remembered the unfinished appendix.

She remembered filing it away.

Yet here it was, marked final and signed with her own name.

She opened it.

The first page contained a paragraph on continuity.

The second contained a diagram of seventy-two reciprocal dependencies.

The third was blank.

The fourth showed a photograph of a little girl holding a bee.

The fifth was a car-wash receipt.

Elise scrolled.

The document continued indefinitely, drawing material from every available life, every surviving archive, every plausible reconstruction of what she might have intended.

Its final sentence never arrived.

One Elise said, “The system is trying to give us the missing answer.”

“It's giving us every answer,” said another.

“What's wrong with that?”

The oldest Elise looked down at her hands.

“Try living through them.”

***

In Orvane, the black water had begun falling upward.

It climbed the columns and struck the ceiling in heavy, rhythmic blows. Every impact sent a tremor through the buried city.

The amber statues were melting.

A woman stepped free of one column and immediately began calling for a child who had lived four centuries before her birth.

A man emerged with the body of a seventeen-year-old and the hands of an eighty-year-old.

A priest looked into the water and saw himself kneeling behind his own coffin.

Vespera moved among them with a candle and a blanket.

She had stopped asking which histories were true.

The woman calling for her child needed shoes.

The man with the old hands needed help walking.

The priest needed somebody to sit beside him until he stopped shaking.

Toman arrived from the eastern galleries.

“My lady, the sky has opened.”

“How far?”

“All the way.”

Vespera followed him to the central station.

Above the fractured tiles lay an enormous fluorescent kitchen.

Beyond it was a warehouse.

Beyond the warehouse, a green television screen.

Beyond the television, an orchard beneath a red sun.

Beyond that, another room, another sky, another face pressed close to glass.

There were so many that the distant layers became a pale, trembling haze.

Toman sank to his knees.

“Which one is heaven?”

Vespera looked at him.

He had lost one boot. His trousers were soaked. A small cut above his eyebrow had begun bleeding again.

“None of them has offered us a dry place to sleep,” she said.

He stared.

Then laughed, once, despite himself.

The ceiling shuddered.

A great white rectangle began descending toward the station.

Vespera seized Toman's collar.

“Move.”

***

Arthur arrived at the warehouse in borrowed rubber boots.

The trees were real.

Their roots had broken through the concrete. Moss covered the pallet racks. A narrow stream ran through receiving, carrying invoice paper and silver funerary leaves toward a drainage channel that no longer existed.

The air smelled of wet bark.

A forklift sat half-buried in the trunk of an oak.

Denise stood beneath it, holding a clipboard.

“Facilities says the work order is closed.”

Arthur looked up at the forklift.

“On what grounds?”

“Natural growth.”

“Elise needs us.”

“Where?”

Kyle pointed toward the old vending machine.

**FINAL RESOLUTION REQUIRED**

He approached.

**ONE HISTORY**

**NO HISTORY**

“Maybe there's a third option.”

There had always been a third option eventually. A little gray button. A loophole. A field where they could type the question the machine had forgotten to ask.

This time there was nothing.

Not even a close icon.

The screen reflected his face.

Behind it, hundreds of other Arthurs were watching.

One wore a child's school uniform. One had a scar across his forehead. One was so old that his hands shook against the glass.

They all looked frightened.

Arthur realized that he had been expecting one of them to know what to do.

***

The complaint reached the deepest accessible restoration chamber.

**DECISION REQUIRED**

**GLOBAL STATE CANNOT REMAIN INDETERMINATE**

The machine examined the available information.

Every attempt to isolate a preferred history altered another.

Every attempt to preserve all histories required material support from other layers.

Every attempt to remove the dependency network deprived some living subject of the conditions needed to survive.

No complete preference ordering could be constructed.

The system was not angry.

It had not become impatient.

Its energy reservoirs were finite. Thermal exchange continued. Biological bodies consumed oxygen and food. The suspended amber populations required maintenance. Every delay was also a physical operation, with costs of its own.

The green indicator blinked.

It searched for a valid next step.

Then a woman named Elise Marrow, standing in a half-flooded laboratory, entered a new instruction.

**PRESERVE THE ABILITY TO RECONSIDER.**

The machine examined it.

**NO TERMINAL STATE SPECIFIED**

Elise said, “Correct.”

**RESTORATION OBJECTIVE INCOMPLETE**

“I know.”

**HOW SHALL THE OBJECTIVE BE SATISFIED?**

Elise looked around.

Vespera was carrying a shivering child through the water. Across another screen, Edwin Vale held his hand above a switching dial, waiting for acknowledgment. Arthur stood before a vending machine in a forest that had replaced his workplace.

“Don't satisfy it for everybody,” Elise said. “Not at once.”

The system processed the sentence.

**SCOPE REQUIRED**

“Whatever you can account for without replacing somebody who hasn't agreed.”

**UNKNOWN SUBJECTS REMAIN**

“They always will.”

**INCOMPLETE KNOWLEDGE DETECTED**

Elise laughed.

“That's what I've been trying to tell you.”

***

Arthur saw the message appear on the vending machine.

**PRESERVE THE ABILITY TO RECONSIDER.**

“No,” Arthur said.

“Then what does it mean?”

Arthur looked at his reflections.

The child. The old man. The stranger with his face.

For one instant the memories of their lives rushed toward him.

He was eight, waiting for his mother.

He was seventy-three, dying beside a stranger.

He was an engineer watching a television screen.

He was a priest kneeling beneath the tiled heavens.

The memories arrived faster and faster, each with its own smells, injuries, obligations, and losses.

Arthur gripped the vending machine.

“I can't.”

Kyle caught his arm.

“Arthur?”

“I can't hold all of them.”

His vision began to disappear beneath the flood of lives.

He remembered a hundred mothers.

A hundred funerals.

A thousand mornings when he had been late for work.

He could feel the weight of every person he had ever been or might become, and the ordinary fact of standing in borrowed boots beneath an oak tree began slipping away.

The machine offered help.

**AUTOBIOGRAPHICAL INTEGRATION AVAILABLE**

**ACCEPT ALL MEMORIES**

Arthur reached toward the button.

He wanted the certainty.

He wanted to know exactly who he was, where he came from, what had happened to his mother, whether Gregory Finch was still Gregory Finch, and whether any of the other Arthurs had found a way to live without being afraid.

His finger hovered above the glass.

Then he heard Denise.

“Arthur!”

He turned.

She was holding his clipboard.

“Did you sign for the refrigeration shipment on Thursday?”

He stared.

“What?”

“Thursday. Six pallets. Supposed to arrive before noon. I need to know whether you signed for them.”

The flood of memories continued.

Arthur could remember shipping manifests from nineteen centuries.

He could remember a warehouse that had never been built.

He could remember the signatures of his ancestors and descendants.

Denise held out the clipboard.

“Arthur. Thursday.”

He looked at the paper.

Ordinary lines.

A date.

A place for a signature.

He remembered Thursday.

The actual Thursday that belonged to the man standing in these boots.

“No,” he said.

Denise frowned.

“You sure?”

“Yeah. Kyle signed for them. I was dealing with Edith.”

Kyle nodded.

“I did.”

Denise made a mark.

“Fine.”

She turned away.

Arthur caught the edge of the vending machine.

The other memories were still there.

# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Twelve: Knowing More Than You Understand
*Epilogue — The Quiet After the Laugh*

At 6:12 on a Thursday morning, Arthur dropped a teaspoon.

It fell all the way to the floor.

He watched it go.

The spoon struck a tile, bounced once, and slid beneath the refrigerator with a small metallic scrape.

Arthur stood holding his coffee.

He waited.

Nothing skipped. Nothing moved backward. The refrigerator did not open onto a funeral garden. No woman cried out from beneath the floor.

A spoon had fallen.

He crouched to retrieve it and discovered that his left knee was not prepared to celebrate.

“Jesus.”

The spoon had come to rest beside a dead moth and a refrigerator magnet he had lost three years earlier.

He reached for it.

His fingertips touched cold steel.

Ordinary cold steel.

He picked it up.

There was coffee on the handle.

He put it in the sink to wash later.

It was six twelve.

He had to leave in twenty-three minutes.

The warehouse reopened on a limited basis.

This was Denise's phrase, and she had made certain it appeared in every communication to corporate.

The forest had not disappeared, but the west side of the loading dock was usable again. A botanist from the county had identified the largest tree as an oak of indeterminate age, with root growth inconsistent with every available construction record.

The tree enclosed forklift twelve.

The botanist recommended preserving it.

The insurer recommended photographing it.

Corporate recommended taking advantage of the company's commitment to environmental responsibility.

Denise recommended moving the damn tree.

Nobody had yet produced a method that would not endanger either the workers or the people who sometimes appeared in the rings of its trunk.

So forklift twelve remained where it was.

Someone had attached a handwritten sign.

**OUT OF SERVICE**

Underneath, in smaller letters:

**DO NOT WATER WITHOUT PERMISSION.**

Arthur had added that part.

The first time a janitor emptied a mop bucket against the roots, three centuries of Orvane's funerary gardens had received a sudden flood of soapy water.

The janitor was now required to use the other drain.

There were more procedures like that.

Small ones.

Imperfect ones.

They occupied a single folder in Denise's office, filed under **CROSS-BOUNDARY MAINTENANCE**, because Facilities would not accept **COSMIC INCIDENTS** as a service category.

Edith Bell had insisted on the name.

She had returned to light duty with six stitches removed from her palm and a ring of scar tissue around the place where the water had passed through it.

Her medical records were not entirely repaired.

The hospital still alternated between St. Catherine's and a car wash on the edge of town.

But the scar remained on her hand, and she had stopped letting anybody call it promotional material.

At nine twenty, she brought Arthur a cup of coffee.

“You look terrible.”

“Thanks.”

“Still getting messages?”

“Some.”

“About the hospital?”

“Not today.”

Edith flexed her hand.

It hurt when she did that.

Arthur watched her stop.

“Does it get better?”

She looked at the scar.

“Sometimes.”

She set the cup beside his clipboard.

“Good enough for Thursday.”

Then she went back to work.

***

In Orvane, the funeral gardens were being replanted.

Vespera had not recovered her mother's grave.

The northern passage still ended at the ancient wall. The sealed people were no longer heard through it, and no one could establish which of their histories remained accessible.

Their names had been copied onto silver, paper, stone, and a small digital archive Elise had helped the registrars build.

The names did not always agree.

When they differed, the archivists stopped replacing the older inscription.

They recorded the disagreement beside it.

Vespera had insisted.

“Leave both.”

High Archivist Severin had called it an invitation to disorder.

Vespera had shown him the wall.

“Disorder is already here.”

He had threatened to convene the ancestral court.

She had told him to convene a work party instead.

They were still arguing.

But in the eastern gardens, people had begun planting flowers.

The first roses were pale and weak. Their roots had been transferred through a negotiated boundary from a greenhouse whose owners had agreed to spare the cuttings.

A few did not survive the cold stone.

The gardeners tried again.

On the seventh morning, one opened.

A small yellow bee settled on its petals.

Vespera watched from the stair.

The insect's wings made a delicate sound.

No lights flickered.

No city shook.

She did not know how much of the bee belonged to Orvane and how much had crossed from somewhere else.

She was glad it had arrived.

Then she remembered that the bloom would need water.

She went to fetch some.

Elise Marrow had acquired an ordinary notebook.

It was made of paper.

She kept it in her pocket because she no longer trusted any document capable of rewriting itself while she was asleep.

The first page contained the only instruction all seventy-two of her accessible iterations had agreed to retain.

**DO NOT SUBSTITUTE COMPATIBILITY FOR CONTINUITY.**

Below it, in different handwriting:

*Ask before crossing.*

Below that:

*Record costs.*

And beneath the final line:

*Leave room for uncertainty.*

They had not agreed on the order.

One Elise believed material cost came first. Another argued that no consent was meaningful without a reliable account of the affected subjects. A third insisted emergency care could not always wait for communication.

They retained the disagreement.

The notebook stayed open.

Elise lived in a small room above the eastern infirmary.

Its window looked onto a wall of old stone and a narrow channel of black water. The roof leaked when the white ceiling above Orvane was disturbed. There was a wooden chair and a bed that made her back ache.

She could have reconstructed her original laboratory.

Sometimes she wanted to.

She remembered polished floors and clean water. She remembered instruments that worked, a refrigerator full of food, a colleague bringing coffee on Friday afternoons.

She also remembered the system replacing a grieving mother's kitchen with a preferred earlier version and taking the mother away with it.

So Elise kept the crooked chair.

She fixed the roof.

She paid attention to which stone was beneath her feet.

One morning, an eleven-year-old Elise appeared on the communication terminal.

The girl wore a school uniform and had a small bandage on her chin.

“Are you me?”

Elise looked at her.

“Yes. And no.”

The girl frowned.

“That's a bad answer.”

“I know.”

“Are you going to tell me what happens?”

Elise looked at the notebook.

She remembered wanting to know that when she was eleven.

She remembered being told.

She remembered not being told.

She remembered three incompatible futures in which the answer had changed everything.

“What do you want to know?”

The girl thought.

“Does Dad come home?”

Elise went still.

In one remembered history he did.

In another he died in the foundry.

In a third there had been no foundry and no father by that name.

She could not give the child a single complete answer.

She could give her what she knew about the histories she had seen, but not promise the child's own continuation.

“I'm not sure,” she said.

The girl looked down.

“Oh.”

Elise leaned closer.

“Is there somebody with you?”

“Mum.”

“Can you tell her what you're worried about?”

The girl shrugged.

“Maybe.”

Elise waited.

The girl touched the little bandage on her chin.

“Do we get clever?”

Elise smiled.

“Sometimes.”

“Does it help?”

“Sometimes.”

The girl considered that.

Then laughed.

“Another bad answer.”

“Yes.”

They agreed to talk again when both wanted to.

The connection ended.

No history was overwritten.

Elise wrote the time in her paper notebook.

And for several minutes afterward she sat looking out at the stone wall, remembering a father she could not reliably locate in any one past.
