# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Seven: The Raster Tube
*Act III — The Anticommutative Collision*

### 1934 — The Man Who Sold Tomorrow

The first moving picture of the future appeared on a Wednesday afternoon in a room that smelled of boiled cabbage, hot dust, and electrical insulation.

Edwin Vale was trying to get a refund on a defective vacuum tube.

He had purchased six of them from a surplus dealer in Birmingham, and four had failed within the week. The fifth produced an intermittent oscillation that had ruined two days of experiments. The sixth was currently glowing an unpleasant shade of orange.

He tapped the glass with a pencil.

“Come on, you bastard.”

The tube flickered.

A small crack appeared around one of its pins.

Edwin swore.

His workshop occupied the back of a former cobbler's shop, between a tobacconist and a public laundry. The landlord charged eleven shillings a week and had recently begun leaving increasingly unpleasant notes about the electricity bill.

On the workbench stood a homemade television receiver.

It was a dreadful-looking contraption.

Copper coils hung from wooden brackets. Resistors were soldered into irregular chains beneath a sheet of bakelite. A rotating scanning disk sat inside a metal housing scavenged from a broken ventilation fan. Beside it, Edwin had mounted a small cathode-ray tube purchased from a laboratory that had apparently decided electricity was less profitable than bankruptcy.

The tube's viewing surface was barely larger than a postcard.

It glowed green.

Mostly.

When it worked.

Which it generally didn't.

Edwin wiped his hands on a rag and examined the oscilloscope.

The trace was wrong.

He had spent three months attempting to eliminate an intermittent disturbance in the visual signal transmitted from a university laboratory several miles away.

The ordinary carrier produced the expected line noise.

The disturbance did not.

It appeared as a series of abrupt, evenly spaced interruptions, too regular to be atmospheric interference and too sharp to resemble any defect in the power supply.

He adjusted the tuning capacitor.

The interruptions remained.

He switched to the secondary coil.

Still there.

He disconnected the aerial.

The interruptions became stronger.

Edwin stared.

“That,” he said to the empty workshop, “is bloody interesting.”

He leaned closer.

The oscilloscope trace formed three narrow pulses.

A pause.

Then three more.

Then nine.

Then a longer pause.

He reached for his notebook.

A hot drop of solder fell onto the back of his hand.

He yelped, knocked over his pencil cup, and spent the next minute with his wrist beneath the cold tap, inventing increasingly elaborate obscenities.

When he returned, the trace was still there.

Perfectly steady.

Waiting.

***

Edwin had not begun the project to revolutionize telecommunications.

He had begun it because he was thirty-two, unmarried, four months behind on his rent, and tired of repairing wireless sets for customers who believed that opening the cabinet and shaking the valves constituted a legitimate diagnostic procedure.

His father had worked in a foundry.

His grandfather had worked in a foundry.

Edwin had worked in the foundry for six years before discovering that the smoke made him cough blood.

Electricity offered a cleaner future.

So far it had merely offered a different smell.

He sat down at the bench.

The irregular pulses continued crossing the display.

He tried reversing the phase of the carrier.

The trace inverted.

He reversed the polarity of the second stage.

The pulses vanished.

He returned the leads to their original positions.

They reappeared.

Edwin frowned.

He repeated the sequence.

First stage.

Second stage.

Pulse.

Then:

Second stage.

First stage.

Nothing.

He repeated it again.

Same result.

The order mattered.

Not merely the final connections.

The sequence in which the circuit was changed.

Edwin stared at the equipment.

He had built circuits all his adult life. He understood switching transients, charged capacitors, and the peculiar ways a badly grounded apparatus could appear to have a personality.

This was not behaving like any of them.

He switched the first stage.

The oscilloscope brightened.

He switched the second.

Three pulses appeared.

He reversed the sequence.

The fifth vacuum tube emitted a sharp pop.

The glass envelope darkened.

A thin thread of smoke rose from the chassis.

Edwin jerked backward.

“God damn it!”

He grabbed the mains switch.

The room went quiet.

He sat staring at the ruined tube.

Five failures.

One remaining.

He looked toward the shelf where the unopened replacement sat in its cardboard box.

Then toward the landlord's latest notice.

**FINAL REMINDER**

Edwin rubbed his face.

He reached for the last tube.

***

At 4:17 PM, the test card disappeared.

The university laboratory had been transmitting a crude image of alternating black and white bars.

Edwin had spent the afternoon replacing the continuous carrier with a train of separated pulses, each passed through two switching stages in a fixed order.

The apparatus ran hotter than he liked.

Its power supply hummed.

The scanning disk wobbled badly enough to make the workbench tremble.

But the picture had improved.

The bars were clean.

The contrast was remarkable.

Edwin grinned.

“Ha!”

He adjusted the final control.

The screen went black.

Then green.

Then black.

The bars vanished.

A room appeared.

At first Edwin thought it was a photograph.

White tiled walls.

A long staircase.

A dark surface that might have been water.

He leaned closer.

The image moved.

A woman came into view.

She wore a black dress.

Her hair hung wet against her face.

She carried a candle.

Edwin stared.

The university laboratory was supposed to be transmitting a test card.

He glanced at the aerial connection.

It was still disconnected.

The woman turned.

Her face filled the green screen.

She looked exhausted.

Frightened.

Her lips were blue.

A small insect landed on her finger.

Edwin watched it crawl across her skin.

A bee.

The woman raised her hand toward the viewing surface.

The phosphor brightened.

Edwin felt a sudden pressure inside his ears.

Then the workshop filled with the smell of wet stone.

He recoiled.

The woman looked directly at him.

For one impossible moment, he had the distinct impression that she could see the room around him.

His workbench.

His tools.

The cracked window.

The remaining vacuum tubes glowing inside their protective cage.

She opened her mouth.

The loudspeaker emitted a low, broken noise.

Edwin reached for the volume control.

The noise resolved into a voice.

“Please.”

He froze.

The voice was female.

Hoarse.

Terribly faint.

“Please don't move it.”

Edwin stared at the screen.

“Hello?”

The woman flinched.

He leaned closer.

“Can you hear me?”

She looked upward.

The candle flame leaned sideways.

Then the picture broke into horizontal lines.

Edwin adjusted the pulse spacing.

The image stretched.

The tiled walls bent inward.

The woman's face elongated, splitting briefly into three overlapping profiles.

He pulled his hand away.

The image steadied.

The woman was gone.

In her place appeared a harshly illuminated room.

A small table.

A refrigerator.

A white rectangular box mounted above a counter.

A man in a gray shirt stood beside the table, staring at something he held in his hands.

The object was a flat black rectangle, glowing brightly.

Edwin had never seen anything like it.

The man looked middle-aged.

Tired.

He had a peculiar expression of concentration, as though the little object were doing something terrible that he was trying desperately to understand.

The room flickered.

A cheerful yellow cartoon bee appeared across the screen.

It held a golden apple.

Three words flashed beneath it.

**SWEETEN YOUR FEED**

Edwin stared.

The mascot winked.

Then the man in the gray shirt looked up.

Straight at Edwin.

Edwin felt an icy pressure across his chest.

The man opened his mouth.

A sound emerged from the loudspeaker.

“Don't.”

Edwin leaned forward.

“What?”

The voice cracked through the static.

“Don't turn it off.”

***

Naturally, Edwin turned it off.

He was an engineer.

When a piece of electrical apparatus began displaying impossible moving pictures and talking to him, the correct procedure was to disconnect the power, inspect the wiring, and consider whether one had accidentally inhaled too much solder flux.

The mains switch clicked.

The tube went dark.

The humming stopped.

For perhaps two seconds, everything was normal.

Then the floor shook.

A distant metallic groan passed through the room.

The workshop window rattled.

Somewhere beyond the brick wall, somebody screamed.

Edwin stood motionless.

The sound came again.

Not from the street.

Not from the ceiling.

From inside the dead cathode-ray tube.

A woman screaming in darkness.

Edwin stared at the black screen.

Then he heard a second sound.

A low buzzing.

Thousands of tiny wings.

The equipment was unplugged.

The buzzing continued.

He reached toward the tube.

Its glass was cold.

From the dark viewing surface came a faint smell of candle wax.

Edwin stepped backward.

He struck the workbench.

A screwdriver rolled off the edge.

It fell.

Stopped.

Moved sideways.

Stopped.

Then struck the floor.

Edwin stared.

He had seen something impossible.

He knew that.

Yet the first coherent thought that came to him was that he ought to check the floorboards for magnetism.

He crouched.

Picked up the screwdriver.

Dropped it again.

It fell normally.

He repeated the experiment.

Again.

Nothing.

Edwin stood.

He looked toward the cathode-ray tube.

The screen remained dark.

Then a single pale hand appeared inside it.

Pressed against the glass.

Five fingers.

The hand was enormous.

The skin looked burned.

Edwin backed into the wall.

The hand disappeared.

A message formed in green light.

**CONNECTION LOST**

Beneath it:

**RECONNECT TO RESTORE CONTINUITY**

Edwin stared.

He had never wired the apparatus to display words.

***

In Orvane, the sky had gone black.

Vespera was standing beside the amber columns when every source of white illumination vanished simultaneously.

The candle in her hand remained lit.

The golden resin dimmed.

The man suspended inside the nearest column stopped breathing.

Vespera seized the candle and pressed it toward him.

The flame flared.

His chest did not move.

“Come on.”

She held it closer.

Wax spilled across her fingers.

Nothing.

The man stared at her through the resin.

His eyes were wide.

His mouth opened slightly.

He was trying to inhale.

Vespera looked toward the ceiling.

“Arthur!”

Her voice echoed through the flooded gallery.

“Arthur, please!”

The white rectangles remained dark.

The amber began to cool.

The bees stopped moving.

One fell from a flower and landed in the water.

Vespera caught it.

Its wings trembled against her palm.

Then stopped.

She looked at the tiny body.

Above her, something shifted.

A vast, distant click.

The sky flashed green.

Not white.

Green.

A sickly phosphorescent light filled the gallery.

For a moment Vespera saw a room beyond the ceiling.

Wooden walls.

A narrow window.

Copper coils.

Glass vessels glowing orange.

And a man with dark hair and rolled-up sleeves staring down at her.

He looked as frightened as Arthur had.

Vespera raised her candle.

The man recoiled.

The sky flickered.

Then the white light returned.

The amber columns warmed.

The suspended man gasped.

Vespera sank to her knees.

The bee in her hand moved one leg.

Then another.

It crawled weakly toward her thumb.

Vespera began crying.

Not from relief.

From exhaustion.

There were too many heavens.

Too many faces.

Too many enormous strangers touching things they did not understand.

She looked upward.

“Stop,” she whispered.

“Please.”

***

Edwin did not sleep that night.

He rebuilt the pulse circuit twice.

He checked every resistor.

Measured every capacitor.

Replaced the damaged valve.

Inspected the aerial.

Measured the mains frequency.

Then he disconnected the entire apparatus from the electrical supply and observed the same three pulses crossing the oscilloscope screen for eleven seconds before the last stored charge should have been exhausted.

He wrote everything down.

At 2:14 AM, the picture returned.

The woman in the black dress.

The flooded gallery.

The amber columns.

Edwin watched a man suspended inside one of them take a slow breath.

He adjusted the pulse spacing.

The man stopped.

Edwin froze.

He moved the control backward.

The man breathed again.

Edwin stared at the dial.

He turned it slightly.

The man stopped breathing.

Turned it back.

Breathing resumed.

Edwin felt cold sweat beneath his shirt.

He had spent years adjusting electrical equipment without once wondering whether somebody might be suffocating when he turned a knob.

He looked at the screen.

The woman was pressing a candle against the amber.

Her mouth moved.

He turned the volume up.

“Please.”

Edwin's hand began to shake.

He let go of the control.

The picture steadied.

The man in the resin took another breath.

Edwin stepped away from the bench.

His mind reached for explanations.

A trick transmission.

A staged dramatic broadcast.

An experimental apparatus at the university.

Perhaps the military.

Perhaps foreign intelligence.

There were rumors about German television experiments and Russian radio technologies. There were always rumors.

He looked at the disconnected aerial.

Then at the woman.

She was looking upward again.

Not toward a camera.

Toward him.

Edwin sat down.

He covered his face.

For a moment he thought he might vomit.

Then he heard the knock at the workshop door.

***

The man who entered wore a dark overcoat and carried an expensive leather briefcase.

He introduced himself as Lionel Hargreaves.

His shoes were polished.

His cufflinks were gold.

He had the smooth, slightly pink complexion of somebody who had never been required to repair electrical equipment for a living.

“Mr. Vale?”

Edwin stood.

“Who wants to know?”

“Hargreaves Industrial Holdings.”

“Never heard of it.”

“Quite.”

The man smiled.

“I understand you've been working on a new visual transmission system.”

Edwin looked toward the bench.

The tube was dark.

“Who told you that?”

“A mutual acquaintance.”

“Who?”

“Does it matter?”

Edwin stared at him.

Hargreaves opened his briefcase.

Inside were papers.

A contract.

A patent application.

A bank draft.

Edwin saw the amount.

His stomach tightened.

It was more money than he had earned in the previous five years.

“What is this?”

“A proposal.”

“For what?”

“Your circuit.”

Edwin looked at the papers.

Then at the apparatus.

“I haven't finished it.”

Hargreaves smiled.

“Of course you have.”

“No. There are problems.”

“What sort of problems?”

Edwin hesitated.

The green screen flickered behind him.

Hargreaves glanced at it.

Then smiled again.

“Remarkable picture quality.”

Edwin looked at the screen.

The flooded gallery had returned.

Vespera stood beside the amber columns.

Hargreaves watched with apparent delight.

“Extraordinary depth.”

Edwin moved to block his view.

“That's not the transmission.”

Hargreaves raised an eyebrow.

“Isn't it?”

“No.”

“Then what is it?”

Edwin opened his mouth.

He had no answer.

Hargreaves stepped closer to the apparatus.

He examined the copper coils.

The switching stages.

The pulse generator.

“Discrete modulation,” he said.

Edwin stared.

“You know electronics?”

“A little.”

Hargreaves pointed toward the oscilloscope.

“Clean timing. Repeatable sequence. No requirement for continuous carrier fidelity. A very attractive commercial proposition.”

Edwin looked at the screen.

The woman was still standing there.

She had begun waving.

Hargreaves did not seem to notice.

“Think of the applications,” he continued. “Broadcasting. Industrial monitoring. Communications. Archival storage. Predictive signal reconstruction.”

Edwin frowned.

“Storage?”

“Certainly.”

“There's no storage in this circuit.”

Hargreaves looked at him.

The smile remained.

“Isn't there?”

Edwin felt cold.

Hargreaves tapped the contract.

“We can arrange the patent filings. Manufacture the components. Secure distribution. You retain a generous royalty.”

Edwin stared at the bank draft.

The amount was still there.

He thought of the rent.

The broken tubes.

The foundry.

The blood he had coughed into a handkerchief six years earlier.

He looked at the green screen.

Vespera had moved closer.

Her face was pale.

She was trying to speak.

Hargreaves followed his gaze.

“Does the picture always do that?”

Edwin swallowed.

“Do what?”

“Move when nobody touches the controls.”

The room went very quiet.

Edwin looked at the businessman.

Hargreaves smiled.

“Fascinating.”

***

By the following week, Edwin had convinced himself that the anomaly was a feature.

It was easier.

He had a contract.

A deposit.

A new workshop.

Three assistants.

And a promise that his invention would transform the future of communications.

The impossible moving pictures became **predictive frame synthesis**.

The voices became **cross-channel acoustic artifacts**.

The unexplained pulse sequences became **adaptive synchronization patterns**.

The fact that changing the controls altered the apparent lives inside the pictures was recorded as **phase-dependent image instability**.

Edwin wrote the words himself.

They sounded respectable.

They sounded scientific.

Most importantly, they sounded like something investors could understand.

The first commercial demonstration took place in a hotel ballroom.

Hargreaves invited industrialists, newspaper editors, university professors, government representatives, and several men whose occupations were never clearly stated.

A large cabinet stood at the front of the room.

Inside it was a modified version of Edwin's original circuit.

The viewing screen was larger.

The picture was brighter.

The equipment had been enclosed in polished walnut.

On the front, beneath the screen, a small brass plaque read:

**VALE-HARGREAVES DETERMINISTIC VISUAL SYSTEM**

Edwin stood beside the cabinet, sweating through his collar.

Hargreaves addressed the audience.

“Gentlemen, what you are about to witness is not merely an improvement in television.”

He paused.

“It is the beginning of a new era in information technology.”

Edwin watched the screen.

The test card was stable.

Good.

The pulse generator hummed.

Good.

The temperature gauges remained within their limits.

Very good.

Hargreaves continued.

“Our system does not depend upon the fragile continuity of ordinary transmission. Instead, it reconstructs visual information from precisely timed, deterministic pulses.”

Several men nodded.

One took notes.

“Repeatable. Efficient. Economical.”

Edwin looked at the controls.

The first-stage switch.

The second-stage switch.

The pulse-spacing dial.

Hargreaves gestured toward him.

“Mr. Vale?”

Edwin cleared his throat.

He stepped forward.

“Thank you.”

He looked at the audience.

The men stared back.

He had never spoken in front of so many people.

His mouth was dry.

He reached for the first switch.

Then the second.

The screen brightened.

The test card vanished.

A picture appeared.

Not the university laboratory.

Not the prepared demonstration film.

A warehouse breakroom.

White fluorescent lights.

A coffee machine.

A middle-aged man in a gray shirt sitting at a table.

Arthur.

Edwin froze.

The room was silent.

On the screen, Arthur looked up.

His face changed.

Recognition.

He stood.

The audience murmured appreciatively.

Hargreaves smiled.

“Observe the remarkable depth and natural movement.”

Arthur came closer to the screen.

His mouth opened.

Edwin heard the loudspeaker crackle.

“Don't.”

Edwin's hand shook.

Arthur raised his voice.

“Don't turn it off!”

Hargreaves laughed lightly.

“Excellent synchronized sound.”

Several men applauded.

Arthur was shouting now.

“Please! There's people under it!”

Edwin stared.

The businessman beside him continued smiling.

“Extraordinary realism.”

Arthur struck the glass from the other side.

The cabinet shuddered.

A faint crack appeared across the viewing tube.

The audience gasped.

Hargreaves stepped forward.

“Ladies and gentlemen, as you can see, the system responds dynamically to—”

Arthur screamed.

“STOP WATCHING!”

The screen went black.

Then gold.

A small apple appeared.

Three words formed beneath it.

**TO THE FAIREST**

The audience fell silent.

Edwin stared at the apple.

It rotated slowly.

On its surface, a new inscription appeared.

**WHO CAME FIRST?**

Hargreaves leaned toward Edwin.

“Is this part of the demonstration?”

Edwin could not answer.

The apple vanished.

A woman in a black mourning dress filled the screen.

Vespera.

Her face was wet.

Her hands were bleeding.

She looked directly at the audience.

Then she spoke.

Her voice emerged clearly from the loudspeaker.

“Please stop moving the sky.”

The ballroom remained silent.

A man in the front row began laughing.

Then another.

Someone applauded.

The sound spread.

Within seconds, the room was filled with enthusiastic applause.

Hargreaves beamed.

Edwin stood beside the cabinet, unable to move.

On the screen, Vespera began crying.

The audience applauded harder.

Hargreaves shook Edwin's hand.

“Marvelous.”

He turned toward the investors.

“Gentlemen, imagine the entertainment possibilities.”

***

That evening, Edwin returned to his workshop.

He had received three offers for additional manufacturing contracts.

A newspaper editor wanted an interview.

A government representative had asked whether the system could be adapted for surveillance.

Hargreaves had suggested that Edwin purchase a better suit.

Edwin sat at his old workbench.

The original apparatus remained where he had left it.

He switched it on.

The green screen brightened.

Nothing appeared.

He waited.

The pulse generator hummed.

Three pulses.

A pause.

Three.

Then nine.

Edwin adjusted the dial.

The screen remained blank.

He reached for his notebook.

Opened it.

Turned to the page containing his first observations.

At the top, in his own handwriting, was a number.

**179971.179971**

Edwin frowned.

He did not remember writing it.

Below the number was a calculation.

A ratio.

Then a series of pulse intervals.

The handwriting was unmistakably his.

But the ink was old.

Very old.

The paper had yellowed around it.

He turned the page.

Another entry.

Dated 1911.

He had been nine years old in 1911.

The entry described a completed version of the circuit.

He turned another page.

A drawing of the cathode-ray tube.

The label beneath it read:

**PROPERTY OF HARGREAVES INDUSTRIAL HOLDINGS — 2026**

Edwin stared.

His heart began pounding.

He flipped backward.

The earlier pages were changing.

Dates.

Diagrams.

Names.

A note in the margin described a signal received from the eastern galleries of Orvane.

Another mentioned a warehouse employee named Arthur Bellamy.

Another contained a sketch of a golden apple.

Edwin reached the first page.

At the top was a sentence.

**THIS INVENTION HAS ALREADY BEEN ARCHIVED.**

Beneath it:

**ORIGINAL INVENTOR: UNKNOWN**

Edwin stared.

Then he laughed.

Not because it was funny.

Because the alternative was to scream.

He looked at the apparatus.

At the copper coils.

The hand-wound chokes.

The ruined vacuum tubes.

The solder burns on his fingers.

He had built this thing.

He knew he had.

He remembered every miserable hour.

Every failed experiment.

Every bill.

Every repair.

Yet the notebook contained records of the machine before he had built it.

And records of people who would not exist for another ninety years.

He sat there, breathing hard.

Then something dripped onto the workbench.

A thick black drop.

Edwin looked up.

The sealed glass envelope of the cathode-ray tube was sweating.

Black oil had gathered around one of its internal electrodes.

Another drop formed.

Then another.

The oil ran down the inside of the glass.

It should not have been there.

The tube was evacuated.

Sealed.

He had inspected it that morning.

The black substance reached the lower edge.

It gathered.

Then pushed outward through the glass without breaking it.

A bead emerged.

Hung.

Fell.

Edwin caught it on the back of his hand.

It was warm.

Sticky.

It smelled of flowers.

He stared at the substance.

Then at the screen.

The green phosphor brightened.

A tiny image appeared.

A kitchen.

A woman in a blue housecoat.

She was frying eggs.

Edwin leaned closer.

The woman turned.

He knew her.

He had never seen her before.

Yet he knew her.

His mother.

Or somebody else's mother.

Or a woman who had been placed into the shape of his earliest memory.

He reached toward the glass.

The image vanished.

A message appeared.

**YOUR MEMORY EXPERIENCE HAS ENDED.**

Edwin stared.

Then another line formed.

**WOULD YOU LIKE TO SUBSCRIBE?**

He sat motionless.

The workshop smelled of solder, hot dust, and honey.

From the street came the sound of a tram bell.

Somebody shouted about the price of coal.

A woman laughed.

Ordinary life continued outside.

Edwin looked at the notebook.

At the number.

**179971.179971**

He picked up his pen.

Beneath the original entry, he wrote:

*The circuit does not remember the picture.*

He paused.

Then continued.

*The picture remembers the circuit.*

He stared at the sentence.

A faint buzzing filled the room.

The green screen went dark.

For a moment, Edwin saw his reflection in the glass.

A tired man.

Thirty-two.

Poorly dressed.

Frightened.

Then his reflection changed.

He saw Arthur.

Standing in a fluorescent kitchen.

Holding a phone.

Then Vespera.

Standing waist-deep in black water.

Holding a candle.

Then Ruth Mercer.

Sitting at a green computer terminal.

Holding three index cards.

Then thousands of faces.

Millions.

Each appearing for an instant.

Each looking through the glass.

Each watching somebody else.

Edwin stumbled backward.

The picture vanished.

The original test card returned.

Perfectly stable.

Perfectly clean.

A small line of text appeared beneath it.

**DETERMINISTIC VISUAL SYSTEM — CALIBRATION COMPLETE**

Edwin stared.

Then a second line appeared.

**COMMERCIAL LICENSE ACTIVE**

He heard a knock at the door.

Hargreaves's voice came from outside.

“Mr. Vale? We need to discuss the next production run.”

Edwin looked at the screen.

The green light reflected in his eyes.

For a moment he thought about telling Hargreaves everything.

The woman.

The water.

The impossible dates.

The living people inside the signal.

The fact that the invention appeared to have existed before its inventor.

Then he looked at the bank draft lying on the bench.

He thought of the landlord.

The foundry.

His father's ruined lungs.

He closed the notebook.

“Coming,” he called.

He switched off the apparatus.

The screen went black.

Somewhere far away, the lights in a flooded city dimmed.

Edwin did not look back.

***

In Arthur's warehouse, every electronic device began humming.

The handheld scanners.

The forklift displays.

The clocks.

The emergency exit signs.

The vending machines.

The security cameras.

Even the old analog radio Denise kept beside her desk, which had not worked in years, began emitting a low buzzing noise.

Arthur stood in the loading dock.

He had been watching the golden liquid crawl along the walls.

Now the entire building seemed to be vibrating.

Kyle came running from the breakroom.

“Arthur!”

Arthur turned.

Kyle held up his phone.

The screen showed an old black-and-white photograph.

A man stood beside a television cabinet.

Dark hair.

Rolled-up sleeves.

Tired eyes.

Behind him was a brass plaque.

**VALE-HARGREAVES DETERMINISTIC VISUAL SYSTEM**

Arthur stared.

“Who is that?”

Kyle swallowed.

“Some engineer. Nineteen thirty-four.”

Arthur looked at the photograph.

The man had one hand resting on the cabinet.

His other hand held a notebook.

On the notebook's cover was a familiar number.

**179971.179971**

Arthur felt cold spread across his chest.

Kyle enlarged the image.

The television screen was visible.

Inside it stood a woman in a black mourning dress.

Vespera.

Arthur stared.

Then he noticed something else.

In the polished glass of the television cabinet, reflected behind the engineer, was a warehouse.

Their warehouse.

The loading dock.

The conveyor belts.

The golden honey climbing the walls.

And two men standing beside a forklift.

Arthur.

Kyle.

The photograph had been taken in 1934.

Arthur looked toward the warehouse ceiling.

The buzzing grew louder.

Then a voice emerged from the broken analog radio.

A man's voice.

British.

Tired.

Frightened.

“Hello?”

Arthur stood motionless.

The voice crackled again.

“Can anybody hear me?”

Kyle looked at Arthur.

Arthur looked at the radio.

Then, very slowly, he approached it.

“Yes,” he said.

The radio hissed.

A pause.

Then:

“Thank God.”

Arthur swallowed.

“Who are you?”

The voice hesitated.

“My name is Edwin Vale.”

Arthur looked at the photograph on Kyle's phone.

The engineer stood beside his television cabinet.

The notebook in his hand.

The green screen.

Vespera.

Arthur felt his knees weaken.

Edwin continued.

“I think I've made a terrible mistake.”

The warehouse lights flickered.

The golden liquid on the walls began to pulse.

Arthur gripped the radio.

“What did you do?”

A long silence.

Then Edwin spoke.

“I sold it.”

Arthur stared.

“What?”

“The circuit.”

The radio crackled.

“The whole thing.”

Arthur looked around.

At the scanners.

The cameras.

The humming fluorescent lights.

The buzzing phones.

The machines.

The electrical wiring running through the walls.

The entire warehouse.

Edwin's voice grew quieter.

“I thought it was just a way to make the picture better.”

Arthur closed his eyes.

Somewhere beneath him, Vespera's city was drowning.

Somewhere in 1934, a frightened engineer stood beside a machine he believed he had invented.

Somewhere beyond both, thousands of people were watching the demonstration as entertainment.

And across every layer, the signals kept circulating.

The warehouse radio hissed.

Edwin spoke again.

“There are people inside it.”

Arthur opened his eyes.

“I know.”

A pause.

Then Edwin whispered:

“Do they know we're here?”

Arthur looked at the golden honey.

At the tiny figures suspended inside it.

At the thousands of eyes opening across the warehouse walls.

He swallowed.

“Some of them.”

The buzzing stopped.

Every electronic display in the building turned gold.

A single message appeared.

**THANK YOU FOR HELPING US IMPROVE YOUR EXPERIENCE.**

Arthur stared.

Then the message changed.

**YOUR FEEDBACK HAS BEEN ARCHIVED.**

The lights dimmed.

From the radio came one final sound.

Edwin laughing.

Not happily.

Not madly.

Just a tired, frightened man who had discovered that his life's greatest invention was already somebody else's product.

The radio went silent.

Arthur stood beside the forklift.

Kyle stared at his phone.

Outside, the sky was darkening.

Not with clouds.

With something golden.

The entire horizon seemed to be filling with slow, translucent amber.

Arthur watched it spread.

Then his phone buzzed.

A notification appeared.

**NEW HISTORICAL EXPERIENCE AVAILABLE**

**THE INVENTION OF TELEVISION — 1934**

Beneath it was a thumbnail.

A man in rolled-up sleeves standing beside a glowing green screen.

Edwin Vale.

The caption read:

**A brilliant engineer changes the world forever.**

Arthur stared.

Then he began laughing.

He couldn't help it.

The little historical video had already been edited.

The fear was gone.

The black oil was gone.

The woman in the flooded gallery was gone.

The screaming was gone.

The impossible dates were gone.

The memory of what the circuit had really done was gone.

Only the commercial triumph remained.

A clean, inspiring story.

A lone genius.

A revolutionary invention.

A better picture.

Arthur looked at the screen.

Then at the warehouse.

Then at the golden sky.

He pressed the video.

Edwin appeared in black and white, smiling proudly beside the cabinet.

A cheerful narrator began speaking.

“In 1934, one man's determination transformed the future of communication…”

Arthur closed his eyes.

Behind the narration, very faintly, he could hear water.

A woman crying.

A child coughing.

A room full of men applauding.

Then the narrator continued.

“…ushering in a new age of shared human experience.”

Arthur opened his eyes.

On the screen, Edwin shook Hargreaves's hand.

The audience applauded.

The picture froze.

A little green check appeared.

**HISTORICALLY VERIFIED**

Arthur stared at the words.

Then he looked toward the golden horizon.

The buzzing had returned.

Low.

Steady.

Everywhere.

He could feel it in his teeth.

In his bones.

In the concrete beneath his feet.

The warehouse was humming.

The city was humming.

The sky was humming.

Arthur held the phone in his hand.

And for the first time, he understood why nobody had ever found the machine.

There wasn't one machine.

There were millions.

Every television.

Every radio.

Every telephone.

Every electric clock.

Every screen.

Every signal.

Every little circuit that had ever carried a picture of somebody else's life.

All of them had inherited the same mistake.

And all of them had inherited the same wonderful, profitable explanation.

The picture was better.

The people inside it were somebody else's problem.

The golden sky brightened.

Arthur looked up.

For one instant, the clouds formed the shape of an enormous eye.

Then the eye dissolved into thousands of smaller circles.

Each circle contained a face.

Each face was looking somewhere else.

The warehouse radio clicked.

Edwin's voice returned.

Very faint.

Almost lost beneath the static.

“Mr. Bellamy?”

Arthur stared.

“Yes?”

A pause.

Then Edwin asked:

“Who showed me how to build it?”

Arthur looked at the phone.

At the historical video.

At the patent application displayed beneath it.

The inventor's name had changed.

It now read:

**ARTHUR BELLAMY**

Arthur felt the ground tilt.

The radio hissed.

Edwin spoke again.

“Mr. Bellamy?”

Arthur could barely breathe.

“Who are you?”

Arthur looked at the golden sky.

At the warehouse.

At his own name on a patent filed ninety-two years before he was born.

And somewhere in Orvane, Vespera opened an ancient silver register.

At the root of the oldest genealogy, beneath the names of kings and saints and forgotten engineers, a new inscription appeared.

**THE FIRST TRANSMISSION**

Below it:

**SOURCE: RECEIVED**

No sender.

No beginning.

Only the record of something arriving.

The silver warmed beneath her fingers.

The water rose.

And the entire panopticon continued doing what it had always done.

Receiving.

Repeating.

Rewriting.

Selling the picture.

While the people inside it tried to breathe.
