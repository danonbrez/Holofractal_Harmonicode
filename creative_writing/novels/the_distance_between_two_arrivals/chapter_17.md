# Chapter 17: The First Broadcast

The truth reached Meridian at noon and was misunderstood by one o'clock.

That wasn't a failure of the broadcast. It was a property of eighty-six thousand people receiving news that changed the map of their lives.

Anja stood in Habitat Twelve beneath a ceiling hung with paper planets. The civic channel showed three tables: confirmed observations, unresolved inferences and immediate safety measures. Holt introduced the evidence; Nalin explained Home's verified population; Peren described the corrected resource ledger; Kala said plainly what the Nacre science did and did not establish. A consent screen gave residents ways to challenge the material or request their own records.

Sev spoke last.

"For fifty-six years," he said, "our public system described Habitat Seventy-Two as unoccupied. That description is false. Three thousand one hundred eight people have been living there, maintaining their own infrastructure, raising families and keeping records. The ship owes them acknowledgment and practical restitution. The navigational consequences are under independent review. Our immediate responsibility is to protect everyone aboard while that review proceeds."

He paused.

"We have not cancelled the arrival burn."

The sentence made the crowd around Anja murmur. It was meant to reassure them. To some it sounded like proof that the discovery changed nothing. To others it meant the commander intended to land regardless of the hidden people or the planet.

Anja heard a woman behind her say, "So they had an extra city all along and never told us. Where did our ration work go?"

A man answered, "If they could hide a city, what else are they hiding?"

Someone else said, "My brother died of an infection last winter while that place got secret medicine."

The man with the brother was not lying. His grief could turn inaccurate inferences into an accusation because grief had little patience for distinctions that did not change the past.

Anja turned to explain the medical transfer history, but the crowd was moving toward the civic desk. One of the children knocked down a paper planet and started crying. The sound was ordinary and terrible.

***

In Home, people gathered in the kitchen and listened to their existence become public property. Nalin's words were broadcast twice. Tavi watched a report that showed their engineering diagrams beside a picture of the sealed door. The reporter called them "survivors of a lost compartment" and displayed footage of an old fire.

"We asked them not to say recovered," Tavi muttered.

"They didn't," said the baker.

"Not precisely."

On the next channel a commentator demanded immediate integration. "The ship cannot have an entire population operating beyond governance," he declared.

Daret switched it off.

"Beyond whose governance?" he asked.

A young woman named Rue advocated opening the borders at once. She had never been outside and had kept a list of places she intended to visit, beginning with the public pool in Habitat Four. Her brother objected that open transit would bring police, cameras and strangers who wanted to see how the hidden people slept.

"They'll come anyway," Rue said. "I'd rather meet them at the door than wait for them to decide what we are."

Tavi watched Nalin listen. He looked older by a year than he had that morning.

***

By late afternoon, rallies had formed in three registered habitats. Some demanded reparations for Home. Others opposed sharing fuel and reserves with people they regarded as undocumented dependents. A group outside the Navigation Office called for postponement of all landing decisions. A smaller and angrier group called them enemies of arrival.

Sev invoked the emergency crowd-separation rules. Transit between certain districts was restricted for six hours. He permitted independent review of the restrictions, but he also signed an order placing the Nacre landing evidence under command coordination until the orbital plan was complete.

Holt challenged the latter order. It was broader than the emergency required.

"You're separating people to stop them hurting one another," he told Sev. "That may be justified. You're also taking custody of evidence that has already been promised to independent archives. That isn't crowd control."

"An uncontrolled interpretation could undermine the burn preparation," Sev replied.

"An interpretation is not a thrust command."

"It can make one impossible to issue."

"Then face the disagreement. Don't solve it by reducing the number of people allowed to know."

Sev's expression hardened. "I will not make the ship's survival contingent on whether the public approves a model it cannot review in time."

"Public review has already begun," Holt said. "It will not be improved by telling them which parts of their history they may inspect."

The commander ended the call and issued a narrowly worded evidence retention order instead. It still troubled Holt, because the difference between a narrow emergency and a broad habit was often the next signature.

***

At shift end Anja was in a transit car when it stopped between habitats. The vehicle's announcement blamed temporary security coordination. Through the glass she saw six schoolchildren in arrival uniforms waiting on the opposite platform, accompanied by two anxious adults. One child held a sign that read EVERYBODY COUNTS. Another held a sign that read DON'T TAKE OUR HOME.

They were not enemies. They were frightened children learning their first public language of conflict.

The car doors eventually opened. Anja made her way to the engineering deck. Her terminal showed three pending messages: a demand from Command to deliver the mass proof, a request from Home for more filter supplies, and an emergency notice from Miro.

The notice read: MULTIPLE PATIENTS, TRANSIT OBSTRUCTION. MEDICAL ASSISTANCE REQUIRED.

Anja called him. The line filled with voices and a high irregular alarm.

"Where are you?" she asked.

"At the junction infirmary. They're blocking the route into Home. Some people say the medical supplies belong to registered patients."

"I'll come."

"Bring your engineering clearance. We need the doors open."

She took her work coat from the hook and ran.

By the time she reached the junction the protesters had dispersed, but their delay had lasted thirty-seven minutes. It was long enough for several patients to worsen and one elderly woman to stop breathing.

Her name was Elsa Bener.
