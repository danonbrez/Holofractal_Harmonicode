# Chapter 31: The Cost of Being Right

The trapped mechanic's name was Aro Fen. He had spent thirty-one years servicing pressure joints and complained bitterly about every new safety form the ship introduced. He had never met Anja. When she reached through the breach, he gripped her wrist with such strength she thought his hand might break a bone.

"Don't pull," he whispered. "My leg's caught."

Anja turned her lamp. The support beam had pinned his lower leg against the frame. Aro was losing blood slowly but steadily. Miro crouched beside her, placing a tourniquet above the injury and checking Aro's pulse.

"We have minutes before the swelling closes off circulation," he said.

The beam could be lifted with a hydraulic brace, but the brace needed a stable anchor. The nearest anchor point lay beside the old acoustic array Anja had used to establish the original phase-order discrepancy. The array carried the last local reference specimen of the reddish mineral deposits from Home, a sample Kala still wanted for comparison with Nacre's ocean signals. Disconnecting it would destroy some unreplicated calibration history and contaminate the specimen.

Anja considered trying to preserve the instrument. The space was too narrow for a separate mounting bracket. She could spend ten minutes building one, or she could use the array housing now and break it.

Aro groaned. Miro looked at her.

"Anja," he said, "choose."

She pulled the array's emergency isolation handle and disconnected its cooling line. The apparatus fell silent. She fitted the brace against the reinforced housing and felt the metal take the load.

"Ready," she said.

Daret activated the lift. The beam rose a few centimeters. It was enough. Miro dragged Aro's leg free and covered the wound while the others pulled him toward the hatch. Aro cried out once, then went quiet, his face drained of color.

As they carried him away Anja saw the acoustic specimen jar crack beneath a pressure strut. Red dust dispersed through the wet air and settled on the floor like powder from an old brick.

There was no way to gather it without introducing more contaminants. She looked at the ruined instrument and felt the irrational pang of losing something that had brought the truth to light.

Then she heard Miro calling for a stretcher.

The choice was over.

***

Aro survived surgery. His leg did not. The surgeon explained that tissue had been crushed beyond repair and that there had been complications from compromised circulation. Miro recorded the injury as an emergency consequence of the Habitat Thirty-Eight pressure failure. He did not write a sentence about fate.

When Aro woke, Anja came to the ward carrying a cup of broth.

"I heard you broke an expensive instrument," he said.

"I did."

"Was it mine?"

"No."

"Then I can't complain about the workmanship."

He smiled weakly. Anja sat down.

"You lost your leg because of a command preparation that shouldn't have happened," she said.

"I gathered."

"We'll preserve the evidence. The responsibility won't be assigned to the joint simply because it was the place that broke."

Aro stared at the ceiling. "I spent my life fixing joints other people ignored until they failed. I didn't expect the last one to get me."

"I'm sorry."

"Save it for the people who signed the order." He turned toward her. "I would like the record to say I was working on a legal repair when the pressure hit. I don't want some report to call it operator exposure."

"I'll see that it does."

"Then we're square for now."

She left the ward with a grief she did not know where to put. Aro was alive and angry and would have to learn to live in a body the ship had changed. The evidence of that change would be recorded accurately. It would not make him whole.

***

The official incident review began while the lower spine was still warm.

Dima delivered the manual-control logs. Rusk testified that Sev had shown him the coolant fault report and invoked emergency preparation authority. He admitted that he had not asked for the complete revised schedule. He had believed a competent commander and a real fault were sufficient justification. They had not been.

Sev made no attempt to shift blame onto the junior officer.

"I ordered the preparation," he said. "I knew the direct profile lacked certification. I believed maintaining the option would preserve a chance of immediate arrival. That belief did not satisfy the emergency condition. I exercised an authority I had not earned."

Ressa asked whether he still believed the longer route was inhumane.

"I believe it will hurt people," he said. "I also believe the cost of that route cannot justify an unlicensed maneuver that hurts them in another way."

He requested removal from command pending formal inquiry. The civic court accepted, and Dima took operational control under a bounded emergency delegation with an independent civilian witness. Rusk was removed from manual authority pending retraining and review.

The transfer was not a purification ceremony. It was a repair of the decision process while the ship was still in danger.

***

Anja returned to Bay Four to inspect the damaged acoustic array. Nyla had recovered most of its signed digital readings from distributed archives. The unreplicated physical specimen was gone. Kala was disappointed but not surprised.

"We have other observations," she said. "We can gather new data during the long route."

"I wanted to protect the original proof."

"You protected a person. The proof was never more valuable than him."

"That sounds like a story we tell ourselves afterward."

"Perhaps. But the lost sample cannot justify leaving Aro pinned beneath a beam."

Anja opened the retained files. The order-dependent signatures remained, each with a preserved acquisition history. Their copies would have to be checked against surviving witnesses, and the lost specimen's physical provenance would be explicitly marked incomplete. The truth did not require her to pretend nothing had been lost.

At sunset ship time she found Tavi in the public pool. The girl was floating on her back, eyes closed, moving carefully as though she trusted the water only one breath at a time.

Tavi sat up when Anja approached. "Did they get everybody out?"

"Yes."

"Did anybody die?"

"No. Aro lost a leg."

Tavi looked down. "I don't know what to do with a result like that."

"Neither do I."

They sat side by side while children splashed in the shallow end. Anja was tired enough to fall asleep standing. Tavi offered her a dry towel and a piece of bread wrapped in paper.

"Elsa would say eat it," Tavi said. "Then complain if you looked tragic while chewing."

Anja ate. The bread was salty, and for a few minutes neither of them tried to make the event mean something larger than it had.

Outside the pool the ship's engines waited for a lawful command.
