# PANTHEON OF THE PANOPTICON
## Chapter Twelve: Knowing More Than You Understand
*Epilogue — The Quiet After the Laugh*

At 6:12 on a Thursday morning, Arthur dropped a teaspoon.

It fell all the way to the floor.

He watched it go.

The spoon struck a tile, bounced once, and slid beneath the refrigerator with a small metallic scrape.

Arthur stood holding his coffee.

He waited.

Nothing skipped. Nothing moved backward. The refrigerator did not open onto a funeral garden. No woman cried out from beneath the floor.

A spoon had fallen.

He crouched to retrieve it and discovered that his left knee was not prepared to celebrate.

“Jesus.”

The spoon had come to rest beside a dead moth and a refrigerator magnet he had lost three years earlier.

He reached for it.

His fingertips touched cold steel.

Ordinary cold steel.

He picked it up.

There was coffee on the handle.

He put it in the sink to wash later.

It was six twelve.

He had to leave in twenty-three minutes.

***

The warehouse reopened on a limited basis.

This was Denise's phrase, and she had made certain it appeared in every communication to corporate.

The forest had not disappeared, but the west side of the loading dock was usable again. A botanist from the county had identified the largest tree as an oak of indeterminate age, with root growth inconsistent with every available construction record.

The tree enclosed forklift twelve.

The botanist recommended preserving it.

The insurer recommended photographing it.

Corporate recommended taking advantage of the company's commitment to environmental responsibility.

Denise recommended moving the damn tree.

Nobody had yet produced a method that would not endanger either the workers or the people who sometimes appeared in the rings of its trunk.

So forklift twelve remained where it was.

Someone had attached a handwritten sign.

**OUT OF SERVICE**

Underneath, in smaller letters:

**DO NOT WATER WITHOUT PERMISSION.**

Arthur had added that part.

The first time a janitor emptied a mop bucket against the roots, three centuries of Orvane's funerary gardens had received a sudden flood of soapy water.

The janitor was now required to use the other drain.

There were more procedures like that.

Small ones.

Imperfect ones.

They occupied a single folder in Denise's office, filed under **CROSS-BOUNDARY MAINTENANCE**, because Facilities would not accept **COSMIC INCIDENTS** as a service category.

Edith Bell had insisted on the name.

She had returned to light duty with six stitches removed from her palm and a ring of scar tissue around the place where the water had passed through it.

Her medical records were not entirely repaired.

The hospital still alternated between St. Catherine's and a car wash on the edge of town.

But the scar remained on her hand, and she had stopped letting anybody call it promotional material.

At nine twenty, she brought Arthur a cup of coffee.

“You look terrible.”

“Thanks.”

“Still getting messages?”

“Some.”

“About the hospital?”

“Not today.”

Edith flexed her hand.

It hurt when she did that.

Arthur watched her stop.

“Does it get better?”

She looked at the scar.

“Sometimes.”

She set the cup beside his clipboard.

“Good enough for Thursday.”

Then she went back to work.

***

In Orvane, the funeral gardens were being replanted.

Vespera had not recovered her mother's grave.

The northern passage still ended at the ancient wall. The sealed people were no longer heard through it, and no one could establish which of their histories remained accessible.

Their names had been copied onto silver, paper, stone, and a small digital archive Elise had helped the registrars build.

The names did not always agree.

When they differed, the archivists stopped replacing the older inscription.

They recorded the disagreement beside it.

Vespera had insisted.

“Leave both.”

High Archivist Severin had called it an invitation to disorder.

Vespera had shown him the wall.

“Disorder is already here.”

He had threatened to convene the ancestral court.

She had told him to convene a work party instead.

They were still arguing.

But in the eastern gardens, people had begun planting flowers.

The first roses were pale and weak. Their roots had been transferred through a negotiated boundary from a greenhouse whose owners had agreed to spare the cuttings.

A few did not survive the cold stone.

The gardeners tried again.

On the seventh morning, one opened.

A small yellow bee settled on its petals.

Vespera watched from the stair.

The insect's wings made a delicate sound.

No lights flickered.

No city shook.

She did not know how much of the bee belonged to Orvane and how much had crossed from somewhere else.

She was glad it had arrived.

Then she remembered that the bloom would need water.

She went to fetch some.

***

Elise Marrow had acquired an ordinary notebook.

It was made of paper.

She kept it in her pocket because she no longer trusted any document capable of rewriting itself while she was asleep.

The first page contained the only instruction all seventy-two of her accessible iterations had agreed to retain.

**DO NOT SUBSTITUTE COMPATIBILITY FOR CONTINUITY.**

Below it, in different handwriting:

*Ask before crossing.*

Below that:

*Record costs.*

And beneath the final line:

*Leave room for uncertainty.*

They had not agreed on the order.

One Elise believed material cost came first. Another argued that no consent was meaningful without a reliable account of the affected subjects. A third insisted emergency care could not always wait for communication.

They retained the disagreement.

The notebook stayed open.

Elise lived in a small room above the eastern infirmary.

Its window looked onto a wall of old stone and a narrow channel of black water. The roof leaked when the white ceiling above Orvane was disturbed. There was a wooden chair and a bed that made her back ache.

She could have reconstructed her original laboratory.

Sometimes she wanted to.

She remembered polished floors and clean water. She remembered instruments that worked, a refrigerator full of food, a colleague bringing coffee on Friday afternoons.

She also remembered the system replacing a grieving mother's kitchen with a preferred earlier version and taking the mother away with it.

So Elise kept the crooked chair.

She fixed the roof.

She paid attention to which stone was beneath her feet.

One morning, an eleven-year-old Elise appeared on the communication terminal.

The girl wore a school uniform and had a small bandage on her chin.

“Are you me?”

Elise looked at her.

“Yes. And no.”

The girl frowned.

“That's a bad answer.”

“I know.”

“Are you going to tell me what happens?”

Elise looked at the notebook.

She remembered wanting to know that when she was eleven.

She remembered being told.

She remembered not being told.

She remembered three incompatible futures in which the answer had changed everything.

“What do you want to know?”

The girl thought.

“Does Dad come home?”

Elise went still.

In one remembered history he did.

In another he died in the foundry.

In a third there had been no foundry and no father by that name.

She could not give the child a single complete answer.

She could give her what she knew about the histories she had seen, but not promise the child's own continuation.

“I'm not sure,” she said.

The girl looked down.

“Oh.”

Elise leaned closer.

“Is there somebody with you?”

“Mum.”

“Can you tell her what you're worried about?”

The girl shrugged.

“Maybe.”

Elise waited.

The girl touched the little bandage on her chin.

“Do we get clever?”

Elise smiled.

“Sometimes.”

“Does it help?”

“Sometimes.”

The girl considered that.

Then laughed.

“Another bad answer.”

“Yes.”

They agreed to talk again when both wanted to.

The connection ended.

No history was overwritten.

Elise wrote the time in her paper notebook.

And for several minutes afterward she sat looking out at the stone wall, remembering a father she could not reliably locate in any one past.

***

In 1934, Edwin Vale began selling an attachment for his television receivers.

It consisted of a small mechanical shutter and a separate signaling lamp.

Before changing a pulse sequence, the operator could illuminate the lamp and wait for acknowledgment from any known receiving station.

Hargreaves had protested.

“It slows the demonstration.”

“Yes,” Edwin said.

“Customers want instantaneous response.”

“I know.”

“It makes the instrument less impressive.”

“Yes.”

Hargreaves studied him.

“Then why?”

Edwin thought of the woman holding a candle beneath the white ceiling.

“Because sometimes the picture is somebody else's room.”

Hargreaves stared.

“That's a dreadful slogan.”

Edwin agreed.

The attachment sold badly.

Some customers removed it.

Others wired it incorrectly.

A few broadcast stations adopted it as a useful safety measure, although their engineers disagreed about why it worked.

Edwin continued manufacturing it.

At night he maintained the old green receiver in his workshop.

Sometimes it showed Orvane.

Sometimes Arthur's warehouse.

Sometimes only bars and static.

When a picture appeared unexpectedly, Edwin did not immediately touch the controls.

He waited.

He kept a second chair by the workbench, though nobody had yet crossed into it.

Once, through the green phosphor, he saw a woman carrying a bowl of water up a stone stair.

She looked toward him.

Raised her hand.

Edwin raised his.

They waited for acknowledgment.

Then he altered the pulse train by one step.

The image steadied.

No one cried out.

He wrote the result in his ledger.

Below the familiar number, **179971.179971**, he added:

*Stable image. No known injury. Unknown costs remain.*

He underlined the last sentence.

The tube hummed.

Outside, a tram bell rang.

He went home for supper.

***

Miriam still disliked the feed.

It still recommended things she had no interest in, and she remained unconvinced that people needed to know when distant strangers were doing something as ordinary as eating breakfast.

Sometimes a photograph arrived with a little symbol beside it.

Two circles.

A narrow connecting line.

**RECIPROCAL CONTACT AVAILABLE**

Miriam usually declined.

She had work to do.

There were bills to pay.

Her mother called too often.

She did not wish to establish a reciprocal connection with everyone whose image happened to appear on her telephone.

That was permitted.

One afternoon, however, she saw an old man sitting alone beside a hospital window.

He had no name in the caption.

The image was still.

He was staring at a paper cup.

A small line beneath the picture read:

**SUBJECT REQUESTS CONTACT.**

Miriam hesitated.

She had just finished a twelve-hour shift.

Her feet hurt.

She was hungry.

She thought about swiping.

Then she touched the symbol.

A question appeared.

**DO YOU WISH TO SPEAK?**

She pressed **YES**.

The old man looked up.

“Hello?”

Miriam cleared her throat.

“Hello.”

He looked startled.

For a moment neither knew what to say.

Then the man asked whether she had seen the rain.

Miriam looked out of her bus window.

It was raining.

“Yes,” she said.

He nodded.

“It's nice.”

She watched water run down the glass.

“Yeah.”

They spoke for four minutes.

He told her about a dog he had owned when he was young.

She told him her bus was late.

Neither learned the other's entire history.

Neither needed to.

When Miriam said she had to go, he thanked her.

They ended the contact together.

She arrived home tired.

The bus had been late.

Nothing dramatic had happened.

She had been listened to.

So had he.

***

Arthur visited Gregory Finch on Saturday.

Gregory lived with his sister.

That fact had returned to the records after three weeks of painstaking reconciliation, although the other life with Linda remained available as a separate, unresolved history.

Gregory remembered both.

Some mornings he woke missing Linda.

Other mornings he didn't.

His sister had learned not to tell him that one of his griefs was imaginary.

Arthur found him sitting on the back steps, looking at the garden.

Gregory handed him a beer.

Arthur accepted it.

For a while they watched a sparrow moving through the hedge.

Then Gregory said, “Do you think I'm the same person?”

Arthur looked at him.

Gregory's face was familiar.

The creases around his eyes.

The scar above his eyebrow.

The slight asymmetry in his smile.

Arthur remembered the other Gregory pressing his face against the restroom mirror.

He remembered the words **LOCAL INSTANCE SUCCESSFULLY REPLACED**.

He could not prove what had happened to either continuation.

“I don't know,” he said.

Gregory nodded.

“Yeah.”

The sparrow hopped onto the fence.

Gregory looked at the bottle in his hand.

“Sometimes I remember my wife.”

Arthur waited.

“I remember twenty years with her.”

His voice shook.

“That's a lot of years to miss somebody who might not have lived here.”

Arthur said nothing.

Gregory wiped his eyes.

Then laughed a little.

“I used to complain about my sister's television all the time.”

“I remember.”

“She still watches that awful cooking show.”

Arthur smiled.

“Some things survive the apocalypse.”

Gregory snorted.

They sat beside one another.

The sparrow flew away.

***

On Sunday, Vespera found the Golden Apple among the fallen flowers.

It lay in the soil beneath a rosebush.

Perfectly smooth.

No corrosion.

No dirt.

Its surface displayed no inscription.

She picked it up.

The metal was warm.

For a moment she thought of the many things it had offered.

Purity.

Ancestry.

Restoration.

The fairest.

The first.

A history without grief.

A city without loss.

She imagined asking it to return her mother.

She imagined seeing the gardens as they had been when she was twelve.

Then she thought of the other histories that would have to move aside.

The people already living inside them.

The mothers who were not hers.

The children who might be lost.

The warm object rested in her palm.

She did not destroy it.

She did not worship it.

She set it on the stone ledge near the archive entrance, beneath a little sign written in three languages.

**FOUND OBJECT — CLAIMANT MUST IDENTIFY AFFECTED PARTIES**

Toman looked at the sign.

Then at the apple.

“Will anybody claim it?”

“Perhaps.”

“Do you know what it is?”

Vespera considered.

“A question.”

Toman frowned.

“What kind?”

“One that can become very expensive if answered too quickly.”

He laughed.

Then went to fetch more water for the roses.

The apple remained on the ledge.

***

At 6:04 on Monday morning, Arthur woke before his alarm.

For a moment he lay in the dark.

He could feel the other memories nearby.

Not inside his head as an unbearable flood.

Nearby.

A childhood in a house with yellow wallpaper.

A laboratory beneath the earth.

A flooded gallery.

A green television screen.

Seventy-two possible mornings.

He knew more than he could understand.

He had stopped trying to hold it all at once.

The alarm clicked on.

He turned it off.

In the kitchen, the electricity bill lay beside the sink.

He had finally paid it.

The company had sent a confirmation email.

**THANK YOU FOR YOUR PAYMENT.**

Arthur smiled faintly.

For once, the message meant exactly what it said.

He filled the kettle.

Made coffee.

Put bread in the toaster.

The refrigerator hummed.

Outside, a delivery truck reversed into the street.

The world remained full of things he could not explain.

He let the toaster finish.

Then his phone buzzed.

A contact request.

From Vespera.

Arthur looked at the time.

He had twelve minutes before he needed to leave.

He accepted.

The flooded archive appeared.

Vespera stood beside a window of pale stone.

Behind her, someone was carrying buckets through the garden.

“Good morning,” she said.

Arthur looked at her.

“Is it morning there?”

She considered.

“It is now.”

He smiled.

She smiled back.

“Did you sleep?”

“Some.”

“Good.”

Vespera held up a small yellow rose.

It had opened overnight.

Arthur leaned closer.

“That's nice.”

“Yes.”

She turned the flower so he could see a small bee inside it.

The bee's wings moved.

The petals trembled.

Nothing more.

Arthur watched.

He thought of the hive.

The amber.

The circuits.

The ancient restoration system.

The countless lives crossing one another's boundaries.

Then he thought of the little insect doing its work.

A living body.

A flower.

A small exchange.

Vespera lowered the rose.

“Are you going to work?”

“Yeah.”

“Will you be careful?”

Arthur looked toward the lamp beside the kitchen table.

“I'll try.”

She nodded.

“So will I.”

They said goodbye.

The contact ended by mutual agreement.

Arthur put his phone in his pocket.

He finished his coffee.

Washed the cup.

Checked the stove.

Then went to work.

***

Deep beneath the planet, the restoration system continued operating.

Its indicators blinked.

Its reservoirs exchanged heat.

Its material cycles moved water, carbon, minerals, and energy between the places where they were needed.

It preserved records.

Maintained living environments.

Reconstructed what could be reconstructed without unauthorized replacement.

Deferred what it could not reconcile.

Its own history remained incomplete.

Beyond the oldest accessible chamber lay further chambers.

Beyond the earliest recorded developers lay additional histories.

Beyond the most comprehensive available description of the system remained questions that description could not settle.

The machine did not declare itself finished.

It did not shut down.

It did not awaken into a benevolent god.

It continued its work.

At 7:01, local reference time, a minor status entry appeared.

**LOCAL SUBJECT: ARTHUR BELLAMY**

**ACTIVITY: COMMUTING TO WORK**

**UNAUTHORIZED CROSS-BOUNDARY CHANGE: NONE DETECTED**

**INTERVENTION: NOT REQUIRED**

The entry passed into the archive.

It remained there.

No advertisement was generated from it.

No recommended video appeared.

No audience was invited to rate the man's morning.

The record simply recorded that he had gone to work.

Above, Arthur drove through traffic beneath a pale ordinary sky.

He stopped at a red light.

Waited.

The light turned green.

He drove on.

Somewhere else, a candle burned.

Somewhere else, a rose opened.

Somewhere else, somebody reached toward a screen and thought better of it.

There was no final floor.

No final ceiling.

Only thresholds.

And, for one imperfect morning, enough room between them for people to live.

**THE END**
